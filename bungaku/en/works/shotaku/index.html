About this work

A piece marking the period when Nagai Kafū was inclining toward the taste for Edo, published in April 1912 in Mita Bungaku, which he edited. It draws, in humour and paradox, the contemporary man of letters Professor Chinchin, who cannot get on with the civilization of modern Japan and escapes the world in a kept woman's house by a canal, letting his mind play among the chic and the quiet of Edo. Irony at a thin, Western-struck modernity and affection for a dying Edo culture form two faces of one thing, in a novel with a strongly essayistic flavour.

Summary

From the day he realized he could no longer bring himself to go along with the general drift of the world, a certain contemporary man of letters, styling himself Professor Chinchin, took to shutting himself away in a kept woman's house by a canal and passing his days there in weary reverie. He wears Western clothes, rides the streetcar, and writes novels when asked — knowing well enough the ways of the world so as not to lose out in the modern struggle for existence — but he writes all of it off as mere "making shift to get by," and needed the house by the canal as a backstage room where he could, unseen by anyone, take off his mask. A black, gleaming lattice door, a cat asleep by the brazier, a hanging scroll in the alcove — amid the quiet, dim stillness of the kept woman's house, he steeps himself utterly in the taste and feeling handed down from Edo. Weaving in irony at a shallow modern civilization forever chasing after the West, Kafū, through the figure of Professor Chinchin, tells of his attachment to a vanishing Edo chic, in a spirit of humour and paradox.

The opening (original text)

From the day he knew he could never, with his whole heart, bring himself to go along contentedly with the general drift of the world, he came more and more to spend his weary, solitary days of reverie only at the kept woman's house by a certain canal. It was not simply that there was nothing interesting left in the world nowadays; he could no longer bear having thrust before him, without end, every last thing he had no wish to see.

Shōtaku by Nagai Kafū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But enough of this digression. In the kitchen of the kept woman's house, the whitebait grilled with sea urchin that the mistress has taken such pains to prepare is ready, and the scene of the two of them passing dishes back and forth to one another, a truly enviable, blossoming picture, is one I would like to say awaits the next volume — but for reasons of my own I shall write no further of it here. I ask the reader's understanding.

Shōtaku by Nagai Kafū (Aozora Bunko)
