About this work

A short story about a woman possessed by the entertaining of guests, seen through her maid's eyes, published in the magazine Hikari in January 1948. Her husband has gone missing at the front, and Sasajima and his friends make themselves at home, using up her furniture and her health. Still she throws herself into hosting them, saying only that she cannot say no; and the maid who tells it, exasperated at first, comes to see something bottomlessly kind in her. A fine example of Dazai's late monologues in a woman's voice.

Summary

To the eyes of the narrator, a maid in a house on the outskirts of Tokyo, her mistress is a strange creature. Every time the front doorbell rings, the lady goes tense like a small bird an instant before it takes flight at the sound of an eagle's wings; she greets the visitor with an odd cry that is somewhere between weeping and laughing, and runs herself ragged between the parlor and the kitchen. Since the end of last year, when she invited in Sasajima, her husband's friend, whom she had not seen for ten years, his companions have been coming and staying the night, until the house has become a kind of dormitory for them. Wine, chicken hotpot, baths in the morning — unable to say no, the mistress even joins them sleeping all in a row on the floor, while her property and her health are worn away. In time she coughs blood, resolves to go home to her family, and is about to set out for the station — when Sasajima appears at the front door again.

Characters

  • The mistress　A lady who behaves as though possessed by the entertaining of guests. Her husband has been missing on an island in the South Seas
  • I (Ume)　The maid in her service, who tells what became of the household
  • Sasajima　A friend of the master's, a bachelor of medicine, who takes to coming with his companions and staying
  • Doctor Imai　A young doctor among Sasajima's companions, who says outright that the place is simply an inn

The opening (original text)

My mistress had always been fond of fussing over guests and entertaining them; no — in her case one might rather say that she was frightened of guests than that she liked them. When the front doorbell rang and I had gone to answer it and then came to her room to tell her the visitor's name, she would already have on her face that strangely tense look of a small bird an instant before it takes flight at the sound of an eagle's wings; and pushing back her stray hair and straightening her collar and half rising, before she had heard half of what I had to say she would be up and out into the corridor and running with quick little steps to the entrance, and there greeting the guest at once with that strange cry, like the note of a flute, somewhere between weeping and laughing,

The ending (original text)

When I had been sent out on an errand, and opened at the market the traveling handbag my mistress had hurriedly given me in place of a purse to take money from, I was startled to see her ticket torn in two; and when I realized that she must have torn it quietly the very moment she met Sasajima at the door, I was struck dumb by the bottomless kindness of her, and at the same time felt that I had been shown for the first time in my life that human beings possess something noble, something quite unlike any other animal. And so I took my own ticket out from the fold of my sash and quietly tore it in two as well, and went on looking about the market to see what more I might buy to bring home for them to eat.
