What is Shōchōshi (Symbolist Poetry)?

Category
Form / Genre

Poetry in the tradition of the Symbolism ("symbolisme") that arose in France in the latter half of the nineteenth century. Rather than stating emotion or idea directly, it expresses them suggestively, through the evocative power of a symbolic object or the musicality of language's sound. Ueda Bin's collection of translated poetry "Kaichōon" ("The Sound of the Tide," 1905), introducing 57 poems by 29 poets in dignified, translated verse, had a decisive influence on its introduction to Japan, and the celebrated translations of Carl Busse's "Über den Bergen" ("Over the Mountains") and Verlaine's "Il pleure dans mon coeur" ("Fallen Leaves") remain widely recited even today. Kanbara Ariake and Miki Rofū attempted it in their own poetry, and Kitahara Hakushū opened a world of sensual Symbolist poetry in "Jashūmon" ("The Heretics," 1909). Nagai Kafū's collection of translated poetry "Sangoshū" ("The Coral Collection," 1913) likewise carried this current forward, bound up with the aesthetic sensibility of the Aesthetic school, and the influence of French Symbolist poetry reached far into modern Japanese poetry, down to Miyoshi Tatsuji, who left "Sokuryōsen" ("The Surveying Ship") in the Shōwa era, and Nakahara Chūya, drawn to Rimbaud and Verlaine.
