About this work

A short story by Osanai Kaoru, published in "Chūō Kōron" in 1911. Bairyū, a young apprentice geisha of Akasaka, talks straight through the night she spent in a landslide-flood at Tōnosawa in Hakone the summer before, exactly as she would be talking in a banquet room. There is almost no narration at all: from beginning to end the piece is one girl's chatter, a bold form that captures the speech and the temper of the geisha quarters of the day. Even on a night of fleeing for her life she stuffs tinned food into her breast, and her talk keeps coming back to things to eat — and that garrulousness makes the confusion of the disaster all the more vivid.

Summary

Bairyū, an apprentice geisha of Akasaka, tells of the night of the great flood at the inn Suzuki in Tōnosawa, Hakone, the summer before. The party was five: the elder sister who had once been sung of as the greatest beauty in Japan, Tamaryū from the same house, Wakana from Shinbashi, the maid Otomi, and Bairyū. A pretty child with cool eyes, she is nevertheless, where eating is concerned, a champion on a level with Wakana, and there is never a story of hers without food in it. On the evening of the day after they arrived, she was in the bath washing her hair with egg, having forgotten her cleansing powder, when the water suddenly turned black and cedar needles came floating up. Otomi comes flying in: "It's the water, we have to run!" Tying a towel around her wet hair like a headband and trailing one of the inn's lent bathrobes, she pushed a tin of beef and a tin of pickles into her breast as she went out — food would be hard to come by — but she had put them open end down, and the juice went to a mush inside her clothes. In the pitch dark and the pouring rain, nothing sounded but the fire bell and the shouting of people. They fled into the geisha house across the way, passed the night there, and went home over the mountains. Afterward a Hakone geisha who came to Akasaka told her how, that night, both pairs of her good clogs had been walked off in, leaving her in difficulties. Bairyū, who knows very well whose feet those were, goes to apologize — and she hates apologizing more than anything.

Characters

  • Bairyū　An apprentice geisha of Akasaka. A refined girl with cool eyes, known for her appetite, whose talk is never without food in it — the narrator
  • Elder sister　A beauty once sung of as the finest in Japan. A calm sort who never lets her guard slip at anything
  • Tamaryū　An apprentice geisha of the same house as Bairyū
  • Wakana　An apprentice geisha of Shinbashi. With Bairyū, one of the "two champions" of eating
  • Otomi　The maid of Bairyū's house, who comes flying into the bath to warn of the flood

The opening (original text)

Nothing happened the night we arrived. The girl from the picture-postcard shop stole my rice crackers, that's all. I caught her at it and there was a great fuss. But elder sister said the poor thing, do forgive her, so I forgave her. — Bairyū, an apprentice geisha of Akasaka, is telling the story of the great flood she met with last year at the Suzuki in Tōnosawa, Hakone.

The ending (original text)

And then I apologized to that person, Kodama or whatever her name was. I hate apologizing, I really do, but I hate being called a thief, so I apologized. And it turned out she didn't even remember my face properly. What a waste, wasn't it.
