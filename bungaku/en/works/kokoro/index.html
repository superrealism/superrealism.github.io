About this work

The last of Natsume Sōseki's late trilogy, serialized in the Asahi Shimbun in 1914. Told in three parts — "Sensei and I," "My Parents and I," and "Sensei's Testament" — it traces the loneliness and buried guilt of a man who lived by the spirit of the Meiji era, seen first through the eyes of a young admirer and then through Sensei's own long letter. Its themes of egoism and conscience, and of friendship set against love, have kept it among the most widely read works of modern Japanese literature.

Summary

A university student, "I," meets a man he calls Sensei on the beach at Kamakura and, drawn to him, deepens the acquaintance. Sensei lives quietly at a distance from the world, and behind his weariness with life the narrator senses some dark past. Later, called home by his father's illness, the narrator receives a long letter from Sensei — a testament. In it Sensei sets down his past. As a student he had fallen in love with the daughter of his boarding house, and so had K, his closest friend. When K confessed his feelings, Sensei moved first and arranged the marriage for himself. K, learning of the betrayal, took his own life, and Sensei lived out his years carrying that guilt. Then, with the death of the Meiji Emperor and General Nogi's suicide to follow his lord, Sensei too chooses death, following the spirit of Meiji to the grave.

Characters

  • I　A university student drawn to Sensei. The narrator of the story
  • Sensei　An educated man who lives at a distance from the world, carrying a dark past
  • K　Sensei's closest friend in their student days. An austere, single-minded seeker
  • The young lady (Shizu)　The daughter of the boarding house, later Sensei's wife
  • The landlady　The young lady's mother and mistress of the boarding house

The opening (original text)

I always called him Sensei, and so here too I will write only Sensei and not disclose his real name. This is less out of any deference to the world than because it comes more naturally to me. Whenever I call the man to mind, the word "Sensei" rises at once to my lips. It is the same when I take up my pen. I could not bring myself to use some cold and distant initial.

Kokoro by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I cannot bear to set a black mark upon my wife's memory. My one wish is that the memory she keeps of her own past should be preserved as nearly spotless as it can be. So even after I am dead, so long as she is living, keep it all within you — my secret, confided to you and to no one else.

Kokoro by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

I always called him Sensei.

An opening that quietly begins with a remembrance of the man he called Sensei. This is the tone that carries through the whole novel, one of Sōseki's late masterworks on human solitude and egoism.

I began by flatly declaring, “Anyone with no ambition to better himself spiritually is a fool.” These were the very words K had once used to me, while the two of us were traveling together in Bōshū.

A scene in which he throws K's own words straight back at him, now that K has become his rival in love. This single remark drives his friend to the brink, and becomes Sensei's lifelong burden of guilt.

“Ma'am — please give me your daughter's hand.”

The scene in which Sensei proposes marriage to his landlady. Spoken without a word to K, who loved the same woman, this single line summons his friend's death and a lifetime of regret.

At that moment I felt as though the spirit of Meiji had begun with the Emperor and ended with the Emperor.

Sensei's reflection on the death of the Meiji Emperor. Together with the news of General Nogi's ritual suicide that follows, he comes to layer his own death over the ending of an entire era.

“I am a lonely man.”

Words Sensei lets slip after asking the narrator why he keeps coming to visit. The loneliness of a man who keeps others at a distance even as he longs for them seeps into this brief confession.

But you know, love is a sin. Do you understand?

A cryptic remark Sensei makes about love on the way back from viewing the cherry blossoms. The past with his friend K, revealed later in his testament, gives these words their full weight.

We who are born into this modern age, so full of freedom, independence, and the self, must all taste this loneliness as the price we pay for it.

A passage in which Sensei speaks of loneliness as the fate of modern people. The question that runs through the whole novel—the loneliness born in exchange for individual freedom—is voiced here.

Ordinarily, everyone is good. Or at least, everyone is an ordinary person. That is what makes it so frightening—that at the decisive moment, they suddenly turn bad.

Sensei's answer when talk turns to whether there are bad people in the world. It sounds like a forewarning both of his uncle's swindling of his inheritance and of the betrayal Sensei himself will later commit.

I should have died much sooner—why have I lived until now?

Words K added at the very end of his suicide note. In a letter without a single word of reproach for Sensei, this one line leaves a wound in his heart that never heals.

Before I die, I want to trust someone—even just one person—and die having trusted them. Can you be that one person? Will you be? Are you serious, from the bottom of your heart?

Sensei's reply when the narrator presses him to tell of his past. Having doubted people all his life, Sensei chooses the narrator as the one person he will trust—leading to the testament that follows.
