About this work

Sasaki Naojirō's translation of Poe's 1839 gothic masterpiece "The Fall of the House of Usher," from his complete Poe published by Daiichi Shobō. Concerned for his old friend Roderick Usher, the narrator visits an old house standing beside a gloomy tarn, and watches as a brother tormented by unbearably acute nerves, a twin sister buried all but alive in illness, and the house itself go down together as if answering one another. It lays the fate of a building over the fate of a family and holds to a heavy, oppressive description throughout: one of the central works of Poe's weird fiction.

Summary

On a dull autumn day with the clouds hanging low, the narrator answers an urgent letter from Roderick Usher, the friend of his boyhood, and comes to the House of Usher in its lonely tract of country. The old house, sunk beside a black tarn, has a thin fissure running from the roof down to its base. Roderick, whom he has not seen for years, has grown morbidly acute in his nerves: he cannot bear the least light or sound, smell or taste, and even what he wears and eats is narrowly limited; and his twin sister, the lady Madeline, is wasting day by day of an illness no one can name. To comfort his friend the narrator spends the days reading with him and sitting through his improvisations on the guitar and his gloomy paintings. Before long Madeline dies. Saying that it is only until she can be moved to the family burying ground far away, Roderick lays her body in a damp vault within the house and closes the heavy iron door. From then on his manner grows more and more unhinged. One night, in a raging storm, the narrator reads an old romance of chivalry to quiet him — and the sounds that ought to be in the story, the cracking of a door, the death shriek of a dragon, the ringing fall of a shield, come back uncannily as real noises from somewhere deep in the house. At last Roderick cries out half mad: his sister was buried living, he has heard her stirring in the coffin for days, and he did not dare to speak. At that instant the great door swings open by itself, and there stands the lady Madeline, blood upon her shroud. She falls upon her brother, and Roderick too dies in the very terror he had foreseen. Fleeing the house in horror, the narrator sees by the light of the blood-red full moon the fissure widen before his eyes, and the House of Usher split in two and sink without a sound into the dark tarn.

The opening (original text)

During the whole of a dull, dark, and soundless day in the autumn of the year, when the clouds hung oppressively low in the heavens, I had been passing alone, on horseback, through a singularly dreary tract of country; and at length found myself, as the shades of the evening drew on, within view of the melancholy House of Usher. I know not how it was — but, with the first glimpse of the building, a sense of insufferable gloom pervaded my spirit.

The ending (original text)

From that chamber, and from that mansion, I fled aghast. The storm was still abroad in all its wrath as I found myself crossing the old causeway. Suddenly there shot along the path a wild light, and I turned to see whence a gleam so unusual could have issued; for the vast house and its shadows were alone behind me. The radiance was that of the full, setting, and blood-red moon, which now shone vividly through that once barely-discernible fissure, of which I have before spoken as extending from the roof of the building, in a zigzag direction, to the base. While I gazed, this fissure rapidly widened — there came a fierce breath of the whirlwind — the entire orb of the satellite burst at once upon my sight — my brain reeled as I saw the mighty walls rushing asunder — there was a long tumultuous shouting sound like the voice of a thousand waters — and the deep and dank tarn at my feet closed sullenly and silently over the fragments of the "House of Usher."
