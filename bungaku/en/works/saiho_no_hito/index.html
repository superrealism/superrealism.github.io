About this work

One of Akutagawa's last works, written just before his suicide and published the month after his death. Declaring that he will draw his own Christ not as historical fact but as he himself feels him, he reads the figures of the Gospels — Mary, the Holy Spirit, Joseph, John and the rest — in thirty-seven fragments. He defines Mary as that which would protect eternally and the Holy Spirit as that which would eternally go beyond, and calls Christ a ladder cruelly broken in the attempt to climb from heaven to earth. A poet's Christology, and at the same time a self-portrait.

Summary

Ten years or so ago I loved Christianity artistically — Catholicism in particular. The psychology of the martyrs gave me a morbid interest, like the psychology of fanatics. But of late I have begun to love the man Christ himself, as the four biographers handed him down — so Akutagawa begins. "My Christ," born in Japan, does not necessarily look out over the lake of Galilee. Under a persimmon tree hung with red fruit, the inlet of Nagasaki too can be seen. Therefore, without regard to historical fact or geographical fact, he will set down only what he feels — and with that reservation the fragments begin. Mary is only a woman; she is not "the eternal feminine" but "that which would eternally protect." The Holy Ghost is "that which would eternally go beyond," walking beyond good and evil; and Joseph the carpenter was, after all, the first of the superfluous men. ... Herod, John the Baptist, the devil, the angels, the apostles, Judas, Pilate. Christ's journalism, his grandiloquence, his solitude. Akutagawa reads Christ now as a poet, now as the representative of "the children of the Holy Ghost." Goethe too was one of the Christs, and in life he was greater than Christ; but we love Goethe not because he was a child of Mary but only because he was a child of the Holy Ghost. Christ's life was wretched. It is "a ladder broken pitilessly in the attempt to climb from heaven to earth," and it leans still in a downpour beating out of a dim sky. In the last fragment, "The Man of the East," he touches on the people of the East who would build a hygiene upon nirvana, and lays down his pen imagining a dialogue between Laozi and Confucius.

The opening (original text)

1. Behold the man Some ten years ago I loved Christianity artistically — Catholicism in particular. The "Church of Our Lady of Japan" at Nagasaki remains in my memory to this day. Such as I am, I was no more than a crow busily picking up the seed that Mr. Kitahara Hakushū and Mr. Kinoshita Mokutarō had sown.

Saihō no hito by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Christ said, "The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the son of man hath not where to lay his head." His words hold, most likely without his being conscious of it himself, a dreadful fact. Unless we become foxes or birds, a roost is not easily to be found.

Saihō no hito by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)
