About this work

Sakaguchi Ango's best-known fantastical story, published in the first issue of the magazine Nikutai in June 1947. Working from his particular sense that what lies beneath cherry blossom is something to be feared, it follows the fate of a mountain bandit at the mercy of the uncanny beauty he has carried off from the capital. Regarded as the high point of the fantastic and the cruel in postwar literature.

Summary

A bandit who lives on Suzuka Pass carries off a beautiful woman raised in the capital from among the travellers and makes her his eighth wife. Wilful, she has him kill the wives he had before her one by one, and in time wants to leave the mountains and live in the capital. Once installed there, she has him kill people and collects their severed heads, and amuses herself with a game of heads. Grown weary of life in the capital, the bandit resolves to go back to the mountains, and the woman, seeing that she cannot live without him, agrees to go. Carrying her on his back through a forest of cherries in full bloom, the bandit notices that the hands on his back have gone cold, and looking round sees that she has turned into a hideous demon. Strangling it desperately, he comes to himself to find the woman as she had been, dead and covered with petals. The instant he reaches out to brush the petals from her face, her body vanishes into a scattering of petals, and his own body, reaching after her, vanishes too into the hushed emptiness.

Characters

  • The bandit　A man who lives on Suzuka Pass. The protagonist, bewitched by the beauty from the capital and driven by her fate
  • The woman　The beauty raised in the capital and carried off by the bandit. Cruel and wilful, she is in truth a demon

The opening (original text)

When the cherry blossom comes out, people carry bottles of sake and eat dumplings and walk beneath the flowers and grow merry, calling it a splendid view, calling it the fullness of spring; but this is a lie. Why a lie? Because people gathering beneath the cherry blossom to get drunk and be sick and quarrel is a thing of the Edo period; in the far past people thought the ground beneath the cherry blossom fearful, and no one thought of calling it a splendid view. Nowadays, since to speak of the ground beneath the cherry blossom is to think of people gathered there drinking and quarrelling, we have persuaded ourselves that it is a cheerful and lively thing; but take the people away from beneath the cherry blossom and the scene becomes a fearful one. There is a Nō play too in which a mother whose beloved child has been carried off by a kidnapper goes mad searching for him and comes to a grove of cherries in full bloom, and in the shade of the petals as far as she can see she draws the phantom of her child, and dies mad and is buried in the petals. If there is no human figure beneath the flowers of a cherry grove, it is nothing but fearful. Long ago the road over Suzuka Pass too was such that travellers had to pass beneath the flowers of a cherry forest. Out of the flowering season it was well enough; but when the season came, every traveller went strange in the head beneath the flowers of that forest.

The ending (original text)

He looked about him for the first time. Above his head there were flowers. Beneath them an infinite emptiness was quietly full. The flowers came down, whispering. That was all. Outside it there was no secret whatever. After a while he felt one single lukewarm something. And he realized that it was the sorrow in his own breast. Wrapped in the clear coldness of the flowers and the emptiness, that faintly warm swelling came to be understood a little at a time. He tried to take the petals from the woman's face. As his hand was about to reach her face, it seemed that something had changed. And then under his hand there were only fallen petals, and the woman's form had vanished and become only a number of petals. And his hand, which had tried to part the petals, and his body too, were gone by the time he stretched them out. Behind, there was nothing but petals, and the cold emptiness stretched taut.

Famous passages

When the cherry blossoms bloom, people carry bottles of sake and eat dumplings and stroll beneath the flowers, giddy with talk of a magnificent view and a splendid spring — but this is a lie.

An opening that declares a lie the common belief that all is beautiful beneath the blossoms in full bloom. It is Ango's representative work, depicting the void and the terror lurking beneath the flowers.

Beneath the blossoms, though there was no wind at all, it felt as though a great wind were roaring.

The sensation of the mountain bandit as he steps beneath the cherry trees in full bloom. The contradiction of a wind roaring where there is no wind at all names the terror lying beneath the blossoms.

Even now, no one knows the secret beneath the forest of cherry trees in full bloom. Perhaps it was the thing called “solitude.”

The nature of the forest's terror, revealed by the narrator near the end. It suggests that what the man feared all along may have been none other than “solitude.”

Only the petals were left, and a cold emptiness stretched taut.

The final sentence. Beneath the cherry trees in full bloom, both the woman and the man vanish, leaving only petals and a cold void—a beautiful, fantastical ending.
