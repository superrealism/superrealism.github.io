About this work

A set of festival dolls that an old family ruined by the Restoration is to sell to an American in Yokohama. The wish of a girl of fifteen to set them out just once more before they go is refused by both her father and her brother — a short story in which Akutagawa gives one night in a household of the first years of Meiji as an old woman's recollection.

Summary

The Kinokuniya house, which had once served the great feudal lords in matters of money, had fallen on hard times with the collapse of the Tokugawa shogunate. After repeated fires and failed ventures, the family is at last forced to sell even the daughter Otsuru's set of hina dolls to an American in Yokohama, for thirty yen. After Maruza, the antique dealer who brought the deposit, leaves, the mother sits sewing with tears welling in her eyes, and her son Eikichi — who prides himself on his modern, "enlightened" ways — laughs at her in an odd manner at the sight; Otsuru strikes her brother, he slaps her back, and the whole household falls into a quarrel. Before long the mother takes to her bed with a facial boil, and there is no more room to speak of the dolls. Even so, on the eve of the dolls' delivery, Otsuru begs her father, "as the one wish of my whole life," to let her see them once more — but he will not hear of it: "Once a deposit is taken, the goods belong to another." On the way home, her brother, resting his hand on the mudguard of the rickshaw, tells his sister that their father forbids it because seeing the dolls again would only stir up everyone's regret. That evening, a new lamp, replacing the old inextinguishable light, lit the storehouse brightly. Waking suddenly late that night, Otsuru finds, at her bedside, the profile of her aged father, who has taken out every single doll and set them all out, gazing at them alone. Whether or not it was a dream, the old woman even now cannot say for certain. A piece capturing one night in a household as the old order passes away, in an age when the old was giving way to the new.

The opening (original text)

"Faces I could not forget, coming out of their box — a pair of dolls" — Buson This is the tale of an old woman. ...It was around November that the promise was made to sell the dolls to a certain American in Yokohama. My family, called Kinokuniya, had for generations served the great feudal lords in matters of money, and my grandfather in particular, called Shichiku, had risen to become one of the great men of finance in his day, so that the dolls, though they were mine, were quite splendidly made.

Hina by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

As for why I say it might have been a dream — ah, that I have already told you. But was that night's viewing of the dolls truly a dream? Might it not have been an illusion, conjured up unawares out of my own single-minded longing to see the dolls once more? Even now, sometimes, I find myself at a loss to say whether it was true or not, even to myself. But this much is certain: that late that night, I saw my aged father, alone, gazing at the dolls. This much I am sure of. And so, even if it was a dream, I feel no particular regret over it. For in any case, I saw before my very eyes a father no different at all from the father I had always known — a father, weak and womanish, and yet, for all that, solemn.

Hina by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)
