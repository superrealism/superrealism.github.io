About this work

A short story published in Shashin Hōchi in 1925. On the seventh night after the death of his shy younger brother, dead at twenty, the narrator turns the pages of his diary and reads out of those neat entries a secret love and a tragedy of crossed signals turning on a code. A cipher hidden in the way a stamp was stuck on, and his brother's pitiful mistake in reading it — the pleasure of deduction and grief for a young dead man meeting quietly. A lyrical piece from Ranpo's early work.

Summary

On the night of the seventh day after the death, "I" take up the diary of my shy younger brother, dead at twenty, in his study. Turning the pages and pitying a brother who died without ever knowing love, I notice that he had secretly cherished a feeling for a certain woman — Yukie — and that the two seem to have exchanged letters. In the diary is set down the strange private signal they used: a cipher in which meaning was given to the direction in which the stamp was stuck on the envelope. As I decipher it, a heartbreaking crossing of purposes between my brother and Yukie comes to light. Painstaking as he was about the cipher he had devised himself, he was dull at reading hers, and for that his love was never fulfilled. And what maddened my breast was not simple pity for the two of them. Two months before my brother's death, an engagement had been settled — between Yukie and myself. Exposing my burning head to the frozen wind of the winter night, I am struck down by a fact that cannot be taken back. An unforgettable early piece of Ranpo's, putting grief for a young dead man into the devices of cipher and deduction.

The opening (original text)

It was on the night of exactly the seventh day after the death. I had gone into my dead brother's study, and taking out this and that of the things he had left written, I was sunk in my own thoughts. The night was not yet far advanced, and yet the whole house was damp with tears and utterly still. And on top of that — it sounds rather like a play of the new school, I know — from far off came the calls of the street vendors, in a tone that sounded truly sorrowful. I fell into a childlike, deeply felt mood I had long forgotten, and happening upon my brother's diary lying there, I opened it and looked.

Nikkichō by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Ah, merely by opening my brother's diary I had touched upon a fact that cannot be taken back. In what words shall I describe how I felt then? Had it been only grief at the pitiable failure of two young people, it would have been well enough. But there was in me another, more selfish feeling. To put my burning head into the frozen wind of the winter night, I thrust my feet into a pair of garden clogs that stood there and went unsteadily down into the garden. And with my heart just as disordered as it was, I walked round and round among the trees, endlessly. Thinking of the engagement between Yukie and myself, settled two months or so before my brother's death, which cannot be taken back.

Nikkichō by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)
