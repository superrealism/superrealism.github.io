About this work

A short piece Hara Tamiki wrote, on receiving the collection Pastoral from Hori Tatsuo, about the style he longs for and about where he himself is going. He wishes he could become a man capable of being moved by himself, quotes Rilke's last words, and is lashed by Flaubert's line that he is himself the desert, and at the same time the traveller, and the camel. Written a little over a year before his suicide, it leaves the quiet sentence wondering whether his own road has not already been decided.

Summary

Hori Tatsuo has sent him a beautiful book with his signature in it, Bokka. That small collection by a man whom he only reveres from a distance, and has never once met, seemed to him a flower blooming in a desolate place. As he read it, Hara found himself thinking about prose style. A style bright and quiet and clear and dear; a style that seems a little to indulge itself and yet holds something stern and deep; a style beautiful as a dream and yet as certain as reality — this is the style he longs for. But in the end a style must be nothing but the reflection of the mind that makes it. ... Thinking that four or five readers would be enough, he asks himself whether he is a reader of his own work, and reads his earlier pieces over. What he had written with his heart in it seemed able to move him after all. Quoting Rilke's words on finishing the last of the Elegies, he thinks how good it would be if a day came when he could say such a thing. When he told a friend that once he had written what he wanted to write he would like to go quickly to the other world, Okuno Shintarō telephoned him: "You must not die." That someone should worry about him made him glad. A letter of Flaubert's, who expected nothing of life but the writing of his work, whips his heart. And in the margin of The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge, which he carries with him, Hara has written this: autumn of the twenty-fourth year of Shōwa — may it not be that my road is already decided?

The opening (original text)

Mr. Hori Tatsuo was good enough to send me a beautiful book with his signature in it, called Bokka. I only revere Mr. Hori from a distance and have never yet met him; but this seemed to me like a flower blooming in a desolate place. Reading this small collection, I found myself thinking about prose style.

Sabaku no hana by Hara Tamiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

From of old there seem to be more than enough sturdy writers and great writers. But for me the writers I feel drawn to and hold dear seem to grow fewer and fewer. In the margin of The Notebooks of Malte Laurids Brigge, which I carry about with me in my wanderings, I lately wrote this. Autumn of the twenty-fourth year of Shōwa: may it not be that my road is already decided? If I can bear the desolation and let one thread of what is dear seep through, it seems there is nothing more to wish for.

Sabaku no hana by Hara Tamiki (Aozora Bunko)
