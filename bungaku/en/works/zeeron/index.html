About this work

Makino Shin'ichi's central work, and a fantastical short story compressing the manner called the Greek Makino, in which he likens the scenery of his native Ashigara to Greek myth and mediaeval chivalry. The narrator loads a bronze bust modelled on himself onto the back of the old packhorse Zeeron and sets out to carry it to a friend in the mountains, and that one day's journey is drawn in an unrestrained style mixing humour and fancy. The summit of Makino's writing, where reality and delusion dissolve into each other.

Summary

Having settled all his affairs — books, furniture, debts, and the rest — in preparation for a "new life close to nature," "I" find myself at a loss over what to do with a single bronze bust that cannot be sold: a portrait of myself, titled "Statue of Mr. Makino," made by the sculptor Kyōkawa. I resolve to carry it to my elder friend Fujiya, who lives deep in the mountain gorges, and beg him to keep it safe. Packing the bust into an especially sturdy mountaineering rucksack, and setting out with a stout walking stick and a mountain knife at my side, I borrow a cart-horse named Zeeron from a watermill, meaning to make for Mount Yagura, which resembles a seated Daruma doll. But Zeeron proves no fine steed at all — merely a decrepit, lame, stubborn old drudge of a horse who will not obey. As I struggle up the difficult mountain path, the heavy bust on my back and the obstinate horse before me, the boundary between reality and fantasy begins to dissolve in my mind. The statue on my back seems to come alive and dance; the forest looks like the bottom of a pond; the crows look like fish; Zeeron and I both seem to have sprouted gills. Even as the heavy load tears at the skin of my back, I find myself entranced by these visions, half-thinking I might as well fling this troublesome bust into the depths of Onirui Marsh and be done with it.

Characters

  • I　A self-professed Stoic, an impoverished wandering writer. He carries a bronze bust modeled on himself to a friend deep in the mountains
  • Zeeron　A cart-horse borrowed from a watermill in Tsukada village. Bold in name only — an old, stubborn, lame drudge of a horse
  • Fujiya Hachirō　My respected elder friend. A scholar of Greek classics and chivalric literature, who calls his mountain-gorge estate "Pierrefonds"
  • Kyōkawa Makio　An impoverished sculptor. Spent two years making the "Statue of Mr. Makino"

The opening (original text)

Having finished settling every last matter — books, furniture, debts, all of it — in preparation for a new life close to nature, I found myself, at the very last, at a loss over the disposal of one bronze bust that could not be sold. I packed it into an especially sturdy, oversized mountaineering rucksack, took up a thick walking stick, tucked a mountain knife at my side, and set out. Since the newly planned scheme of my new life was already close at hand, this errand had to be dispatched as swiftly as possible. And so, early that morning, I flung myself, dressed in hasty mountaineering clothes, aboard the express train departing from Shinjuku, got off at Kashiwa Station, four stops short of the terminus, and, without pausing so much as to catch my breath, hurried on foot about a league north to Tsukada village, where, as planned, I had to borrow the cart-horse Zeeron from a watermill I knew there.

The ending (original text)

Delivering ceaseless assaults upon Zeeron's hindquarters, I came at last to a forest that resembled the bottom of a marsh. The treetops looked like waterweed at the bottom of the pond; looking up at the "surface of the water," the crows flying home to roost looked like fish, and it seemed as though both Zeeron and I had sprouted gills. And yet — the skin of my back, torn open by the heavy load, stung as though scalded, water seeping searingly into the wound! Could it be blood flowing there instead? I wondered.
