About this work

Chōbei, a first-rate plasterer of Honjo, has ruined his household through an unmatched love of gambling; at the year's end he swears to reform on the fifty ryō his daughter Ohisa has raised by selling herself to the Yoshiwara. On the way home, however, he meets the young shop-servant Bunshichi about to drown himself at the riverside, and throws him the fifty ryō — all he has. Edo stubbornness and Edo kindness tangle together toward a happy ending in one of the best-known human-feeling stories of San'yūtei Enchō, still performed today.

Summary

Chōbei, a plasterer living in Daruma Yokochō in Honjo, has the skill of two men and has ruined himself with gambling, and at the end of the year comes home stripped of everything he had. Unable to bear the sight of her father, his daughter O-Hisa goes of her own accord to the Kadoebi in Yoshiwara, and through the mistress's kindness fifty ryō is handed to Chōbei — on the understanding that if it is not repaid by the last day of next year, O-Hisa will have to go out to the customers. Moved by his daughter's sacrifice and resolved to mend his ways, Chōbei on his way home finds a young man about to throw himself off the Azuma bridge. He is Bunshichi, a clerk of the Ōmiya, a tortoiseshell wholesaler in Shirogane-chō, who believes that the fifty ryō of collected accounts entrusted to him by his master has been picked from his pocket, and means to die by way of apology. Telling him plainly that this is money his daughter sold herself to raise, but that a life is worth more, Chōbei throws him the fifty ryō and walks away. But Bunshichi's fifty ryō had in fact been delivered and never stolen at all. The next day the master of the Ōmiya, having learned what happened, calls at Chōbei's house, returns the fifty ryō, and asks to adopt Bunshichi as his son. And what comes carried up in a palanquin, announced as a present of fish, is O-Hisa, whom the Ōmiya has bought out. Bunshichi and O-Hisa are married, and with the master's help set up a shop for Bunshichi motoyui cord in Kōjimachi — a happy ending indeed.

Characters

  • Chōbei　A plasterer of Daruma Yokochō in Honjo. An obstinate but warm-hearted man of Edo, with the skill of two men and ruined by gambling
  • O-Hisa　Chōbei's daughter, who sells herself to Yoshiwara to bring her father to his senses
  • O-Kane　Chōbei's wife
  • Bunshichi　A clerk of the Ōmiya, a tortoiseshell wholesaler of Shirogane-chō, who tries to drown himself believing his collections have been stolen
  • The master of the Ōmiya　Bunshichi's employer, who learns the truth, returns the fifty ryō, buys O-Hisa out and makes a match of them
  • The mistress of the Kadoebi　The mistress of the house in Yoshiwara, who takes pity on O-Hisa and lends Chōbei the fifty ryō

The opening (original text)

It is a short piece, and I shall tell you the somewhat old story of the origin of Bunshichi motoyui. Things were very different in the time of the Tokugawa from what they are now. In the old days there were men who called themselves idlers, and simply lived without working. It was quite possible then to live and eat while doing nothing. And how did they manage to live while idling? By doing what was forbidden under heaven. Nowadays such things are strictly dealt with, so strictly that one might think it impossible even to do them in secret — and yet you will see signboards hung up in plain sight, with a fire-lighter's die dangling and the flower cards laid out; and if one buys them and plays openly at the shopfront, since it is no hindrance to anyone's pleasure, there is no reproof at all. To do it in secret and gamble for money on a large scale suits those who have plenty of money and nothing to do with their tedium, who do not care whether they win or lose and mean only to pass the time; but when a man who lives from day to day, who cannot eat unless he goes out to work, takes it up, his trade naturally goes to ruin. Since it is done for gain, he never tires of it, and plays on, and stays away from his work three days running, and it becomes a hindrance; which is why the authorities speak strictly of it. And yet the men who gamble and drink and are hopelessly idle are often, as it happens, good craftsmen: men who, if they only put their backs into the work, could earn a great deal and live very well, and it is a pity they are so idle — and they are the very ones with the good hands. In Daruma Yokochō in Honjo there was a plasterer named Chōbei, who did the work of two men, quick and neat, clean at the edges, one of the few who could lay a coat as even as a sugar cake without a blemish; and it was said that if he plastered a storehouse door no fire would ever get in, so that whatever craftsman built the storehouse, the door was always given to Chōbei — such was his skill; and a thorough idler he was, all the same.

Bunshichi mottoi by San'yūtei Enchō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

MASTER: Come now, she must be here by this time. And saying it he opened the paper door; and — as things were in those days — a four-hand palanquin came flying up with three tattooed bearers, set down at the mouth of the alley; and when the curtain was lifted, out came O-Hisa, dressed quite differently from yesterday, splendid, stepping out of the palanquin. O-HISA: Father, I have come home. CHŌBEI: Ah — O-Hisa... how do you come to be here? O-HISA: The master of the tortoiseshell shop here has bought me out, and I have come home. O-KANE: Oh, O-Hisa, you are back! And starting up, and feeling awkward, she turned about and hid behind the screen. And after this Bunshichi and O-Hisa were married, and the master set them up with a branch of his name, and they opened the shop for Bunshichi motoyui at Kōjimachi Rokuchōme — a happy story indeed.
