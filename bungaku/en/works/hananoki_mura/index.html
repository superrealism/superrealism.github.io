About this work

The title piece of the collection of children's stories by Niimi Nankichi published in 1943, the year he died. It draws in his own warm hand how five thieves who come to the peaceful village of Hananoki in early summer give up, through a meeting with one child, the mind for wrongdoing. Who was it, in the end, that saved a village of good-hearted people from the thieves? A central children's story by the author of "Gon the Fox," full of plain humour and trust in human beings.

Summary

On an early-summer afternoon, when young bamboo shoots were stretching skyward and the pine cicadas were singing, a band of five thieves came to the peaceful village of Hananoki. Slipping into the village to scout it out for a robbery, they scattered off, each in his own direction — but every one of them was too good-natured a fellow to truly become a thief, and none of them could bring himself to go through with the wickedness. The leader of the band, exchanging words with a child of the village, is touched by the boy's innocence and by the goodness of the villagers' hearts, and little by little forgets his intent to steal. His companions, too, are each won over in turn by the village's peaceful ways and its warmth of feeling, until at last all five give up their wicked trade together and mend their ways. Afterward the villagers try to find the child who saved them from the thieves' harm, but however they search, he is nowhere to be found. And so they settle on the belief that it must have been the little stone Jizō by the earthen bridge, who had put on straw sandals and walked among them. It was precisely because the village was home to such good-hearted people that Jizō came to save them from the thieves — a fine example of Nankichi's fairy tales, filled with simple humor and trust in humanity.

Characters

  • The five thieves　Break into Hananoki village to steal, but are too good-natured to fully commit to wickedness. Won over by the village's warmth, they mend their ways
  • The leader of the thieves　Meets a village child and, touched by his innocence, forgets his intent to steal
  • The child (Jizō)　The child who leads the thieves to reform. The villagers later come to believe he was the incarnation of the stone Jizō by the earthen bridge

The opening (original text)

One Once upon a time, a band of five thieves came to the village of Hananoki. It was an early-summer noon, when the young bamboo, here and there against the sky, was stretching out its slender, tender green shoots, and in the pine woods the pine cicadas were singing jii-jii-jiii.

Hananoki mura to nusubito tachi by Niimi Nankichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

That Jizō should have put on straw sandals and walked about is, to be sure, a strange thing, but it seems to me that the world may well allow for a wonder or two of that size. And besides, since this all happened so long ago, it hardly matters either way. But if it really were true, then it was because the people of Hananoki were all such good-hearted people that Jizō came to save them from the thieves. And if that is so, then it follows, too, that a village must be a place where good-hearted people make their home.
