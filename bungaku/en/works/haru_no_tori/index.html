About this work

The narrator, a teacher in a provincial town, meets Rokuzō, an idiot boy led by the hand by a beautiful mother, on the castle hill where the ruins stand. Rokuzō cannot speak properly, but plays innocently at imitating birds, and the narrator is drawn to him and tries to teach him to read and write. But nothing reaches the boy's mind. In time Rokuzō throws himself from a high stone wall, trying to fly like a bird — a fine late short story by Doppo, drawing in rich lyricism the pitifulness of a boy and the grief of those who mourn him.

Summary

Six or seven years ago, "I" was teaching English and mathematics in a certain provincial town. The town had a hill called Castle Hill, where the ruins of an old castle remained, and I would always climb it whenever I went for a walk. One day, on Castle Hill, I meet a boy being led by the hand by a beautiful mother. His name is Rokuzō — a child born mentally impaired, an idiot, who can barely speak. But he spreads both arms and mimics the flapping of a bird's wings, and skillfully imitates the cawing of a crow, and plays about with an innocent charm. Drawn by his loveliness, I try to teach Rokuzō to read and write. But however patiently I teach him, not the slightest trace of letters or words will take root in the boy's mind. Even so, Rokuzō grows attached to me, and comes to spend his time with me on Castle Hill. One day, Rokuzō stands atop the high stone wall of Castle Hill, spreads both arms as always — as though this time he truly believed he would become a bird and fly — and leaps out into the air, and falls to his death. His mother, having lost her own child, tells me, her eyes changed with feeling, of how much Rokuzō had loved birds. Just then, a single crow rises up from the woods of Castle Hill, and the mother watches it go, lost in a daze. A lyrical masterwork of Doppo's later years, moving the reader with the pathos of a boy who died dreaming of flight, and the sorrow of his mother.

Characters

  • I　A teacher of English and mathematics in a certain provincial town. The narrator, who meets Rokuzō on Castle Hill and is drawn to him
  • Rokuzō　A boy born mentally impaired. He loves to mimic birds, and dies trying to fly through the air
  • Rokuzō's mother　A beautiful mother who deeply cherishes Rokuzō. She mourns her child's death

The opening (original text)

Six or seven years ago now, I was once a teacher of English and mathematics in a certain province. In that town there was a hill called Castle Hill, thick with great dark trees, not very high, but rich in scenery, and so I would always climb it whenever I went out walking. At its summit the ruins of the castle still remained. Ivy and creeping vines twined about the high stone walls, and the way they turned a deep crimson had a beauty beyond words. Where the great keep had once stood was now level ground, where dwarf pines had sprung up here and there over time and the summer grasses grew thick without a gap, presenting a sight that could not help but call the past to mind, in all its pathos.

Haru no tori by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Yes, Roku loved birds so. Whenever he saw a bird he'd spread out both his own hands, just like this" — and the mother mimicked the flapping of a bird's wings — "and go flying about all over the place, just like this. Yes, and he was so good at mimicking the crow's cry, too" — and watching her tell it, her eyes changing with feeling, I could not help but shut my own eyes. Just as a single crow rose lazily from the woods of Castle Hill and flew off toward the shore, crying two or three times as it went, the idiot boy's mother suddenly broke off her talk and watched it go, lost in a daze, forgetting even herself. What, I wonder, did Rokuzō's mother see in that one crow?
