About this work

A short story published in the magazine Shinchō in May 1919, built on something the author saw on the Yokosuka line. Worn out and listless, the narrator watches a young girl throw mandarin oranges from a train window to her younger brothers, and is unexpectedly lifted by it. A few pages only, yet it catches the instant of brightness that falls across a dull day in a vivid contrast of colour, and has long been taught in schools as a showcase of Akutagawa's technique.

Summary

On a clouded winter evening the narrator sits in a second-class carriage on the up train from Yokosuka, waiting for it to start, weighed down by a weariness and tedium he cannot name. A country girl with chapped cheeks comes bustling into the second-class carriage clutching a third-class ticket. Her shabby look and her want of manners irritate him, and he drops his eyes to the evening paper to forget she is there; but the commonplace items in its columns seem to him too a symbol of a life that is unintelligible and base. When the train enters a tunnel the girl goes out of her way to open the window, which had been shut, and the soot pouring in sets him coughing violently. He is on the point of scolding her when the train comes to a crossing at the edge of a town, and there stand three small boys, raising their voices and waving. The girl leans half out of the window and throws down to her brothers five or six mandarins she had been keeping in her breast. In the instant he understands that a sister on her way to service is repaying the brothers who came to see her off, the color of the mandarins, dyed with the warm light of the sun, burns itself into him — and he is able, for a moment, to forget his unintelligible, base and tedious life.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, who boards the train weighed down by weariness and tedium
  • The girl　A country girl on her way out to service, who throws mandarins to the brothers seeing her off at the crossing

The opening (original text)

It was the dusk of a clouded winter day. I had taken a seat in the corner of a second-class carriage on the up train from Yokosuka and was waiting blankly for the whistle. The lamps had long since been lit, and, unusually, there was not one other passenger besides myself. Looking outside, I saw that even the figures of people seeing others off had for once vanished from the dim platform; there was only a puppy in a cage, barking now and then in a mournful way.

The ending (original text)

At some point the girl had returned to the seat opposite mine, and there she sat as before, her chapped cheeks buried in her light-green woolen muffler, holding her third-class ticket tight in the hand that clasped her large bundle. .......... And it was then, for the first time, that I was able to forget a little of my nameless weariness and tedium, and of this unintelligible, base and boring life.

Famous passages

It was only then, for the first time, that I was able, if only for a moment, to forget my inexpressible weariness and ennui, and this incomprehensible, base, and tedious life.

The closing line, just after watching a girl toss mandarin oranges from the train window to her little brothers. A gray landscape takes on color for just one moment — the clearest ending among Akutagawa's short pieces.

The level crossing on the edge of town in the gathering dusk, the three children crying out like little birds, and over them the vivid color of the tangerines falling helter-skelter — all of it passed outside the train window in the blink of an eye.

The scene in which a young girl going into service throws tangerines from the train window to the little brothers who have come to see her off. The vivid color scattering over the crossing at dusk transforms, in an instant, the weary heart of the narrator.
