About this work

A piece of self-examination Shimamura Hōgetsu placed in 1909 in lieu of a preface to his collection Studies in Modern Literature. The man regarded as the theoretical support of naturalism begins by writing that he is not yet qualified to discuss a view of life, and exposes without reserve that what moves him is neither faith nor truth but resignation and knowledge. Beneath morality and beneath sympathy alike stands the self — and before a knowledge that has seen this, every view of life loses its value as belief. The conclusion he reaches is that this is an age of confession. An essay driving the intellectual core of naturalism into himself rather than into others.

Summary

Setting out to sum up the essays on art of his last two or three years, Hōgetsu felt the need of backing them with a view of life. But is a view of life not the declaring of an ideal or a conclusion for a life of action? If so, he has as yet no right to discuss it — coming near his fortieth year and looking back, his heart is still like an empty ruined house, all makeshift patching of a broken floor and a leaking roof, he begins. What, then, rules those days of his? It is, after all, nothing but the stale ordinary morality. But he cannot believe it the highest thing; and though there is discontent and rebellion and scorn and doubt and despair in him, he has not the courage to trample it deliberately underfoot, and is dragged along by the force of custom. What is there in the end is only the resignation of "there is nothing to be done." What is it that makes him resign himself? Not God and not faith, but his own knowledge, he answers. Break morality, and the life of one's own next future is at once threatened. Name and profit and person and family — at the bottom of each there is always the one stone of "one's own life." Cheap sympathy too grows thicker the nearer its object is to oneself, which is a certain fact; and even acts in which one becomes a machine for duty or for an ideal run up against the doubt that they may be a transformation of self-love. Thus knowledge accomplishes ordinary morality as an expedient, and at the same time strips it of all sublimity and all solemnity. Open one's eyes, and what shows its face is neither god nor Buddha but the self — and yet this new morality of the self stirs not the least feeling of reverence. Having lost the object of adoration and longing, an ash-gray heaven and earth lies before him without end. Hōgetsu names this the stirring of the first desire, or the religious desire, and, unable to give up the wish for the color of red blood and the color of blue sky, acknowledges a life of uncertainty and unease wandering between two poles. The heart of one who can believe is at peace; the heart of one who criticizes is always restless. Here he comes to want to curse his own overbearing knowledge itself. Therefore he cannot now set up a settled view of life; now he is fit only to confess, as it is, his own doubt and uncertainty. "Put away falsehood, forget ornament, look sharply at your own present state, and having looked, confess it in earnest." In this sense, he declares, ours is an age of confession. As an appendix he examines one by one those things that history calls "naturalism in the view of life" — Rousseau's "back to nature," Nietzsche's doctrine of instinct, the nihilism of Turgenev and Tolstoy — and concludes that every one of them presents to him only one fact within his doubt, and is no final solution.

The opening (original text)

Now, setting out to sum up the essays on art of my last two or three years and to bring my thought to a stage, I have felt the need of backing them with a view of life. But what, after all, is a view of life? The central meaning of human life is, needless to say, action. Is a view of life not an idea arrived at concerning what appears to be the purpose of a life of action, what appears to be its general direction? Is it not the declaring of an ideal or a conclusion for a life of action? If it is so, then I have as yet no right to discuss a view of life. For my present condition with regard to my own life of action recognizes no clear ideal and no conclusion whatever. What is the purpose of human life? What ought we to make the ideal of our living? I do not understand it in the least.

In Place of a Preface: On Naturalism as a View of Life by Shimamura Hōgetsu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Therefore I am at present unfit to set up a settled view of life. Now I am rather fit to confess, as it is, my doubt and uncertainty. So far is truth; beyond that there is a danger of fabrication. And taking myself for a standard and looking over the world, I suspect that those who discuss views of life are all of them at much the same point as I am. If so, then the whole world may be in an age of confession. Put away falsehood, forget ornament, look sharply at your own present state, and having looked, confess it in earnest. Is there in the world today any text more fitting than this? In this sense, ours is an age of confession. Or it may be that man can never at any time rise above the age of confession.
