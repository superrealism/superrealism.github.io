About this work

The story of Miura Uemon, the beautiful page who had all of Imagawa Ujizane's favor, who flees with the fall of his master's house, is stripped of everything by peasants, and is betrayed by the castle commander he turns to and loses his life. Uemon, who goes on saying "only spare my life" to the very end, is derided by the warriors of the warring states who held death the honorable thing; but the author sees in him the discovery that "here too is a human being." A representative work of Kikuchi Kan's early historical fiction, which turns the whole value of the samurai code inside out.

Summary

On the day at the end of the Tenshō era when the Imagawa seat at Fuchū fell to the forces of Oda, Miura Uemon, a page of seventeen, abandoned his lord, left his horse behind, and fled toward the castle of Takatenjin. Scuffling with the village children of whom he asked the road, and naming himself out of vexation, he is recognized as the favorite who brought the house of Imagawa down. Surrounded by Yasōji and his men, who make a trade of hunting fugitives, he is stripped of everything including his gold-mounted sword, and is taunted — say that you want to live, your bow is not low enough — until he bows his head to the ground and is let go in nothing but a loincloth. Two days later he reaches Takatenjin and turns to Amano Gyōbu, the castle commander on whom he had put an obligation in his years as a hostage. Gyōbu receives him warmly at first; but on learning that Ujizane has taken his own life and that Uemon has fled, he decides to offer his head to Oda as proof that he harbors no second thoughts. Bound and told that this is the punishment for disloyalty, Uemon only weeps aloud and puts his hands down and begs for his life. Gyōbu, mocking him, demands one arm in exchange for his life, then both hands, then his right leg, and each time Uemon nods and lets it be done. Set there in his blood, his mouth still working, he is beheaded at the last. The narrator sets it down that among the records of the warring states, filled with brave men and heroes, here too was one human being.

Characters

  • Miura Uemon　A page of seventeen who had Imagawa Ujizane's favor. He clings to his life to the very end
  • Yasōji　A man of the village who makes a trade of hunting fugitives
  • Amano Gyōbu　The commander of Takatenjin castle, who receives Uemon warmly and then means to kill him to prove his loyalty to Oda

The opening (original text)

It is a country place not far from Fuchū in Suruga. It was one fiercely hot summer day at the end of the Tenshō era. For ten days past there had been nothing but days like it. Under that burning sky, along the highroad four or five chō away, men of the Oda forces had been passing all morning, one after another. All of them were sweating heavily, and with the dust sticking to the sweat their dark faces looked darker still. Yet, disturbed as the times were, the peasants pulling weeds in the paddies and treading the waterwheels were comparatively calm about it. For one thing, there was not a crop anywhere in sight that was worth plundering: there was the comfort of knowing that however greedy the Oda men might be, they would hardly cut rice that was still only in flower; and for another, they were so used to the alarms of war that they had settled into the wisdom of the English tradesmen and were carrying on business as usual.

The ending (original text)

"Uemon, do you still want your life?" said Gyōbu. But it seemed that Uemon could no longer hear. The swordsman put his mouth to Uemon's ear and said: "Do you want your life?" Uemon's mouth worked. At that moment Gyōbu gave the sign. The swordsman set his blade for the fourth time and, with a cry, struck off the head. The head rolled two or three feet over the sand, and where it stopped the mouth worked again. Had it not been parted from his lungs, it would surely have said, "I beg you, spare my life." When one reads the documents of the age of the warring states, there are heroes as thick as clouds in the sieges and the field battles, brave men who swing an iron bar of eighteen kan about like a hemp stalk, and champions who wrench off an enemy general's head; but one is always missing a human being who is human. When I read Asai Ryōi's "Inu Harikō" and learned of the end of Miura Uemon, I felt for the first time that I could not but say, "There is also a man."
