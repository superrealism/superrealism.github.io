About this work

A short story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in a special number of Shūkan Asahi in 1958, and one of his central pieces of common-people writing. It draws Jūkichi, a cabinetmaker in a back tenement in Edo, and his family: a clumsy father who lacks the knack of switching to more profitable work as the times require, and who escapes into drink and makes trouble for his household, and the wife and children who will not give him up all the same, caught in fine dialogue and humour. Known too as one of the sources of Tasaka Tomotaka's 1965 film.

Summary

Jūkichi, a cabinetmaker living in a back tenement at Nagasawa-chō in Nihonbashi, is an earnest man devoted to nothing but the making of gokiri braziers; but with the change of the times his work has grown thin, and on the evenings of the pay days he drinks too much and has to be looked after by his wife O-Nao and the children. Shinsuke and Shinjirō, who trained at the same shop with him, changed early to trades that follow the fashion and have prospered, and they urge Jūkichi to give up his obstinacy and turn to work that pays. Unable to refuse outright and torn with worry, Jūkichi drinks too much, brings home a poor man named Kisuke whom he does not know at all and gives him drink — and in the night their household goods are stolen. Convinced that he is the plague of his own family, Jūkichi tries to slip out of the house like a man running away by night; but his wife and children wake and hold him back. Ryōkichi, the eldest son, and the rest all insist that if he is going they are going too, and refuse to let the family be broken up. Touched by their love, Jūkichi gives up the thought, and chooses to leave the shop and go on making gokiri braziers with his own hands. And still on the pay-day evenings there is Jūkichi drinking too much and grumbling on; but the household that receives him, poor as it is, is full of an unchanging warmth.

Characters

  • Jūkichi (Jū-san)　A maker of gokiri braziers in Nihonbashi. The hero, clumsy and fond of drink, who cannot give up his pride in his craft
  • O-Nao　Jūkichi's wife. A stout-hearted, hard-working woman who holds the family together
  • Ryōkichi　Jūkichi's eldest son. At fourteen he helps the household as an apprentice at a fishmonger's that does catering
  • O-Tsugi　Jūkichi's eldest daughter. At thirteen she does piecework with her mother
  • Kamekichi　Jūkichi's second son, aged seven
  • O-Yoshi　Jūkichi's youngest daughter. At three she talks beyond her years
  • Shinsuke and Shinjirō　Jūkichi's former fellow apprentices, friends who turned early to trades that suited the times
  • Kisuke　A man without family whom Jūkichi brings home from his drinking

The opening (original text)

The people of that tenement could be sure of hearing Jū-san's spirited grumbling on the evening of the fourteenth and the last day of every month. It need hardly be said that the fourteenth and the last day were the pay days, the days when the craftsmen received their wages, and also the days when their families waited for the master of the house to come home with his wages.

Chan by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Come in, will you," O-Nao's voice can be heard saying, keeping it down. "You are troubling the neighbors. For pity's sake, come in." "I can't come in," Jūkichi answers easily. "I drank it all and spent it all, so there's hardly any money left, you see; so I won't." Ryōkichi takes her place, and then O-Yoshi's voice is heard. "Tan, henna," says O-Yoshi, putting on airs. "You say henna, don't you — henna, tan."

Chan by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
