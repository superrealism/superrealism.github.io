About this work

The second collection, put together by his wife Tomi from the papers Yagi Jūkichi left when he died of tuberculosis at twenty-nine. Poems of a few lines, pared down further than in Autumn Eyes, cross the clarity of a man with death before him with an inexhaustible affection for his small children and his mother. That the rain goes on soaking the earth; that he would like to crouch beside the thing called rain and watch what it does. A book of poems in the shape of prayer.

Summary

"At dusk / when I open my eyes / far away my mother in my home country too / opens her eyes" — the collection begins with that opening poem, "My Mother's Eyes." There is "Moon-Viewing," in which, struck by the moonlight, he wants to set out taro and pampas grass and bean curd; a poem of three lines that says, "I think the flowers are falling; / I think I shall catch them / in this palm of my hand"; and "Tears," in which he says that because everything was so dull he wept in the bright sunlight. The commonplace moments of daily life become poems just as they are. But the shadow of illness is heavy. "The wind sounds in the maize; / it says, die; / it says, die; / I think I shall go and die" ("The Wind Sounds"). In "My Child Is Ill," his own face grows huge and tries to cover the coughing child. And even so the poet sings of going ringing away across the great sky, and writes that he "throws away beautifully" the buds of the chrysanthemum. In the pieces near the end he wishes to crouch beside the rain and watch what it does, writes that when snow falls he feels funny little dwarfs are romping at the foot of a tree, and sets down only: "God, I have come to want to meet you." In the last piece he tells of the night of a fever when he fell asleep with Christ in his mind, and of the noble face that appeared in his dream, which he knew of itself to be his own. When he woke the fever had not gone down, but strangely his heart was at peace — so it closes.

The opening (original text)

My Mother's Eyes at dusk when I open my eyes far away my mother in my home country too opens her eyes and I feel that she is saying, poor dear little thing Moon-Viewing when the moon shines on me by the light of the moon my heart is struck and I want to set out washed taro and pampas grass and bean curd and to make a noise: moon-viewing! moon-viewing!

Famous passages

If, into this brightness, one plain, simple koto were placed, unable to bear the beauty of autumn, the koto would surely begin to sound, softly, of itself.

A poem of only four lines, “A Simple Koto.” A piece as limpid as an act of faith, in which a koto begins to sound of its own accord, unable to bear the brightness of autumn.
