About this work

A self-selected collection by Yamanokuchi Baku, born in Okinawa, who went on writing poems through twenty years of wandering and extreme poverty. Heavy subjects — poverty, marriage, his home country — sung in an easy humour and a transparent colloquial Japanese. Poems one cannot forget after a single reading, such as "A Landscape with Mourning," which begins with looking behind him and finding his parents there.

Summary

"I look behind me / it is my parents / behind my parents are their parents" — "A Landscape with Mourning," placed at the head of the book, opens with lines like these. Look back, and only the parents of parents of parents go on into the depths of the past; look forward, and only the children of children of children go on to the far edge of the future. And within that landscape, "a baton of God lies fallen / the blood-stained earth lies fallen." In the following poem, "The World Is a Various Place," he lines up, one after another: people eat rice; a beast called a baku, sharing his own name, eats dreams; sheep eat paper; bedbugs suck blood; and some people even come to eat other people — and then sings that in the Ryūkyūs there are voices that cry standing at graves, and trees that grow on tears. In "Tatami," onto tatami that had held nothing at all, a married life is summoned into being, and one by one there appear: himself, become a husband; a woman, become a wife; a paulownia chest of drawers; a kettle; a brazier; a dressing table. In "Charcoal," the poem draws the black hands, feet and face of the old charcoal-seller who says "there's no charcoal" when the poet goes to buy some, though the man has clearly just come out from among the charcoal himself. Every one of these poems takes the poet's own life of extreme poverty as its subject, and yet there is neither resentment nor self-pity in them. If anything, it is the distance from which Baku turns even himself into an object of laughter that keeps his poetry light and buoyant throughout. The poems in this collection are arranged in order of composition from the back of the book toward the front, so that the further one reads, the further back one travels into the poet's earliest years.

Famous passages

When walking wore me out I crawled into the gap between the night sky and the earth to sleep I slept buried in the grass I slept anywhere at all

The opening of “The Pattern of My Life.” After matter-of-factly piling up his days of sleeping rough, the poet, born in Okinawa, laments that when autumn comes he cannot sleep at all — his representative poem.
