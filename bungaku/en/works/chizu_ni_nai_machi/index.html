About this work

The man named Terauchi who told the narrator this story beat his own head against a pillar and died that night — one of Hashimoto Gorō's central works, published in Shin Seinen. An old man wrapped in a blanket speaks to a young man sunk penniless on a bench by the gourd pond at Asakusa: tobacco, bathhouse money, meals — all can be had free if you use the back ways of the city. And in time he is shown into a district that appears on no map, not even the General Staff's.

Summary

In a room in a lunatic asylum, Mr. Terauchi told "me" this story. About seven that evening he beat his head against the pillar of his room and died. It has all been settled as death from illness. — The story begins with a comical adventure he had in the spring of his twenty-odd year. ... Having lost his post and being without a penny, he threw his papers and his certificate and his family register and his empty purse into the Gourd Pond at Asakusa from a bench, and laughed in his despair. And at the other end of the same bench an old man wrapped in a ragged blanket laughed in the same way. The old man shared his bananas, got tobacco from somewhere, made up the price of a bath and took him to one, and put him up at a free lodging house. The next morning the old man tells him the back ways of the city. That if you go round twelve shooting galleries you can get tobacco; that in the busy quarters there are holes where ten and fifteen sen pieces have fallen; that if you dress as a laborer you can get into the union canteen. "A sin that has no dealings whatever with those around one is no sin at all in society." ... The old man says further that there is a "good thing" which money cannot buy, and makes him wait in Hibiya Park. A man with a dagger springs out of the trees, forces him to change his stand-collar student's uniform for his own Ōshima silk, and runs off. To Terauchi, now a fine young gentleman, the old man drew the way with a stone in the earth of the park. The line joining the Mitsukoshi and the station; the tobacconist with the white postbox; the alley that branches at the fourth house; the black board fence. "That town has no name; a town like that is not even on the General Staff map." If you walk there whistling, something good will surely happen. — He did as he was told, and spent an unforgettable week with a beautiful woman named Miyoko. But the moment he left that house, a motorcar followed him doggedly and tried to run him down.

The opening (original text)

The man called Terauchi who told me this story, I am told, made his illness worse and beat his head against the pillar of his room and died at about seven on the evening of the very day, at the end of November last year, on which I heard it and went home. Seven o'clock — it was less than three hours after he had seen me out. The acquaintance who mentioned it to me in the course of ordinary talk, seeing my unusual astonishment, told me that Mr. Terauchi's death was of course a suicide, but that properly it should be called a death from illness, and that everything had already been settled quite properly as a death from illness. But at that moment I was already doubting whether that "death from illness" could truly be called a death from illness.

Chizu ni nai machi by Hashimoto Gorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"The baby that woman took to the Kabuki — ah, that is certainly my child. Out of their one wish for a child they used me in that way. And having used me, they tried to kill me. The motorcar 11-1666 was the car that tried to run me down when I came out into the street for the first time in a long while from that strange town. I know the driver's face! And when at last I contrived to meet that old man — what do you think? — with their money and their power they ended by putting me into a place like this. The more I explain, the more I am made a madman; they have thrown me into this hell from which there is no escape. Ah, who, who will believe even a little of what I say? Who will acknowledge that child, the viscount that is to be, as my child?" In a fairly free room of the lunatic asylum, Mr. Terauchi told me this much of a story. When I heard that he had killed himself I remembered it vividly. Will the reader, then, take this story too for the words of a madman and not believe a word of it, not even think about it?
