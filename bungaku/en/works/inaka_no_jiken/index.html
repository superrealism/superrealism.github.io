About this work

A sequence of very short pieces published irregularly in Tantei Shumi, Ryōki and other magazines between 1927 and 1930. Yumeno sets out one after another the foolish or faintly sinister things he says he saw and heard in the country around his native Kitakyūshū, each in a few lines to a few pages. A rice thief caught with absurd ease, a scandal set off by a masseur, an uncanny business that shook a village: country oddities and true stories, told in Yumeno's characteristically deadpan voice and black humour. No great detective and no great case, but an odd, faint comedy and dread hang over the whole.

Summary

"All of them are things that happened in a certain district of northern Kyushu, my home country, and all of them things I saw or heard myself" — with that disclaimer the author sets down one small country incident after another. In "A Big Clue," a gambler who has stolen polished rice from the village headman's granary is caught with absurd ease, fast asleep beside the cart loaded with the stolen bales. In "The Masseur's Daytime Fire," a masseur touches off a ridiculous commotion. There are foolish blunders, uncanny happenings that make the flesh creep, and incidents in which human greed and stupidity show through, each cut short and set beside the next. Some of them are events so small that they went into the newspaper as items of five or six lines. Deliberately withholding places and names, the author tells the odd stories he remembers in a level voice, and says that their very foolishness may, on the contrary, take the fancy of readers who live in the city. A sequence of short pieces, rich in deadpan narration and black humor, in which Yumeno Kyūsaku, the master of detective fiction, drew on his own province for material.

The opening (original text)

Someone made off with four bales of polished rice from the granary at the village headman's place. Early the next morning the policeman from the station came to look into it, and there in the rain-softened ground were the clear tracks of the wheels of a cart that seemed to have carried the bales away. Following the tracks, they came to a solitary house at the edge of a village on the road to town, and under its eaves stood the cart with the rice bales on it; and on the bench beside it, with a cloth tied round his head, a man lay sprawled out at full length, snoring away.

Inaka no jiken by Yumeno Kyūsaku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

All of them are things that happened in a certain district of northern Kyushu, my home country, and all of them things I saw or heard myself. Some went into the newspaper as items of five or six lines; but thinking that their very foolishness might on the contrary take the fancy of those who live in the city, I have written down as many as I remember. Since there is the matter of the places concerned, please forgive me for having left out the places and the names.

Inaka no jiken by Yumeno Kyūsaku (Aozora Bunko)
