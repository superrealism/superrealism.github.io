What is Kōgo Jiyūshi (Colloquial Free Verse)?

Category
Form / Genre

Poetry written in everyday spoken language (kōgo), in a free form unbound by fixed syllable counts, replacing the shintaishi that had depended on the written language and fixed patterns such as the 7-5 meter. Kawaji Ryūkō's "Chirijiri" ("Scattered," 1907), published in the magazine "Shijin," is regarded as the first work of the form, and it spread in resonance with the spirit of Naturalist literature, which sought to depict reality just as it was. In the Taishō era, Takamura Kōtarō, in "Dōtei" ("The Journey," 1914), set humanist vitality to powerful colloquial language, and Hagiwara Sakutarō, in "Howling at the Moon" (1917), sublimated a morbidly acute sensibility into the musicality of colloquial verse, bringing colloquial free verse to completion as an art form. Murō Saisei's "Ai no Shishū" ("A Collection of Poems of Love," 1918), spoken of alongside Sakutarō, and Sakutarō's own second poetry collection, "Aoneko" ("The Blue Cat," 1923), deepened this current still further, and thereafter modern Japanese poetry came to take colloquial free verse as its standard form.
