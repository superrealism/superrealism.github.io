Famous passages by Miyazawa Kenji

Well-known lines from the works of Miyazawa Kenji.

42 passages

Hoshi meguri no uta

The scorpion with its red eye the eagle with wings spread wide the little dog with its blue eye the coiled serpent of light.

A song of Kenji's own composition, also sung in “Night on the Galactic Railroad.” Calling up the constellations by color and shape alone, it turns the night sky itself into a children's song.

Yukiwatari

The snow had frozen through, harder than marble, and the sky too seemed made of a cold, smooth slab of blue stone. “Hard snow, kanko; frozen snow, shinko.”

The opening of the story. The children's chant bounces with the joy of a winter day when the snow is frozen hard enough to walk anywhere across the fields, and it leads on to their meeting with a young fox.

The Wild Pear

Two baby crabs were talking at the bottom of the pale blue-green water. “Kurambon laughed.” “Kurambon laughed, kapukapu.”

A scene from a story that depicts the bottom of a mountain stream as “two blue slides of a magic lantern.” With nothing more than the unexplained word “kurambon,” the world of the young crabs springs vividly to life.

And with that, my magic-lantern show comes to an end.

The closing of a story told as “two blue slides of a magic lantern.” As the narrator draws the curtain, the world of the mother and baby crabs quietly recedes.

Spring and Asura

The phenomenon called I is a single blue illumination of a hypothesized, organic alternating-current lamp

The opening of the preface to the poetry collection “Spring and Asura.” A cosmology mingling science and faith, in which the self is grasped as a single flickering light, is placed at the very threshold of Kenji's poetry.

My sister, who before this day is out will go far away — sleet is falling, and outside it is strangely bright

The opening of “The Morning of Last Farewell,” sung on the day his sister Toshi died. The figure of a brother running out to fetch sleet engraves the deepest sorrow found anywhere in Kenji's poetry.

The bitterness of anger, and its blueness— at the bottom of the light of April's strata of air, spitting, gnashing my teeth, pacing back and forth, I am a single Asura.

The central lines of the title poem, “Spring and Asura.” Amid the light of spring, the poet depicts himself, walking with his anger and anguish, as an Asura—a dweller in the Buddhist realm of strife.

What we raised the alarm about as a fire just now was a rainbow. It has held, taut and clear, for a full hour already.

The short two-line poem “Report.” Conveying a rainbow so vivid it was mistaken for a fire in the polite tone of an official report, it captures surprise and humor in a single moment of landscape.

Thank you, my brave little sister. I too will go straight on.

A passage from “The Morning of Last Farewell.” Realizing that his dying sister asked him to fetch sleet in order to comfort him, Kenji tells her, with gratitude, of his resolve to live on.

The sword is lightning, the rustle of pampas plumes; the rain of fire scattering in the constellation of the Lion vanishes without a trace across the river of heaven; striking and perishing, all is one life.

The close of “Harakitai Sword Dance Troupe.” It depicts the sword dance, a folk performing art of Iwate, in images of lightning and a rain of fire scattering among the constellations. The line “striking and perishing, all is one life” gives the dance a cosmic sense of life.

The Restaurant of Many Orders

Two young gentlemen, dressed up exactly like English soldiers, shouldering gleaming rifles, and leading two dogs as white as polar bears, were walking deep in the mountains, over a stretch of rustling fallen leaves, saying things like this.

An opening that begins with the figures of two gentlemen who make light of the mountain. It opens an ironic children's story in which the one who “orders” and the one who is ordered eventually change places.

But their faces, which had once crumpled up like paper, never went back to how they had been — not even after they returned to Tokyo, not even after a hot bath.

The final line, which coldly casts off the two men, saved and back out of the mountain. Only the single fact that their terror-twisted faces never recover again remains, as their punishment.

“Please, everyone, do come in. There is no need to hold back.”

The first of the notices posted at the entrance of the Wildcat House. The two gentlemen read it in the way that suits them and rejoice, but on a second reading a hidden meaning appears. This wordplay sets the story's trap in motion.

The Acorns and the Wildcat

“Very well. Silence, all of you. Here is my verdict. Whichever among you is the least splendid, the most foolish, the most hopeless, the most utterly worthless, the one who seems to have his head squashed flat — he is the most splendid of all.”

The verdict the wildcat, coached by Ichirō, hands down to the acorns squabbling over which of them is grandest. Turning the very yardstick of value upside down, this scene shows Kenji's thought at its most direct.

Chūmon no ōi ryōriten jo

Even if we do not have all the rock sugar we want, we can eat the clean, clear wind and drink the lovely peach-colored light of morning.

The opening of the preface to the only collection of children's stories published in his lifetime. It speaks Kenji's view of the world: even without material plenty, one can receive beautiful things from nature.

I cannot tell you how much I hope that some few pieces of these little stories may, in the end, become clear, true food for you.

The close of the preface. The earnest wish of a writer offering his stories as nourishment for the reader's heart—“clear, true food.”

Ozbel and the Elephant

Now, Ozbel — he really is something.

An opening narrated by a cowherd. A remark poised between exasperation and admiration establishes, all at once, both the sheer scale of the exploiter and the view of the common people looking up at him from below.

“Oh, thank you. I really have been saved.” The white elephant smiled forlornly as he said it. Hey, [one character illegible], I've told you not to go into the river.

The white elephant's words after his companions rescue him, followed by the last line of the story. This enigmatic ending, in which the narrator suddenly calls out to someone, has stirred readers' imaginations — all the more because one character of the manuscript cannot be read.

Neko no jimusho

I half agree with the lion.

The last line, added by the narrator after the lion dissolves the cats' office, where bullying has run rampant. In the reservation “half” lies Kenji's complicated feeling, which will not let the story end as a simple tale of good rewarded and evil punished.

The Life of Guskō Budori

Guskō Budori was born in the great forest of Ihatov.

The opening of the tale. It begins the life story of Budori, born the son of a woodcutter in the forest of Ihatov, who lives through cold summers and famine and becomes an engineer who gives himself for the people.

And so the many fathers and mothers of Budori, who would otherwise have met the fate with which this story began, were able to spend that winter happily with many Budoris and Neris, with warm food and bright firewood.

The close of the tale. After Budori stays behind on the volcanic island and warms the climate at the cost of his life, it tells how people like his own family long ago were saved, bringing a story of self-sacrifice quietly to an end.

Night on the Galactic Railroad

But what, in the end, is true happiness?

A question at the very heart of the story, asked again and again during the night journey across the Milky Way. Miyazawa Kenji's lifelong search for what true happiness might be is distilled into this short question.

Campanella, it's just the two of us again, isn't it. Let's go on together, all the way, wherever it takes us.

Words Giovanni speaks to his friend near the end of their journey across the galaxy. Immediately after, Campanella's seat is found empty, and an eternal parting comes.

Please, the next time, use my body for the sake of everyone's true happiness.

The prayer of the Scorpion's Fire, as Campanella tells it. A wish that one's own life be used for the happiness of others names the very theme of the work.

Like that scorpion, if it were truly for everyone's happiness, I wouldn't mind my body being burned a hundred times over.

The resolve Giovanni speaks to Campanella after hearing the story of the scorpion's fire. The wish at the heart of the work: to seek “true happiness” through self-sacrifice.

“Now, class, this pale white thing that has been called a river, or the trail of spilled milk—do any of you know what it really is?”

The opening: the teacher's question in an afternoon lesson. With this question about the true nature of the Milky Way, Giovanni's journey through the galaxy quietly begins.

I'm not afraid anymore, not even of a great darkness like that. I'll go and search for everyone's true happiness, I promise. Let's go on together, all the way, as far as it takes us.

The vow Giovanni speaks before the dark hole of the Coalsack. Right after, Campanella vanishes, and these words become the guide by which Giovanni, left alone, will live.

My, now this is something! Why, this is a ticket that'll take you even to the true heaven. Not just heaven — it's a pass that lets you go anywhere you please. With this you could go as far as you like on an imperfect fantasy fourth-dimensional Milky Way railway like this one. You two are really something.

The words of the bird catcher on seeing Giovanni's ticket. Calling it a ticket “that'll take you even to the true heaven,” “a pass that lets you go anywhere you please,” he impresses on us how special Giovanni's journey is.

“That god of yours is a false god!” “Your god is a false god!” “No, he isn't.” “What sort of god is your god?” the young man asked with a smile. “I don't really know much about it. But not that kind — the one true God.” “The true God is, of course, only one.”

An argument between Giovanni and the sister and brother who are about to get off for heaven. The childish exchange, each calling the other's god “a false god,” leads into the work's fundamental question of what the true god is.

Gauche the Cellist

Gauche played the cello at the moving-picture theater in town. But he had a reputation for not being very good at it.

An opening that introduces a poor cellist, forever being scolded by his conductor. The nightly visits and practice sessions with animals quietly begin to change him.

Ah, cuckoo. I'm sorry about back then. I wasn't really angry at you.

The closing, on the night his concert succeeds. His apology to the bird he once drove away, after making it endure his practice, quietly shows how far Gauche has come.

Matasaburō of the Wind

Doddo-do, dodo-udo, dodo-udo, dodo-u Blow away the green walnuts too Blow away the sour quinces too Doddo-do, dodo-udo, dodo-udo, dodo-u

An opening song that turns the sound of the wind directly into words. It ushers in twelve strange days in September, during which children come to believe that the transfer student Takada Saburō is Matasaburō, a spirit of the wind.

“Oh, I get it. He's Matasaburō of the Wind!”

What Kasuke cries on seeing the mysterious new pupil who has appeared at the small school by the mountain stream. Among the children, the rumor spreads that the boy may be Matasaburō, the spirit of the wind.

The Nighthawk Star

The nighthawk was, in truth, a very ugly bird.

A children's story that opens by unhesitatingly calling a despised bird “ugly.” Contemplating the sin of eating in order to live, the nighthawk becomes a star.

And the Nighthawk Star went on burning. It went on burning forever and ever. It is burning still, even now.

The ending. The nighthawk, scorned as ugly, keeps climbing into the sky until it becomes a blue star. The line saying it is still burning leaves behind both salvation and sorrow.

Nametokoyama no kuma

Bear. I didn't kill you because I hate you. It's my trade, so I have to shoot you. I'd like to do some other work with no sin in it, but I've got no fields, the trees all belong to the government, and when I go down to the village nobody will deal with me. I've no choice but to be a hunter. If it's your ill luck to be born a bear, it's my ill luck to be in this trade. Hey. Next time, don't be born a bear.

The words Kojūrō speaks to a bear he has just shot, over its body. In the dialect of his region he talks of the fate that binds creatures who must kill in order to live, and Kenji's prayer for all life shows through.

Kenjū kōenrin

Ah, truly, who is wise and who is not, no one can tell. Only, the working of the Ten Powers is wondrous beyond all measure.

The words of the scholar who learns that the cedar grove Kenjū planted has survived as a playground for children. Recognizing the worth of what Kenjū, laughed at as a fool, had done, it calls into question the very yardstick by which we measure people's wisdom.

Be Not Defeated by the Rain

Not losing to the rain Not losing to the wind Not losing to the snow nor to summer's heat With a strong body Unfettered by desire Never letting anger show

A prayer-like poem found written in Kenji's notebook after his death. In plain, simple words it sings of an ideal way to live, and remains beloved and recited to this day.

Called a good-for-nothing by everyone Neither praised Nor a burden to anyone That is the kind of person I want to be

The closing of this poem, written down in his notebook. He wishes to be neither praised nor a trouble to anyone, only a “good-for-nothing,” as people call it.

Porāno no hiroba

That clear, transparent wind of Ihatovo; the blue sky that keeps a coolness in its depths even in summer; the city of Morīo, adorned with beautiful woods; the glittering waves of grass on its outskirts.

A passage in which the narrator, Kyūsto, fondly recalls the days he spent in the city of Morīo. The light and wind of Ihatovo, the land Kenji imagined as an ideal place, are called up vividly in a chain of limpid phrases.

Shicchū

It's no good, is it — it won't stop — it keeps welling up and welling up — I haven't slept since last night, the blood keeps coming, so — everything around is blue and deep and still — it seems I'll be dying soon. But what a lovely wind this is! The festival of Clear and Bright is near, and so, as if surging up and welling out of the blue sky, such a clean wind comes. Over the young maple buds and the downy blossoms it raises waves like autumn grasses, and the rush mat with its scorch marks is blue as well. Whether you are on your way back from a medical meeting or what, I don't know, but you wear a black frock coat and take such earnest care of me, so I have no complaint even if I die now. Though I am bleeding, that I am so easy and feel no pain — has half my soul left my body, I wonder? Only, because of the blood, I can't say so, and that is hard. From where you stand it must be a terrible sight, but what I can see is only the beautiful blue sky after all, and the wind, clear and transparent.

The whole of “Speaking with My Eyes,” one of the sickbed poems of Shicchū. What the narrator, coughing up blood as he looks up at the doctor, sees is only the blue sky and a clear wind — a limpid gaze in the face of death that moves the reader deeply.

Nōmin geijutsu gairon kōyō

Until the whole world is happy, there can be no happiness for the individual

A passage from the introduction. It directly expresses Kenji's refusal to separate personal happiness from the world as a whole, and his commitment to thinking about people's lives and art together.
