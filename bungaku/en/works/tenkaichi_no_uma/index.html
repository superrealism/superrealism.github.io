About this work

A children's story by Toyoshima Yoshio, published in Suzuki Miekichi's "Akai Tori" in 1924. Jinbei, a packhorse man of a mountain village, makes his living hauling timber to a town five ri away with the fine black horse he bought by selling the fields he inherited from his parents. One snowy evening a devil's child appears, having lost his magic when a dog bit off the tip of his tail, and begs to be lodged in the horse's belly until the end of February. A creature in trouble is to be given a bed, even a devil — Jinbei's open-handedness, and what becomes of a devil's child who grows too fat and sticks in a horse's throat, told in a light, swinging voice.

Summary

Jinbei, a packhorse man of a mountain village, dotes like a miser on the splendid black horse he bought with the money from the fields his parents left him. One winter day, on his way back from carrying timber to town, he is caught in the snow, and out from under the cliff beside the darkening highway springs something black about the size of a cat. A face neither human nor monkey, hooves like a goat's, a short tail — a devil's child. A week ago, it says, while it was playing with its fellows, a hunting dog bit off the tip of its tail, and with that it lost both the art of flying through the air and the art of changing shape, and has been shivering with hunger and cold ever since. If Jinbei will lend it the horse's belly for a lodging until the end of February, it says, it will make the horse ten times as strong in return. Jinbei opens the horse's mouth, and it leaps in at a bound. From the next day the black horse gallops up the slopes with mountains of timber on the cart, going the five ri three times a day, and becomes the talk of the district. As the end of February nears, the horse's belly begins to swell. At daybreak on the first of March the horse grinds its teeth and starts to thrash. Putting an ear to its mouth, Jinbei hears the devil's child: it has grown too fat on good food, is stuck in the throat, and cannot get out. Make the horse yawn a great yawn, it says — but no one on earth knows how to make a horse yawn.

Characters

  • Jinbei　A packhorse man of a mountain village. Easygoing, but he prizes his fine black horse above everything
  • The black horse　The glossy young horse Jinbei bought by selling his fields. Lodging the devil's child, it gains ten times, and later a hundred times, its strength
  • The devil's child　A little imp of the mountains who lost his magic when a hunting dog bit his tail. He rents the horse's belly for a lodging

The opening (original text)

1 In a mountain village in the country there lived a packhorse man named Jinbei. He was an easygoing soul who idled about as long as he had money and went to work when the money ran out. His work was to carry the timber that came down from the mountains to a town some five ri away. He would pile the round and squared logs, all fragrant with new wood, high on a sturdy cart, harness the black horse with the bells around its neck, and set off along the five ri of highway early in the morning — clop clop, jingle jingle — and be home again by evening.

The Finest Horse Under Heaven by Toyoshima Yoshio (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

From that time on, Jinbei's black horse had the strength of a hundred men — a hundred horsepower — and did prodigious work. People were astonished. Jinbei alone was perfectly cool about it, and went up and down the highway always humming a riddling little song. Devil or no devil, all the same, if he's in trouble, give him a bed. He swallowed down a devil's child and brought it up again with a yawn — the finest black horse under heaven. Get up there, get up there.
