About this work

A novel Higuchi Ichiyō serialized in the Kaishin Shimbun from April 1892. A greedy draper ruins the main house, takes its fortune, and breaks the engagement between two children. It draws the doomed love of a young man reduced to pulling a rickshaw and the daughter of a great house, who meet again on a night of snow and are driven toward a love suicide. One of the few works Ichiyō serialized in a newspaper, an early piece writing an unlucky love within a conventional plot.

Summary

Nitta Unpei, a red-faced, greedy kimono merchant in Uchi-Kanda's Renjaku-chō, envied the prosperity of the Matsuzawa family, fellow merchants of the senior branch. Using as pretext a debt owed to him by the Matsuzawa family, Unpei brings a lawsuit, and in league with his clerk Kanzō, seizes the Matsuzawa house, storehouse, and even their shop name for himself. The Matsuzawa family falls into poverty, and their only son Yoshinosuke — once betrothed to Unpei's daughter Otaka — is reduced to pulling a rickshaw to support his ailing parents. Sunk in resentment, Yoshinosuke and Otaka, a caged bird unable even to send a letter, drift apart, each unable to fathom the other's heart. One snowy night, on her way home from a music gathering, Otaka recognizes the rickshaw puller as Yoshinosuke, hires him, and after a roundabout route, lures him into a restaurant where the two are reunited at last. But Yoshinosuke's father Giemon coldly turns Otaka away, saying he cannot let his son marry the daughter of that inhuman man Unpei. Torn apart and with nowhere left to turn, the two resolve to die together at their ancestral grave, to prove their hearts through death. But Kanzō, who has followed them, bursts in, and beneath a flashing blade, one branch of the twin-rooted tree scatters in vain — Yoshinosuke dies, while Otaka, unable to die, is shut away in a locked room for seven long years. Feigning repentance to lower her captors' guard, Otaka at last slips away one dawn, and follows Yoshinosuke in death atop the mandarin-duck grave.

Characters

  • Otaka　The only daughter of the kimono merchant Nitta Unpei. Betrothed to Matsuzawa Yoshinosuke, she keeps faith with him even after their engagement is broken, and is driven at last to a suicide pact
  • Yoshinosuke　The only son of the Matsuzawa family. Stripped of his house by Unpei's scheme, he is reduced to pulling a rickshaw to support his ailing parents, and dies in the attempted suicide pact with Otaka
  • Nitta Unpei　Otaka's father. A greedy kimono merchant who, envying the senior branch of the Matsuzawa family, seizes their fortune through a scheming lawsuit and breaks off the betrothal
  • Matsuzawa Giemon　Yoshinosuke's father. Ruined and impoverished after Unpei seizes his house, he stubbornly refuses to accept Otaka as a daughter-in-law
  • Kanzō　Unpei's clerk. Aids Unpei in his scheme, and bursts in on the two lovers' suicide pact

The opening (original text)

In a world as fleeting as Zhuangzi's dream of the butterfly, duty and sincerity are nothing but a nuisance — so thinks the man who, right up to the moment of waking, keeps drawing tight the scales of his own greed, upon which the single word 'gold' outweighs all else, so that even the priceless treasure of a child is forgotten in the chase for petty gain and greater loss. The old warning of the overturned cart ahead means nothing to the hand that grips his own shafts and will not let go, in a hawk-and-bear creed where reason always runs crooked — in a certain kimono shop in Uchi-Kanda's Renjaku-chō, a shop noisier not with the chatter of friends but with the constant tread of customers.

Wakarejimo by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Whose is this unchanging bond? The wind sighs through the thousand-year pines, and the bloodstained ground has faded again to green grass, withered now beneath a coating of frost; a single moon shines down in sorrow upon it all — what grudge is there left to mourn, here, above the mandarin-duck grave?

Wakarejimo by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)
