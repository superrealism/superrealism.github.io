What is Kyōka (Comic Waka)?

Category
Form / Genre

A short poem that borrows the form of waka (5-7-5-7-7) for comedy, satire, and wordplay. It had long been composed as a form of playful verse, but it exploded in popularity in Edo during the Tenmei era (1781–1789) of the late Edo period, building the golden age of kyōka history. At its center stood Ōta Nanpo (Shokusanjin), a shogunal retainer who nonetheless wrote vigorously under the pen name Yomo no Akara, and he is counted, together with Karagoromo Kisshū and Akera Kankō, among the three great masters of Tenmei kyōka. In 1783, Nanpo and Akera Kankō compiled the "Manzai Kyōkashū," a collection tailored, in both title and format, to closely mimic the imperial anthology "Senzai Wakashū," gathering the comic verses of more than two hundred poets and sorting them into the four seasons, love, and miscellaneous. Its charm lies in deliberately substituting vulgar content for the dignity of the old poetic tradition, and, alongside senryū, which spread among the common people around the same time, it represents the pinnacle of Edo wordplay. Many kyōka poets doubled as kibyōshi authors or ukiyo-e artists, and it was a presence coloring Tenmei-era Edo culture itself. It declined as a literary art after the Meiji era, but its satirical role for commenting on current affairs was carried on into the senryū columns of newspaper and magazine reader submissions.
