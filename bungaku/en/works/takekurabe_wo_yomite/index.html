About this work

The essay in which Takayama Chogyū praised Higuchi Ichiyō's Growing Up without reserve, written in 1896. Chogyū was among the first to see the uncommon gift of a writer not yet widely known, and calls Growing Up her masterpiece. He describes the work — set in the downtown quarter near the Yoshiwara, drawing the faint first love of Midori and the other children and the frailty of days passing into adulthood — as a fine thing that pierces the subtlety of the human heart within plain material, and goes so far as to call its pathos a confession of us all. A piece of Meiji criticism that led the reappraisal of Ichiyō.

Summary

On his way down from the Hongō plateau toward Sashigaya, Chogyū had once passed through a town called Maruyama Shinmachi, and on hearing that Ichiyō lived in just such a town, he found himself, as he went on to read "Nigorie," "Jūsan'ya," and "Wakaremichi" one after another, astonished at what an extraordinary writer she was. Reading "Takekurabe" only confirms his conviction of her genius all the more. Chogyū judges this single piece — which draws upon the plainest of material to probe the subtle depths of the human heart — a greater masterpiece even than "Jūsan'ya." Shōta, Sangorō, and Shin'nyo, he notes, are each drawn true to their own characters, but most striking of all is Midori. So Chogyū argues, and he is deeply moved by the pathos of Midori — a girl at just that age when she envies her elder sister's glory and delights in hearing tales of the pleasure quarter amid the bustle of the five blocks near Yoshiwara — as she walks, step by step, toward an inescapable fate. A young girl's first love, pure as a jewel, fading away as fragile as dew — "Takekurabe: ah, how dear, how wistfully remembered, is this past of ours" — Chogyū closes by asking whether this work ought not to be read as a "confession" that calls back to life our own lost days of youth. An essay in which the young Chogyū's keen eye for appreciation shines, and a pioneering piece of criticism in the reevaluation of Ichiyō.

The opening (original text)

Once, going down from the Hongō plateau toward Sashigaya, I passed through a place called Maruyama Shinmachi, and when three different people told me that Ichiyō lived in just such a town, I nodded, only with difficulty believing it. Then, as I went on reading "Nigorie," reading "Jūsan'ya," reading "Wakaremichi," I found myself recalling that "Maruyama Shinmachi" once again, and was astonished to find Ichiyō no ordinary writer. At this time there was a dispute, in the pages of "Mezamashigusa," between Ōgai and certain others, over the poet and her personal history, but I paid it no heed. I have confirmed Ichiyō's extraordinary genius all the more, on reading "Takekurabe."

Ichiyō joshi no 'Takekurabe' o yomite by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

A young girl's first love, pure as a jewel, fades away, alas, as fragile as dew — and how pitiable is Midori's fate, clinging to a fleeting dream, never knowing that a fearful, wretched future waits for her with its serpent's mouth already open. And yet by birth she is no different from the rest of us. Friends of thirteen or fourteen might well be taken for a band of innocent, heavenly children, but as the years pass and the heart matures, they mingle at last with the bottomless waters of that murky inlet, and not even the shadow of their original, dewdrop purity remains. Takekurabe — ah, how dear, how wistfully remembered, is this past of ours. Ought not this piece of Ichiyō's be read, in time, as a confession of our own? This is what I feel on reading "Takekurabe." I would not presume to call it criticism.

Ichiyō joshi no 'Takekurabe' o yomite by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)
