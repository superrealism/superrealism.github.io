About this work

Homoi, a young rabbit who has saved a lark's chick, is given a jewel called the fire of the shell. But the stone is a mirror that shows exactly the state of its owner's heart — a children's story by Miyazawa Kenji about pride, corruption and loss. The six days it takes for the beautiful jewel to cloud and finally shatter make one of the sternest and least forgettable lessons in his work.

Summary

On a morning when the fields were full of good smells, the young hare Homoi found a baby skylark fallen into the stream and pulled it out, nearly drowning himself. The next day the king of the skylarks came to thank him and gave Homoi a jewel called the Shell Fire. The stone, which shines like burning fire, is said to shine beautifully as long as its owner is upright, and to cloud when his heart is muddied. His father warns him: "It may cloud before long; if it does, take heed." But with the jewel in his hands Homoi changed before their eyes. He took it as his due that the beasts of the wood should come to flatter him, was talked round by the fox into helping raid a skylark's nest, punished a mole and bullied a family of squirrels. Each time the jewel grew dull and ran with cracks, and each morning, strangely, it came back as it was. But on the sixth morning the Shell Fire, clouded black at last, cracked sharply in two and burst with a snapping noise and scattered like smoke. The powder got into Homoi's eyes, and he lost his sight. The broken pieces presently fitted together again, became the Shell Fire once more, and flew away out of the window. The owl went off jeering. The mother went on weeping. And his father, who had been standing with folded arms, tapped Homoi on the back and said: "Don't cry. This sort of thing is everywhere. You who have understood it so well are the most fortunate of all."

Characters

  • Homoi　The young hare who saves the baby skylark and is given the Shell Fire
  • Homoi's father and mother　Who are anxious at the change in their son
  • The king of the skylarks　Who brings the Shell Fire in thanks
  • The fox　Who leads Homoi astray

The opening (original text)

Now the hares are all in short brown coats. The grass of the field glitters, and the birches here and there have put out white flowers. Truly the field is full of good smells. The young hare Homoi said, dancing about for joy, "Ah, what a good smell. Good, good — the lilies of the valley are simply crisp."

Kai no hi by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Don't cry. This sort of thing is everywhere. You who have understood it so well are the most fortunate of all. Your eyes will surely get well again. Your father will make them well. There. Don't cry." Outside the window the mist had cleared and the leaves of the lilies of the valley were glittering, and the harebells rang the morning bell loudly: "Kan, kan, kankaeko, kanko kanko kan."
