About this work

Hara Tamiki's account of his own experience of the bomb, and a landmark of atomic-bomb literature, published in Mita Bungaku in 1947. The narrator, who laid yellow summer flowers on his dead wife's grave two days before the bomb fell, is struck by the flash and the blast in Hiroshima on the morning of 6 August and survives among the collapsed city and the heaped dead. It does not lose its composure before the horror; the still prose of a clear-eyed observer looking at hell makes the inhumanity of the bomb the sharper.

Summary

Before the first Bon after his wife's death, on the fifteenth of August, the narrator buys yellow summer flowers in town and lays them on the grave where his parents and his wife lie. Two days later, on the morning of the sixth of August, he is in the privy and so escapes with his life; struck by a blow overhead and a darkness before his eyes, he crawls out of the wrecked house in nothing but his underwear. He finds his sister and his brother, and on the way to the riverbank away from the fire he sees countless wounded people, burned and writhing and begging for water, and the transformed shapes of the dead. Unable to hold that sight in human words, he cannot help writing it down in broken fragments of katakana. Later, after the party moves out to the village of Yahata in the country, one after another the burned and the exposed — his nephew among them — lose their hair and grow weaker. The figure of N, a relative who wanders the relief stations and the burned ground for three days and three nights looking for his wife, is set beside them, and the hell the atomic bomb brought is drawn in exact detail in a restrained hand.

Characters

  • I　The narrator. He lays flowers on his dead wife's grave just before the bomb and survives the sixth of August because he is in the privy. He watches and records the horror with a clear eye
  • My sister　Who comes to pull the narrator out of the wrecked house
  • My brother　Who holds the family together and moves them out to the village of Yahata
  • My nephew　A middle-school boy mobilized for the clearing of buildings, who comes home after the bombing and grows weaker as his hair falls out
  • N　A relative who wanders the burnt ground and the relief stations for three days and three nights looking for his wife

The opening (original text)

I thought I would go into town, buy flowers, and visit my wife's grave. In my pocket was a bundle of incense sticks I had taken from the household altar. The fifteenth of August would be her first Bon, but whether this home town of mine would still be safe by then was doubtful. It happened to be one of the days when the power was cut off; but from the morning I saw no one else walking through the town with flowers. I do not know what the flower is called, but it had small yellow petals with a charming country look about it, and it seemed very much a flower of summer. I poured water over the gravestone standing exposed to the burning sky, divided the flowers in two and set them in the vases on either side; and the face of the grave somehow looked fresher for it, and I gazed a while at the flowers and the stone. Beneath this stone lay not only my wife but the bones of my father and mother. I lit the incense I had brought, made a silent bow, and drank water at the well beside it. Then I went home by way of Nigitsu Park; and that day and the next, my pocket still held the smell of the incense. It was the day after that when the atomic bomb came.

The ending (original text)

N went round to relief station after relief station, peering into the faces of the badly wounded. Every face was misery itself, but none of them was his wife's. And after spending three days and three nights looking at corpses and burned patients until he was sick of it, N went at the end once more to the burnt ruins of the girls' school where his wife had worked.
