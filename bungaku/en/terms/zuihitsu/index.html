What is Zuihitsu (Essays)?

Category
Form / Genre

Prose that sets down observations, impressions, and reflections in a free form, following wherever the writer's pen leads. "The Pillow Book," "Hōjōki," and "Tsurezuregusa" are counted as the three great classical essay collections, and Kamo no Chōmei's "Hōjōki," recording the impermanence of a chaotic age in lucid mixed Sino-Japanese prose, is their representative work. In the modern era, Masaoka Shiki, who had championed shasei (sketching from life), opened a new path for the essay in "Byōshō Rokushaku" ("A Six-Foot Sickbed") and "Bokujū Itteki" ("A Drop of Ink"), plainly recording his sickbed days, and Terada Torahiko, a physicist who studied under Natsume Sōseki, opened his own distinctive terrain in essays such as "Donguri" ("Acorns") and "Tensai to Kokubō" ("Natural Disasters and National Defense"), combining a scientist's observant eye with a gentle poetic sensitivity. Many other writers, too, left works with a charm distinct from their fiction: Nagai Kafū's "Hiyorigeta" ("Fair-Weather Clogs"), walking the streets of Tokyo; Tanizaki Jun'ichirō's "In'ei Raisan" ("In Praise of Shadows"), discussing an aesthetics of shadow; Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's aphoristic "Shuju no Kotoba" ("Words of a Dwarf"); and a series of essays on food by Kitaōji Rosanjin. What gives the essay its particular savor is the way the writer's own character and thought come directly into view, unmediated by fiction.
