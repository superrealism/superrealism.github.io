About this work

In the summer of 1935, present at two small eruptions of Mount Asama while summering at Karuizawa, Terada Torahiko recorded them minutely with a physicist's eye, from the rate at which the smoke column rose to the structure of the falling ash. The line often quoted since — that it is easy to fear a thing too little or too much, but very hard to fear it rightly — is placed in this piece. The ending, in which he imagines the literature of an age that classifies eruptions by type and grade, is not to be forgotten.

Summary

On the morning of the fourth of August in the tenth year of Shōwa, no sooner had he lit a cigarette on the terrace of the Green Hotel at Sengataki in Karuizawa than a shattering report rang out. It was neither a cannon nor a gas explosion but the sound of a rock face being broken up with dynamite. Going up to the roof and looking toward Asama, the summit was hidden in cloud; but before long a column of smoke shaped like flowering kale came boiling up above the cap of cloud and rose straight at fifty or sixty meters a second — a speed equal to the highest wind of a typhoon. The skin of the column was carved into fine unevennesses, showing the violence of the disturbance within it. As it rose it whitened, and rings of vortex piled up like the caps of mushrooms stacked one on another. ... The ashfall began on the way down the hill, and by the time he reached the Hoshino hot spring the ash lay thick enough to change the color of the ground. On the surface of a pond ripples like those of light rain appeared and vanished, and the knowledge that ash was falling colored a familiar landscape with a desolate air. Looking at the fallen ash under a binocular microscope, at the core of each grain was a many-sided fragment of rock, with fine ash particles clinging to its surface like rime. Why the two should stick together instead of striking and rebounding — that is a question for the future. At Kutsukake station on the way home, seeing a station man shake his head quietly at a student who assured him that an eruption was nothing at all, Torahiko thinks how hard it is to "fear rightly." There was another eruption on the seventeenth of August, but even in the same Karuizawa the sound was heard quite differently. In time, when the study of volcanoes has advanced, a day will come when eruptions are classified by type and grade. And then, asks Torahiko, what will "literature" become?

The opening (original text)

On the morning of the fourth of August in the tenth year of Shōwa, having eaten breakfast in the third-floor dining room of the Green Hotel at Sengataki in Karuizawa in Shinshū, and having gone out onto that terrace with its fine view meaning to rest at my leisure, I had no sooner lit a cigarette than I heard a shattering report. A report beating out an irregular rhythm — something like "boom, boom-boom, bo-oom" — finished in a mere two or three seconds, and after it a "gō-ō" like the echo of thunder went on for another two or three seconds and died away, weakening, toward the foot of the hills to the south.

Shōbakuhatsu niken by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Volcanic eruptions, however, may be expected, once the study of volcanoes has advanced a little further, to be classified by type and grade, so that one will be able to record that today's was type A, third grade, and yesterday's type B, fifth grade. It is not impossible that an age will come when the newspapers report a double suicide of type S, number thirty-six, and a murder of type P, number two hundred and forty-seven; but it is hard to imagine what "literature" would be in such an age. At least it seems to me that the kind of literature whose condition of existence is the coarseness of head that adorns the "creative writing" pages of the magazines of our day may perhaps disappear.

Shōbakuhatsu niken by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

It seemed to me that while it is easy to fear something too little or too much, to fear it rightly is very difficult indeed.

A passage from an essay on experiencing small eruptions of Mount Asama. These words on the difficulty of “fearing rightly” — neither too much nor too little — are quoted again and again in times of disaster.
