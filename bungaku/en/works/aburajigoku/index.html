About this work

The work Saitō Ryokuu published in the Kokkai Shimbun in 1891, establishing his position as a novelist. Megata Sadanoshin, a straitlaced and well-regarded student of law, has his head turned by a suggestive word from Kouta, a geisha of Yanagibashi, and works himself deeper into the fantasy of an unreturned love. He ruins his studies and himself, and at the end of disappointment and jealousy loses his reason and is destroyed. A tragicomedy of an innocent man played with by the hardened world of the pleasure quarters, drawn through in Ryokuu's own ironic and humorous style.

Summary

Megata Teinoshin is a student of law with the reputation of a diligent and well-conducted young man. At a friendly gathering he meets Kouta, a geisha of Yanagibashi, and is quite carried away by one suggestive word of hers. From then on Teinoshin reads this and that into everything Kouta says, decides on his own that she is fond of him, and lets his fancies swell without end. But to Kouta, who lives in the sharp world of the pleasure quarters, the innocence of a green student is only something to be played with. Sinking deeper into a one-sided love that only grows, Teinoshin ruins his studies and himself, and comes down in the world, driven this way and that by money and desire. Cornered by the sense of betrayal and by jealousy, he loses his reason at last. Holding a grudge with nowhere to go, he sets a pan of oil boiling on the charcoal and throws Kouta's photograph into it, and the story closes with him out of his mind. Ryokuu drew, in a detached and ironic style, the ruin of a green young man played with by a woman full of hints.

Characters

  • Megata Teinoshin　A law student of solid reputation. The hero, who falls in love at first sight with Kouta, a geisha of Yanagibashi, feeds an unrequited fancy until he loses his reason, and is ruined
  • Kouta　A geisha of Yanagibashi. A woman of the sharp world of the quarters, who leads the green Teinoshin on with one suggestive word

The opening (original text)

Having had needlessly put into his head the notion that a true man ought to soar — so that hoes and spades came to look mean to him, and he left the parents who could barely have kept the water in their cups, and came up to Tokyo to be polished, though he was no jewel — he must at first have had schemes enough to remake the five continents that did not suit him. But as one year became two and two became three and he grew used to it, the rotten wind of the capital, blown up out of money, rubbed away by degrees at his sunburned country profile; and when the sign "Books taken in pawn" had come to catch his eye, he would submit to the ceremonious letters from home and, holding that decision is the truest wisdom, strip the lining out of the cotton coat he wore to make up what was wanting for that night's pot — no new thing, but a sad business. The only one of whom people spoke with admiration was that fellow — praised without the least connection, by a certain gentleman from his own province whose name it was awkward even to hear, whose long service had raised his rank — the fellow whose world was the six-mat room at the end on the left at the top of the stairs of the Akimoto lodging house in Tatsuoka-chō, Hongō, twenty-one years old this year and at the height of his training, whom his landlord praised again and again for his early rising: a man named Megata Teinoshin.

The ending (original text)

From the side of the doubt he had hoped was a lie, there was nothing to wonder at in Kouta's leaving her trade, since it turned out to be true; but from the side of the denial he had prayed was true, since it turned out to be altogether a lie, a thing they had never even promised each other seemed to him a change of heart, so that Teinoshin was almost out of his mind, and the more anger showed on the outside the more it deepened his gloom within. That day Teinoshin lay with the quilt over his head, saying he was ill and not going even to the school; but about lamplighting time he got up abruptly and went out, and presently came back with something in a small iron pan, and got into bed again. About what he took to be one in the morning he slowly raised his head, and as if he had forgotten something he suddenly heaped charcoal like a mountain into the brazier and, taking up something like a magazine that lay on the desk, fanned it; and when he had waited for the fire to burn up briskly he set the pan on it — and what was in the pan was oil. The oil grew hot by degrees, and from the time it began to seethe with bubbles great and small, Teinoshin's look grew of itself terrible; and what he threw in as though beating it down was Kouta's photograph, which spun round and sank and came up again at once. In twelve or thirteen minutes the photograph was scorched and blistered, and the eyes and the mouth he had looked at with such joy until yesterday melted away before him; and as though something were still left in the pan, Teinoshin set his hands on his knees and stared fixedly without a blink, and did not know that the night had ended. When the fire weakened at last and it turned at last to ash, Teinoshin's head fell forward, and in the end he lay face down. The landlady of the Akimoto, an early riser, wondered at the smoke still left in the room, and at the sound of her sliding open the paper door of Teinoshin's room to see what the smell was, Teinoshin started awake, and crying "Ah, is it Kouta?" leapt up and came running unsteadily with his feet hardly touching the mats; and at the same moment as the landlady's cry as she stepped back, he fell there in a heap. Pitiable, from then on he kept his bed, his cheeks fell in and his eyes sank, and night and day he raved: that Kouta, that Kouta.
