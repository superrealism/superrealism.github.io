About this work

The collection of short pieces Terada Torahiko went on writing for more than ten years at the head of the haiku magazine Shibugaki. More than two hundred fragments, from a few lines to a page, set down as the pen pleases: observation of people in a tram, failures in the laboratory, records of dreams, film criticism, and the relation of science to art. Neither diary nor essay, this is Terada's freest prose.

Summary

"From around the ninth year of Taishō, on the very first page of Shibugaki, the haiku magazine edited by my friend Matsune Tōyōjō, I have, from time to time, published short, impromptu jottings under the title 'Untitled'" — so Terada explains in his own preface, adding that these were pieces dashed off in haste whenever, pressed by a deadline, he sat down unprepared at his desk and cast about for something, anything, to write about. He modestly describes them as intended less for the magazine's readers than for Tōyōjō and Komiya Toyotaka to read — little more, he says, than a collection of letters, or fragments of a diary. And yet these short pieces, setting out from the smallest matters close at hand, arrive, before one quite realizes it, at something unexpectedly universal. A habit noticed in someone on a streetcar, the movements of a cat, a fire, an earthquake, the phonograph and the radio, the moving pictures, a failed experiment, a conversation with a student, a scene glimpsed in a dream. In one fragment he takes the sparks of a sparkler and discusses the physics of branching, tree-like patterns; in another he reads the structure of linked-verse haikai through a scientist's eye. Though he calls the whole "a collection of letters, or something like fragments of a diary," he adds that, viewed objectively as a whole, there emerges something like a reflection of the changing face of the age, seen through the lens of a single human being — and this, he writes, is his reason for publishing them. Near the very end of the book comes a memory from childhood — of a sideshow tent's "mountain man who eats glass" — that suddenly resurfaced out of the darkness of forgetting more than thirty years later, as he lay ill in bed with a stomach ulcer; and over that pitiable mountain man, who seems to have eaten glass to earn his living and so brought about his own end, Terada superimposes the figures of the gentlemen of the city.

The opening (original text)

Author's Preface From around the ninth year of Taishō, on the very first page of Shibugaki, the haiku magazine edited by my friend Matsune Tōyōjō, I have, from time to time, published short, impromptu jottings under the title "Untitled." Partway through, Komiya Toyotaka joined in as well, and around the fourteenth or fifteenth year of Taishō, it was Toyotaka who took charge of this column almost entirely.

Kaki no tane by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This memory of a strange sideshow seen in childhood suddenly rose up, out of the darkness of forgetting, more than thirty years later, when I myself lay ill in bed with a stomach ulcer. That pitiable mountain man must surely, within a year or two of that time, have contracted an ulcer of his own digestive organs and met a wretched end. He had, in a manner of speaking, eaten glass to earn his living, and so brought about his own end, as though by his own hand. The case of the gentleman of the city may, perhaps, bear some faint resemblance to this as well.

Kaki no tane by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)
