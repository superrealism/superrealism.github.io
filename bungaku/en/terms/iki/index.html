What is Iki?

Category
Other

An aesthetic sensibility that grew up in the townsman culture of Edo, above all in its pleasure quarters. It describes something polished, spirited, and alluring — the opposite of "yabo" (uncouthness). The philosopher Kuki Shūzō analyzed this Japanese term with the methods of European phenomenology in "'Iki' no Kōzō" ("The Structure of 'Iki,'" 1930), published that year in the journal "Shisō" and in book form by Iwanami Shoten. According to Kuki, "iki" consists of three elements: "bitai" (coquetry) directed toward the opposite sex at its core, together with "ikuji" (pride, or stubborn resolve) and "akirame" (resignation, an indifference to how a love affair turns out). He saw the distinctiveness of this aesthetic sensibility in the way the pride sustained by bushidō and Buddhist resignation dissolve together into allure. Whereas the Kansai region's "sui" carries a rich, warm humanity, Edo's "iki" is considered a leaner, more spirited beauty. Kuki discussed "iki" as an experience of meaning unique to the Japanese people, one that resists translation. It is a key to reading Edo culture — kabuki, ukiyo-e, and kouta songs — and it also runs through the sensibility of modern writers such as Nagai Kafū, who pursued the vanishing atmosphere of old Edo.
