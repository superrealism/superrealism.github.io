About this work

An early short detective story by Edogawa Ranpo and the first appearance of his great detective Akechi Kogorō, published in the magazine Shin Seinen in January 1925. The wife of a secondhand bookseller is strangled in what should have been a sealed room, every exit watched; Akechi, an amateur, reasons his way brilliantly to the answer. The story that opened Ranpo's period of orthodox detective fiction.

Summary

Out of work, "I" spends his days at the Hakubaiken, a café he frequents, watching the beautiful wife of the secondhand bookshop across the street. On a sultry evening he goes with Akechi Kogorō — an eccentric fond of detective stories — to look in at the shop, which seems oddly quiet, and the two find the wife strangled in the back room. The police establish that the shop's entrances were watched all that evening by the neighbours, and no one can imagine how the murderer got in or out. Going carefully over the testimony of "I" and of everyone else who had been watching the street, Akechi works out that each watcher had a moment when their view was blocked, and narrows the field. In time he shows that the keeper of the noodle shop in the same tenement and the murdered woman had a secret relationship rooted in the tastes they shared, and that the killing came of its going too far. As if in confirmation, that day's evening paper carried a small item: the noodle-shop keeper had given himself up.

Characters

  • I　A young man out of work. The narrator of the case and Akechi Kogorō's friend
  • Akechi Kogorō　An amateur detective fond of detective stories. The great detective of the story, appearing here for the first time
  • The bookshop keeper's wife　The victim, found strangled
  • The noodle-shop keeper　The murderer, who lives in the same tenement

The opening (original text)

It was one sultry evening in early September. I was sipping iced coffee at the Hakubaiken, a café I frequented halfway along the main street of D. Hill. I had just left school then and had as yet no profession to speak of; my days went in lounging about my lodgings with a book, or, when I tired of that, in going out for an aimless walk and making the round of the cheaper cafés. The Hakubaiken was near my lodgings and stood where I had to pass it whichever way I walked, so it was the one I went into most often. I have a bad habit of settling in once I am inside a café. Having little appetite by nature — and, it must be said, little in my purse — I would order no dish at all but call for a second and a third cup of cheap coffee and sit on for an hour or two. Not that I had any interest in the waitresses, or teased them. It was simply livelier there than at my lodgings, and more comfortable. That evening too, as usual, I took ten minutes over a single glass of iced coffee at my usual table facing the street, and gazed vacantly out of the window.

The ending (original text)

Just then the woman from the tobacconist's downstairs brought up the evening paper. Akechi took it and looked at the local page, and presently he sighed softly and said, "Ah — he could not hold out after all, and has given himself up. A strange coincidence, that we should meet with this news at the very moment we were speaking of it." I looked where he pointed. There, under a small heading, ten lines or so recorded that the keeper of the noodle shop had surrendered himself.
