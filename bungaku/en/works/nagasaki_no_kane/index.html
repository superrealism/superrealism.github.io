About this work

The record in which Nagai Takashi, a radiologist exposed to the bomb at Nagasaki Medical College on 9 August 1945, set down the horror of that day and the relief work with a physician's eye. Publication was long forbidden under the occupation, and when the book finally appeared in 1949 it caused an enormous response, and a popular song of the same title followed. The prayer at the close, crying that war must stop, still strikes the reader.

Summary

The sun of the ninth of August in the twentieth year of Shōwa came up over Mount Konpira as commonly as ever, and beautiful Urakami greeted its last morning — so the book begins. The Nagasaki Medical College began its lectures at eight as usual, and staff and students alike wore air-defense clothes with first-aid materials at their belts, ready for what might come. The alert sounded and was lifted; and then, at two minutes past eleven, the flash ran. Collapsed buildings, flying glass, colleagues with their heads split open and covered in blood. Bleeding from a severed right temporal artery, Nagai organized the relief party and carried patients out of the burning hospital. Some days later he came at last to the ruins of his own house and found, near where the kitchen had been, the bones of his wife Midori. Only the chain of her rosary remained beside them. After that Nagai led his relief party into Mitsuyama and treated the survivors who had fled to the mountain villages. He observed and recorded the symptoms of the atomic bomb sickness — the crash of the white cells, the falling hair, the purpura, the diarrhea — in minute detail. From the position of a physician who was also a patient, a victim who was also a scientist, he went on looking at what the atomic bomb had brought. In time he too collapsed, and the progress of his leukemia became plain. At the end of the book the bell dug out of the ruined Urakami cathedral is rung. "Kaan, kaan, kaan" — the bell that had been forbidden to ring ever since the war began. Men, do not plan war. The people of Urakami, weeping in the atomic field, cry out to the world: may it be granted that this Urakami be the last atomic field in the world.

Characters

  • Nagai Takashi　A radiologist of the Nagasaki Medical College, who is exposed to the bomb and takes up the relief work
  • Midori　Nagai's wife, killed by the atomic bomb
  • Makoto and Kayano　Nagai's children

The opening (original text)

Just before The sun of the ninth of August in the twentieth year of Shōwa came up over Mount Konpira as commonly as ever, and beautiful Urakami greeted its last morning. The chimneys of the various munitions works that filled the flat ground along the river were breathing white smoke; the tiled roofs of the shops that lined the highway ran on like purple waves; the houses on the hills were sending up the smoke of breakfast, the sign of families gathered together; and on the terraced fields along the mountainside the dew was shining on the well-grown potato plants.

Nagasaki no kane by Nagai Takashi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Men, do not plan war. Because there is such a thing as the atomic bomb, war can only be an act of suicide for the human race. The people of Urakami, weeping in the atomic field, cry out to the world. Stop the war. Only make your agreements with one another according to the law of love. The people of Urakami lie down in the ashes and pray to God: may it be granted that this Urakami be the last atomic field in the world. The bell is ringing still.

Nagasaki no kane by Nagai Takashi (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Shining, the autumn clouds vanish high away.

The haiku Nagai Takashi spoke as his last words when, during relief work after the atomic bombing, he fell gravely ill and prepared for death. In the autumn clouds vanishing high away as they shine, he sees his own life.
