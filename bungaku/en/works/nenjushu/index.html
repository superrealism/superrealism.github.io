About this work

A collection of essays in which Saitō Mokichi, who received news of his father's death while studying in Europe, strung together like the beads of a rosary his memories of boyhood in his native Kanakame in Yamagata. Yasokichi, a childhood friend who drowned, his father, the villagers — fragmentary recollections come back with a plaintive note. A central essay collection in which the lyricism of Mokichi the tanka poet shows through.

Summary

In the summer of the twelfth year of Taishō, while Saitō Mokichi was living as a student in Munich, word reached him that his father, back in his native village of Kanagame in Yamagata, had collapsed while working in the fields and died. Amid the loneliness of losing his father, memories of his boyhood rose up in Mokichi's mind one after another, tinged with a certain plaintive sorrow. This book is what he set down, each memory like a single bead on a string of prayer beads. Yasokichi, a childhood friend and grandson of his father's revered teacher, who drowned in a pool at the edge of the village in the summer of the twenty-fifth year of Meiji. His father, stern yet fond of travel. The natural scenery and yearly observances of Kanagame village, and the meticulous travel diary his father left behind — with a poet's clear lyricism and eye for observation, Mokichi tenderly sets down the lost scenes of his childhood and his longing for his departed father, in this representative essay collection.

The opening (original text)

I had given up my classroom in Vienna and, shouldering my pack, set out once more for Munich, the capital of Bavaria, which I was again making for. It was while I was still beset with all manner of hardships there that my father, in my native village of Kanagame in Yamagata Prefecture, passed away. He had been pulling weeds in the fields under the blazing midsummer sun, when he came down with a fever, and in the end he died. This was at the end of July in the twelfth year of Taishō, about a month before the great earthquake struck the Kantō region of Japan. 　I felt a terrible loneliness on learning of my father's death. And so it became my habit that, lying on my back on the bed even in the daytime, memories of my father from my boyhood years would rise up, one after another, into my consciousness, tinged with a certain plaintive sorrow. There were times when I felt I wanted to set them down in writing, and the tale of "Yasokichi" recorded here is one such memory concerning my father. Might not memories such as these be like so many single beads, strung one upon another, on a string of prayer beads?

The ending (original text)

By that time my father, too, was already sixty-four or sixty-five, and from around the age of forty his back had begun to stoop, so that when he set out on his journeys to the western provinces, he would walk with a board wrapped in paper pressed against his belly. He used to say that doing so straightened his back a little, and so I imagine that even his pilgrimage to Kōya must have been a hard one for him. And when I think that my father, too, must have eaten manjū like the ones we ate, sitting forlornly in their broth, I am filled with a strange, wondering feeling. Let this stand as the afterword to Nenjushū.
