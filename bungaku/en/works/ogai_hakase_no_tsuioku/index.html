About this work

Uchida Roan, who came and went freely at his house, draws the young Mori Ōgai — army surgeon and great writer — as he really was. It runs from a first call in Ōgai's unknown days, when Roan was all but turned away at the door, through their friendship, the days at the Sendasanbō house spent talking of poetry and painting until deep in the night, the energy that needed only four hours of sleep and the habit of vast reading, to their last conversation, in which Ōgai promised to write the inscription for Roan's gravestone. Told throughout in anecdotes that could only be Ōgai's.

Summary

Uchida Roan recalls the character of Mori Ōgai on the strength of a friendship dating from the time when Ōgai came before the world with Omokage, put out by the S.S.S. (the Shinseisha). He begins with the affair in which Roan, then an unknown student, called suddenly on Ōgai at Hanazonochō, was asked his business by the lady of the house, and went off in a temper and sent a piece to a newspaper entitled "I called on Ōgai and was not received" — whereupon Ōgai himself came that same night to apologize; and from there he tells of the unexpected side of a man who was at ease with him from their first meeting as though they were old acquaintances. Though he was rumored to be a crotchety man who turned callers away at the gate, he welcomed young visitors gladly and liked to argue until late at night. Taking an anecdote of the literary world written by Yamada Bimyō for Roan's work, he answered it with a great refutation, and when he learned his mistake apologized frankly. There are his energy on four hours' sleep, his arguments for vegetarian food and rice and for the Japanese house, his passion for collecting old maps of Edo and registers of the warrior houses and old books, and his last years given to the researches on Shibue Chūsai and the rest — all drawn with particular anecdotes. Judging that pure literature was not Ōgai's proper field and that it was in textual research that he had it in him to become great, Roan recalls their last conversation, in which he promised to write the inscription for his gravestone — "when you die I shall write it for your stone" — and mourns a death that came too early at sixty.

Characters

  • Mori Ōgai　Properly Mori Rintarō. An army surgeon and a great writer, who published Omokage as one of the S.S.S. The author came and went at his house from his own youth
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who visited Ōgai's house often from the days when he was unknown
  • Yamada Bimyō　The writer of the literary anecdote that brought on Ōgai's misunderstanding
  • Kako Tsurudo　Ōgai's friend from childhood, who acted as chairman of the funeral committee

The opening (original text)

In the days when Sohō's young Kokumin no Tomo was thought more of as the main stage of opinion than the Chūō Kōron or the Kaizō of today, the special supplements of spring and autumn were much talked of as a sort of gala performance by the great men of the time. The first was Bimyō's "The Naked Butterfly," which was well received; but the second, Omokage, was a treasure bowl heaped with jewels of waka and Chinese verse and new-style poetry, and with its changed diction and a beauty of color and a freshness like a basket of fruit it set the public boiling. Who could S.S.S. be? — the riddle of the unknown signature roused the readers' curiosity more and more. After a while it became known that S.S.S. was not one man's name but the roman initials of the Shinseisha, a society of young talents of the Red Gate, and that the army surgeon Mori Rintarō was at its head.

Ōgai hakase no tsuioku by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

It was in the old days, before he went to Kokura, that I was closest to Ōgai. Of late I had grown distant from Ōgai (and from other friends of the literary world as well), and did little more than sit at the same table at an occasional gathering; and yet, remembering, there is no end to what comes back. Even between people who do not go and come, even meeting only once in two or three years, there is an indescribable warmth when one is face to face with a man with whom one was once close. Even if one seldom meets, when it becomes a long parting there is a lonely feeling. And particularly with a great man like Ōgai, who showed energy enough to do the work of several men and to spare, it was a blessing to the world of learning that he should live even a day longer. Sixty-three, they say — but in fact he had only just come to his sixtieth year, and his head was clear until a few days before he died, and he was so full of spirit that he wanted to leave one line more written while there was breath in him; and the death of such a man is a matter of endless regret, like grieving over the death of a young man bright with hope.
