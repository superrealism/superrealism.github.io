What is Min'yūsha?

Category
Society / Group

A publishing house and intellectual group founded in Tokyo in 1887 by the critic Tokutomi Sohō together with disciples of the Ōe Gijuku school in his home province of Kumamoto. The general-interest magazine "Kokumin no Tomo," founded the same year, modeled on the American magazine "The Nation," was Japan's first general-interest magazine, and, criticizing the aristocratic bent of the domain-clique government's Westernization policy, it championed a "heiminshugi" ("commoner-ism") arguing for modernization from the standpoint of the common people, winning broad support among the young. In 1890 it also founded the daily newspaper "Kokumin Shimbun." "Kokumin no Tomo," alongside political commentary, placed weight on literature as well, sending into the world many works that remain landmarks of modern literary history: Futabatei Shimei's translation of Turgenev, "Aibiki" ("The Rendezvous," 1888), Mori Ōgai's "Maihime" ("The Dancing Girl," 1890), and Kunikida Doppo's "Musashino" and "Wasureenu Hitobito" ("Unforgettable People," both 1898). Tokutomi Roka, Sohō's younger brother, and Doppo, who contributed as a war correspondent with "Aitei Tsūshin," also had their base at Min'yūsha, and Roka became an overnight literary star with "Hototogisu" ("The Cuckoo," from 1898), serialized in "Kokumin Shimbun." After the First Sino-Japanese War, once Sohō leaned toward nationalism and drew close to the domain-clique government, the writers who had been drawn to heiminshugi drifted away, and "Kokumin no Tomo" too came to an end in 1898.
