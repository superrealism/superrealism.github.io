About this work

The piece for the year of the monkey — legends of the monkey — serialized irregularly in Taiyō in 1920. From the opening, in which he chases the word for monkey through many languages and proves from Hebrew etymology that the apes King Solomon sent for came from India, Kumagusu's comparative philology and natural history are at their sharpest. Under headings of general remarks, nature and folklore, it gathers up the traditions and beliefs about monkeys from all over the world.

Summary

The face of Lord Ichijō Kaneyoshi, the regent, closely resembled a monkey's. At his coming of age at thirteen a voice was heard in the empty air saying, "They have put a court cap on a monkey's head"; whereupon he at once capped it with, "The coming of age is at the declining of the hour of the sheep." With such an anecdote for a pillow, Kumagusu begins by saying that, the year of the sheep being now on the decline and the year of the monkey drawing near, he has been urged to write about the monkey. First he follows the names of the monkey through many languages: the Hebrew qoph, the Ethiopic kef, the Persian kebi, the Greek kepos, the Latin cebus, the Sanskrit kapi. No monkey is produced in Palestine, where the Old Testament was made; but among the gold and silver, ivory, apes and peacocks that King Solomon had brought once in three years by the ships of Tarshish, neither the ivory nor the peacock nor the ape is a native Hebrew word, but all are founded on Indian words. Therefore Solomon's apes and elephants were not African but Indian — and from such proofs of etymology the talk goes on to the elephants and monkeys carved on the Black Obelisk of Assyria and to the worship of the monkey Hanuman in India. Thereafter the nature of the monkey, the folklore of the monkey, and the relations of monkey and man are told at large; and in closing he takes up the belief that keeping a monkey in an elephant stable keeps the elephant well, explaining that the monkey draws off the evil eye as a lightning rod does, and ends by saying that he awaits the researches of psychologists, agriculturists and veterinarians.

The opening (original text)

(1) General remarks The face of Lord Ichijō Kaneyoshi, the regent, closely resembled a monkey's. When he came of age at thirteen a strange voice was heard in the empty air saying, "They have put a court cap on a monkey's head"; whereupon the lord ran out at once to the veranda and capped it with, "The coming of age is at the declining of the hour of the sheep." The story of the sheep which I began to write for this magazine has been left in the fashionable state of a work stoppage amid the noise of the problem of making a living and the rest; and now, the year of the sheep being on the decline and the year of the monkey drawing near, I have been urged by the Hakubunkan and by my readers alike to write about the monkey, and so this time I offer it short and complete in one piece, that no work stoppage may arise.

Jūnishikō (saru) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

To say a last word: I wrote that in India and Siam it is believed that keeping a monkey in an elephant stable keeps the elephant well.... The evil influence is what the scholars now call the evil eye, and it is because the monkey draws the force of the evil eye away like a lightning rod that the elephant escapes harm.... I fancy that, besides the destruction of vermin and the warding off of the evil eye, elephants and horses and cattle may in fact have a natural liking for the monkey. This matter I leave to the deeper research of psychologists and agriculturists and veterinarians.

Jūnishikō (saru) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
