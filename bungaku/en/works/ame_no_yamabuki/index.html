About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Kōdan Kurabu in 1952. Hyōgo, an elder brother who has fallen ill, and his younger brother Matasaburō. Around the ripples that a single letter from their sister brings into the house, it draws the affection of a family and the release of a young man's heart. A piece with the moist feeling of yamabuki flowers wet with rain.

Summary

Having looked in on his mother's sickroom, Matasaburō goes to his elder brother Hyōgo's room and finds Hyōgo, too, laid up in bed. Hyōgo, thin to begin with, had gone four or five nights without sleep when something unpleasant happened, and he had drunk too much and, toward dawn, had brought up what looked like blood. When Matasaburō asks what has happened, Hyōgo takes a sealed letter from under his pillow and hands it over without a word. It was addressed to Hyōgo, and the sender was their younger sister, Nyoi — and that letter sends ripples through the quiet household. His brother's illness, his sister's letter, and Matasaburō's own heart. The story traces, in a moist, delicate hand, the tangle of feeling among this brother and sister. At the close, Matasaburō, beside a young woman, watching petals scatter and cling to her white skin, thinks, with a feeling as fresh and released as though set free, "What shall I say to my brother." A piece of Yamamoto's, with the delicate, rain-wet feeling of yamabuki blossoms.

Characters

  • Matasaburō　Hyōgo's younger brother

The opening (original text)

Having looked in on his mother's sickroom, he went to his brother's room, and found his brother too talking with the doctor from his bed. The doctor left at once, and his brother lay back down. "What's the matter?" "My stomach's a bit off," Hyōgo said, frowning. "— I hadn't slept well for four or five nights, and then something unpleasant happened, and I'm afraid I had a bit too much to drink last night. Toward dawn I brought up something that looked like blood."

Ame no yamabuki by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The woman's arm was softly rounded and white, full of the healthy blood of youth. In time with the pleasant sound of the shears, a few scattering petals clung to that smooth white skin. — What shall I say to my brother. Matasaburō thought this, with a feeling as fresh and released as though set free.
