About this work

A short story Tayama Katai published in Waseda Bungaku in 1908. Drawing on the Russo-Japanese War, which he experienced himself as a war correspondent, it follows a private who slips out of a field hospital with beriberi to rejoin his unit, and dies alone, spent, without ever getting back to the line. Setting down exactly, in matter-of-fact description with sentiment refused, the miserable and lonely end of one nameless soldier — the opposite of a heroic war story — it is, with The Quilt, the high point of Katai's naturalism, and is known too as a forerunner of antiwar literature in modern Japan.

Summary

On the Manchurian front of the Russo-Japanese War, a common soldier confined to a field hospital with beriberi can no longer bear to while away the hours uselessly in the rear. Breaking free of the army surgeon who tries to stop him, he slips out of the hospital and sets off alone to catch up with his unit. His rifle, his pack, his very legs feel like lead; the illness has not left him at all, and fever and chills sweep through his body without cease. Stumbling on, guided only by the railway line, his condition steadily worsens. Clinging to passing transport soldiers for help, he at last reaches the supply depot at Anshanzhan, where he is given some care, but the pain will not subside, and his consciousness grows steadily more clouded. On a night lit only by a guttering candle, listening to the lonely cry of crickets, the soldier breathes his last with no one at his side. When the depot surgeon comes rushing at dawn, the man has already been dead a full hour. Just then, the guns of the general assault on Liaoyang begin to roar in earnest — and in the shadow of the nation's great war, Katai fixes, with a thoroughgoing, unsentimental objectivity, the lonely and wretched death met by one nameless soldier.

Characters

  • He (the common soldier)　A nameless soldier who slips out of a field hospital despite his beriberi to catch up with his unit. Driving his sick body onward in hopes of returning to the front, he collapses and dies alone, with no one to attend him
  • The depot surgeon　Tries to care for the soldier at the supply depot at Anshanzhan, but arrives too late to be present at his death

The opening (original text)

He started walking. The rifle was heavy, the pack was heavy, his legs were heavy, and the aluminum mess bowl clattered against the sword at his waist. The sound jarred so violently on his overwrought nerves that he tried again and again to fix it, but it would not stop — clatter, clatter, it went on. He had grown sick of it already. His illness had not truly healed, and his breath came in terrible gasps. Fever and chills passed ceaselessly through his whole body. His head burned as though on fire, and his temples throbbed with a violent pulse. Why had he left the hospital? The army surgeon had begged him so earnestly to stay, saying what came after mattered so much — why had he left the hospital?

The ending (original text)

The candle flickered. The crickets went on crying, just as lonesomely as before. At dawn the depot surgeon came. But a full hour before that, he had already died. By the time the first train, amid shouts of "clear the way, clear the way," pulled out toward Anshanzhan, the waning moon hung pale and forlorn, faintly white, in the sky. Before long the sound of guns began to roar in earnest. The attack on Liaoyang, on the first of September, had begun.
