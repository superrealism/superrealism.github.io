About this work

Visiting Hōryūji on official business, the narrator is taught the famous places of Yamato by Omichi, a bright girl he meets at his inn. The next day, having climbed at the risk of his neck to the top of the three-storey pagoda of Hokkiji with a temple boy for guide, he looks down from it on Omichi weeping in a field of rape blossom, pressed close to the priest Ryōnen — the hopeless love of a man who means to take orders and a girl who swears to hold to a love that will not be returned. At night he listens to the sound of her loom and her singing in the inn. A central work of sketch prose by the haiku poet Takahama Kyoshi, warmly praised by Natsume Sōseki.

Summary

"I," traveling through Yamato on official business to investigate old temples, take off my straw sandals at the Daikokuya, an inn before the gate of Hōryū-ji. I am met by O-Michi, a fair-skinned, quick-witted girl of seventeen or eighteen. From the inn's mezzanine floor stretches a whole field of rape blossoms and pear blossoms, and O-Michi points out, one after another with lively animation, every famous spot within sight — Miwa, Hatsuse, Yoshino, Mount Unebi. The next day, having finished my investigation of Hōki-ji, I am persuaded by a young acolyte of fifteen or sixteen to climb the three-storied pagoda. Timid as I am, I scramble my way up at the risk of my life, my haori coat covered in dust, and looking down from the top of the pagoda I see, amid the field of rape blossoms, the monk Ryōnen and O-Michi standing close together, O-Michi pressing her face into a cloth and weeping. The two are lovers, the boy tells me, but are being torn apart because Ryōnen must walk the path of the priesthood. O-Michi is said to have declared that though Ryōnen might cast her aside as he pleases, she herself would go on nursing her unrequited love until she pined away and died. That night, in the hollow emptiness of the inn, "I" sit writing my report, straining my ears toward the sound of two looms' reed-beaters. Told that the clearer of the two is O-Michi's, I listen as the local folk song she sings seems to seep into the stillness of the deepening night. Through a traveler's eyes, this masterpiece of shasei prose gently and purely gathers up the scenery of Yamato together with the shadow of a girl's aching, unrequited love.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, sent to investigate the old temples around Hōryū-ji on official business. He quietly captures, through his eyes, the scenery of Yamato and the people who live there
  • O-Michi　A weaver girl living behind the inn at Hōryū-ji. Quick-witted and cheerful, she carries an unrequited love for the monk Ryōnen
  • Ryōnen　A monk at Hōki-ji. Learned and earnest, but unable to bring himself to a decision, he cannot break cleanly with O-Michi
  • The young acolyte　A fifteen- or sixteen-year-old novice at Hōki-ji. He guides "I" up to the top of the three-storied pagoda, and tells the story of Ryōnen and O-Michi's love
  • The innkeeper's wife (O-Kami-san)　The mistress of the inn, who tells "I" about local life and about O-Michi's circumstances

The opening (original text)

　Before the south gate of the Yumedono at Hōryū-ji stand three inns, clustered close together. At one of them, called the Daikokuya, the rickshaw man set down his shafts. A girl of seventeen or eighteen came hurrying out from within. She was fair-skinned, with a face unusually quick and clever for a country girl. Deftly she took my luggage from the rickshaw man and led the way ahead of me. Going along the corridor, she climbed some three steps, then went along the corridor again and climbed another three steps, leading me at last to the innermost mezzanine room. The sight of her small figure, her back bent under the weight of my luggage, drew at my heart. 　She set the luggage down beside the alcove and threw open wide the paper screens facing south. It was a fine view, one that seemed to take in the whole of Yamato at a single glance. I leaned against the screen just as I was and gazed out. 　Just below this room, a field of rape blossoms stretched on and on in bloom. And it was not only rape blossoms — scattered among them stood pear trees on trellises, their blossoms too now at their height. The yellow of the rape blossoms was like the ground of a piece of woven cloth, and the white of the pear blossoms seemed raised high above it, like a pattern woven in relief.

The ending (original text)

　At last, after a while, the sound of the reed-beaters faded from my hearing altogether, and I finished my draft. Today my investigation was concluded, and tonight I finished writing my report as well. I felt suddenly and thoroughly weary. 　Turning the lamp down low, I changed into my night clothes and stretched my legs out beneath the quilt. The sound of the reed-beaters could still be heard. I had forgotten about it, but the duller-sounding one, too, could still be heard. 　Feeling it almost a pity to fall asleep, I drifted in and out of drowsiness. When the sound of the clock below striking twelve happened to reach my ears, I listened carefully, and found that the duller sound had already stopped — but O-Michi's reed-beater still rang out, clear and bright as ever.
