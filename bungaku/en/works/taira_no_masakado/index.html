About this work

A thousand years under the false charge of high treason — Yoshikawa Eiji draws Taira no Masakado again as one man who lived on the Bandō plain. Against a Kantō still shifting from a primeval state into land where people lived, it traces the road by which he was played with by the great houses of the capital, drawn into the quarrels of his clan, and came at last to be called the new emperor. The closing sentence — that in the end one might say the naked man remained, and that nakedness is a noble thing — states the author's view plainly.

Summary

From its primeval shape, little by little, toward a land where men live — the plain of Bandō was now greatly changing. The nature that had come down from antiquity was scarred here and there, and as though in the agony of an unavoidable moulting, Fuji and Asama and Mount Nasu, which ring that great plain far off, were always sending up sulphur-colored smoke. The story begins from this drawing of the country, and shows the young Masakado. Having gone up to the capital and served Fujiwara no Tadahira, what awaited him when he came home to Bandō was a quarrel with his uncles over land and over a woman. On a journey, lodging at an armorer's house at Noshimo, Masakado catches sight from his horse of a girl of sixteen or seventeen with her face lifted, dazzled, to the morning sun — such fine scenes are set among the great battles. ... The private feud with his kinsmen grows steadily larger, and when at length he attacks the provincial offices the matter is taken for rebellion against the court. Masakado is styled the New Emperor and proclaims the independence of Bandō; but his sovereignty did not last long. In the closing chapter the author dismisses the accounts of Sadamori and Hidesato in the Jinnō Shōtōki and elsewhere as "later embellishment," saying that the warrior houses of later ages gilded them in order to adorn the founders of their families. Therefore Hidesato and Sadamori are idols, not the men themselves. But Masakado still makes one feel the man just as he was. For a thousand years since the Tengyō era he has been dressed in the false garment of an arch-traitor; but no one now takes seriously the talk of his false offices and his usurpation — "one may say that in the end it is the naked man who has remained. The naked is precious."

Characters

  • Taira no Masakado　The man who lived on the plain of Bandō and styled himself the New Emperor
  • Taira no Sadamori　A cousin of the same house, who fights against Masakado
  • Fujiwara no Hidesato (Tawara Tōta)　A magnate of Shimotsuke who joins the campaign against Masakado
  • Fujibito　A pirate of the southern seas who plays with the world

Therefore Hidesato and Sadamori, whatever fine names they may have carried down to this day, are idols, not the men themselves. But Masakado still makes one feel the man just as he was.
