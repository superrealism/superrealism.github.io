About this work

The story of his own life told to "I" by a blind shakuhachi player met twice, once in the streets of Ginza and once on the beach at Kamakura. Told as a child by a fortune-teller that women would be his ruin, the man, for all his mother's warnings, got a girl of a mountain village with child and fled; in Tokyo he took up with the wife of the carpenter next door; and at last, losing his eyesight and his work, he fell to wandering with his flute. With the piercing sound of the shakuhachi for a frame, the whole life of a weak man who can only be carried along by his fate is told.

Summary

"I," having seen a blind man playing the shakuhachi at a crossroads in Ginza, meet him again that summer on the beach at Kamakura where I am staying, invite him home, and ask about his life. The man's name is Shūzō. The son of a poor samurai family of a castle town, he was told as a child by a fortune-teller to "be careful of women all your life." His mother believed it and never stopped preaching to him how dreadful women were; but at twelve he was already being petted by a girl of the neighborhood, and in his heart he was glad of it. Losing his grandmother and his mother, and sent out from his aunt's house as a primary-school teacher, he was in the spring of his nineteenth year as good as tricked by Takenojō of the next village into a union with the man's sister Oyuki. When he was told that she was with child and pressed to marry her, Shūzō could not bring himself to tell his aunt, and, trying to escape the fate the fortune-teller had named, slipped away alone into the dark and fled the district. In Tokyo, after ten years of hardship, he got a place as a hired man in the railway bureau; but he grew friendly with Tōkichi, a carpenter living next door in the tenement, and took up with his wife Otoshi. When Tōkichi quarreled with his master and went off to work in Yokohama, Otoshi moved into Shūzō's house and the two lived quite as husband and wife. Just then an eye disease cost him his post, and as his sight failed Otoshi went off without ado with the head carpenter. Saying that it was all his punishment, the blind man played one more piece and went away, and the next day he was not to be seen at Kamakura.

Characters

  • Tōkichi　A carpenter living next door in the Tokyo tenement

The opening (original text)

It happened four years ago (so a certain man began). I was walking through Ginza on some errand when I saw a man playing the shakuhachi at the corner of a crossroads. Seven or eight people were standing before him, and I too stopped without thinking and joined the listeners. It was the end of May, and the sun had gone down in the west so that the shadow of the houses on the west side had crept two or three feet up from the foundations of the houses opposite. The man playing the flute was lit from the waist up by the clear evening sun. Because it was near dusk the street was at its most crowded: the passing of the horse tramway, the sound of wheels as the rickshaws ran east and west, the tread of people hurrying on their way — all about was a confused uproar. And in this noisy place, at this noisy hour, that man was playing his flute quite at his ease. For that reason, even the spring evening sun on half his body looked to my eyes calm and gentle, and it seemed to me that as far as the sound of his flute reached, a quiet little world of its own had been made.

The ending (original text)

"The two of them went away. I could neither cry out nor weep. When I thought that all this was my punishment, there rose before my eyes the worn figure of my mother, and Oyuki as I had left her with child. I was dismissed from my post; one eye went blind altogether, and the other, though it could see a little, was of no use for anything; and meanwhile the little I had saved was gone. From there I sank to what you see now. But I do not think of it as sad any more. Only when, with the sound of the flute I play myself, I remember my mother whom I loved, I think sometimes that I would rather have died — and yet I cannot die either." Before he left, the blind man played one more piece. I could hardly bear to listen to its mournful, sorrowing sound. The strain of love, the feeling of the past, the sadness of a drifting life — and beneath it all, surely, an everlasting regret. The moon went down in the west, and the blind man went away. The next day I did not see him at Kamakura.
