About this work

Kenji's own preface to The Restaurant of Many Orders, the only collection of his children's stories published in his lifetime. Eating the beautifully transparent wind, drinking the lovely pink morning sunlight — in barely a page, where his stories came from is told as beautifully as it could be. Often quoted as the doorway to his work, more often even than the stories themselves.

Summary

"Even without owning as much rock sugar as we should like, we can eat the clean transparent wind and drink the beautiful peach-colored sunlight of the morning." So Kenji begins, and goes on to say that in the fields and in the woods he has often seen dreadfully ragged clothes turned into the most splendid garments of velvet and woolen cloth set with jewels. He is fond, he says, of such beautiful food and clothes. These stories were all of them received from rainbows and moonlight in the woods and the fields and along the railway line — when he passed alone through the blue evening of an oak wood, or stood shivering in the November mountain wind, he could not help feeling like this, and he has simply written it as it was. So there will be places in them that will do you good, and places that are just that and nothing more. There will be places whose sense is not clear, but of those he does not know the sense himself. And even so: "But I cannot tell you how much I wish that some few pieces of these little stories may become, in the end, your transparent, real food." The twentieth of December, the twelfth year of Taishō. Miyazawa Kenji.

The opening (original text)

Even without owning as much rock sugar as we should like, we can eat the clean transparent wind and drink the beautiful peach-colored sunlight of the morning. And I have often seen, in the fields and in the woods, dreadfully ragged clothes turned into the most splendid garments of velvet and of woolen cloth, set with jewels. I am fond of such beautiful food and clothes.

Chūmon no ōi ryōriten jo by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Even if we do not have all the rock sugar we want, we can eat the clean, clear wind and drink the lovely peach-colored light of morning.

The opening of the preface to the only collection of children's stories published in his lifetime. It speaks Kenji's view of the world: even without material plenty, one can receive beautiful things from nature.

I cannot tell you how much I hope that some few pieces of these little stories may, in the end, become clear, true food for you.

The close of the preface. The earnest wish of a writer offering his stories as nourishment for the reader's heart—“clear, true food.”
