About this work

An early short story by Tsuboi Sakae, published in Bungei in 1938. In an island village suggesting Shōdoshima, it draws, through the eyes of small Ken, who begs to go with his mother to Kobe, the days of a family — a mother wearing herself out in poverty, and a sick younger sister. A child's sulking and the tired gentleness that encloses it are entrusted to unremarkable details of daily life, like radish leaves. The warm eye for ordinary lives that leads to Twenty-Four Eyes shows early here.

Summary

Ken's mother is to go again tonight to the hospital in Kobe, taking the baby Katsuko with her. Ken wants very much to go along on the steamer, but his mother will not give him a good answer. She who at other times carries Katsuko on her back and leads Ken by the hand to the festival and to the sports day will not take him to Kobe. He has never once been on a steamer, and Katsu-chan, a baby, is going for the second time — Ken's grievance only swells. I shall buy you a present, so wait at Grandmother's house, his mother says again and again. But Ken does not understand. Why must his mother cross the sea so many times? What is happening to Katsuko? The days of being left behind, the time spent at his grandmother's house in the next village, and his mother's face when she comes home — a mother managing a poor household and wearing herself out for her children comes gradually into view from the height of a small boy's eyes. And at the end, in Ken's eyes as he is carried home on her back along the night road, there is a sky of stars as clear as autumn.

Characters

  • Ken　A small boy who begs to go with his mother to Kobe
  • Mother　A mother who goes back and forth between the island and Kobe with a baby in her arms
  • Katsuko　Ken's little sister. A baby taken to the hospital in Kobe for her illness

The opening (original text)

I Ken's mother is to go again tonight to the hospital in Kobe, taking the baby Katsuko with her. Ken thought that somehow he wanted to go to Kobe with his mother, but his mother would not give him a good answer at all. Standing beside his mother, who was putting the clothes and the odds and ends spread all over the room into a big wicker trunk, and taking them out again and packing them differently, Ken was in a sulk.

Daikon no ha by Tsuboi Sakae (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Over the dark sea the flares of the fishing boats drifting slowly moved to the right and to the left, and the sky, seen after so long, was bright with stars as on an autumn night. "Oh, what beautiful stars — look, Ken!" His mother stopped and hitched Ken up on her back.
