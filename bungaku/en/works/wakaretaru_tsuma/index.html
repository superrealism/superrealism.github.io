About this work

A short story Chikamatsu Shūkō published in Waseda Bungaku in 1910. The work that raised his name, and known as a bare confessional I-novel. A man who cannot let go of his attachment to Omiya, the wife he has parted from, and who, knowing that he has no right to call her by the familiar pronoun, still cannot help doing so, writes out in letters, with no regard for shame or appearances, the torment of his days since the separation and his fixation on the woman. Famous as a literature of obsession that lays bare an almost witless infatuation.

Summary

"Dear sir — no, dear you. Now that we have parted, I no longer have the right to call you that." So the letter begins, and yet the man, unable to let go of his lingering love for his divorced wife Omiya, cannot help but go on addressing her as "you" all the same. Word has reached him, on the wind, that Omiya has already married another man — and still he cannot help writing this letter. Omiya was a woman who had once made her living in the world of bars and hostessing, and it is impossible to tell truth from lie in anything she says; even her hometown and her name are dressed in layer upon layer of falsehood. Artless and calculating, deeply affectionate and utterly elusive all at once — the man has been dragged along, past all hope of rescue, by this woman. In the letter he lays bare, without shame or reserve, the men in her past, his own loneliness and anguish since their parting, and the jealousy and attachment that still burn in his chest even now. Confessing, without adornment, a foolishness that can only be called blind infatuation, the piece stands as one of the prototypes of the Japanese I-novel. It is the representative work of a literature of obsession that first made Chikamatsu Shūkō's name.

Characters

  • I (the man)　The narrator, who cannot let go of his lingering love for his divorced wife Omiya, and pours his obsession and anguish into a letter
  • Omiya (Shimoda Shima)　The man's divorced wife. Once a woman of the bar-hostess world, she bewitches and goes on dragging the man along with words and deeds that could be truth or lies alike

The opening (original text)

Dear sir. You — now that we have parted, I no longer have the right to call you that. What is more, I hear, on the wind, that you may already have married. If that is so, then you are now, past all recall, another man's wife. To send a letter like this to such a woman is, by every measure of reason, wrong of me to do. And yet I cannot help myself; I still cannot help calling you "you." I beg you — let me call you that, if only within the pages of this letter.

Wakaretaru tsuma ni okuru tegami by Chikamatsu Shūkō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Ah, when I think of it, it grieves me — and yet I had known, long before, from the moment she said, "I went right over and asked about it for you," that it would come to this in the end. All manner of things rose up in me at once, and though I felt something choking at the back of my throat, I bore it quietly, and, putting on a cheerful, amused face, told the story — right there in front of the two of them — of that night she gave me the beating, mimicking even the woman's gestures and the tone of her voice.
