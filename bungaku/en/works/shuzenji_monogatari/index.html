About this work

A play Okamoto Kidō published in the magazine Bungei Kurabu in 1911. Suggested by the mask of Yoriie preserved at Shuzenji in Izu, it is a central work of the new kabuki, staged by Ichikawa Sadanji II and one that raised Kidō's name. It draws the artist's spirit of a mask-carver who will not allow a work that fails to satisfy him, and the mystery by which true art foretells a fate: a masterpiece of modern drama.

Summary

In the village of Shuzenji in Izu lives the mask maker Yasha-ō, to whose home the former shogun Minamoto no Yoriie — driven from Kamakura and now living in seclusion here — comes in person to press for the mask he had commissioned to preserve his own likeness. But Yasha-ō tells him that after half a year's labor, he has yet to carve a single mask that satisfies him. However many times he remakes it, the mask shows no life in it — only, again and again, the face of a dead man haunted by grudge. Yasha-ō, unwilling to let so unsatisfactory a work go out into the world, refuses to hand it over — but his elder daughter Katsura, hoping to soothe Yoriie's anger, presents the mask to him without her father's leave. Yoriie is delighted with the mask, and, in his desire for advancement, the proud Katsura is taken into his household as a concubine and given the name "the Lady of Wakasa." That night, Kanakubo Yukichika, an assassin in the service of the Hōjō, raids Shuzenji by night to take Yoriie's life. Katsura puts on Yoriie's mask and robes and, crying out, "Here stands Yoriie, Left Palace Guard Commander," fights her way through as a decoy to deceive the enemy, but, gravely wounded, collapses at her father's house. Word soon comes that Yoriie too has been slain. Before his dying daughter, Yasha-ō gazes at the blood-soaked mask and realizes, without having known it himself, that the death-mask he had carved was in fact a true likeness of the fate destined for Yoriie all along. Boasting that this is proof his art has reached the divine, and that he is without peer under heaven, Yasha-ō nonetheless takes up his brush once more, to sketch his own daughter's dying face as a model for the future.

Characters

  • Yasha-ō　A mask maker of Shuzenji in Izu. An artist through and through, who will not let an unsatisfactory work leave his hands — and who, in the end, puts his art even above his own daughter's death
  • Katsura　Yasha-ō's elder daughter. Proud and ambitious for advancement, she becomes Yoriie's concubine and dies as his decoy
  • Kaede　Yasha-ō's younger daughter. Modest and craftsmanlike by nature, and Haruhiko's wife
  • Haruhiko　Kaede's husband, and a mask maker apprenticed to Yasha-ō
  • Minamoto no Yoriie　The former shogun, driven from Kamakura and living in seclusion at Shuzenji, who commissions a mask of his own likeness
  • Kanakubo Yukichika　An assassin in the service of the Hōjō, who raids Shuzenji by night to kill Yoriie

The opening (original text)

KATSURA (presently stopping her hands at the fulling block) — I've been pounding on and on until my shoulders and arms feel quite numb. Shall we not leave off now, and call it enough for today? KAEDE — Even so, since yesterday was the last of the Bon holiday, ought we not to set to work in earnest again, starting today? KATSURA — If you wish to work, then work by yourself. You'll earn father's praise, and Haruhiko-dono's too. As for me, I've had enough of it — I hate it. (She flings down the fulling mallet) KAEDE — Sister, we've pounded this paper on the fulling block together for years now, as our humble trade demands, and yet you say you're sick of it, you hate it — this manner of yours these days, so changed from what it used to be, whatever can it mean?

The ending (original text)

YASHA-Ō — However many times I remade this mask, it showed, again and again, unmistakably, the face of death — and that was no fault of my clumsiness, no dullness of my hand. Only now, this very moment, do I understand: it was because such was the fate destined for the Genji general, Lord Yoriie. A man's fate is known to none but the gods — and that it should appear, of all places, first in a work of my own hand, is nature's own response, nature's own mystery. This is what it means for one's art to enter the divine. I, Yasha-ō of Izu — splendid indeed, if I say so myself, and without peer under heaven. (He laughs, well pleased) KATSURA (laughing likewise) — And I too have been a splendid Lady of the Bedchamber. I have nothing left to regret, even in death. Only let me hurry after my lord, to serve him even in the world beyond... YASHA-Ō — Now then, daughter. I should like to copy down the face of a young woman in her death agony, as a model for times to come. Bear the pain a little longer. Haruhiko — brush and paper... YASHA-Ō — Daughter, show me your face. KATSURA — Yes. (Katsura crawls forward, supported by Haruhiko and his wife. Yasha-ō takes up his brush and sets about copying her face. The priest murmurs the nembutsu under his breath.) —Curtain—
