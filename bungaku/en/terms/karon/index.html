What is Karon (Waka Poetics)?

Category
Form / Genre

Writings and treatises discussing the nature and craft of waka, and judging the merits of individual poems. Japan's first sustained work of waka poetics is the kana preface Ki no Tsurayuki attached to the "Kokin Wakashū," which opens, "Japanese poetry has the human heart as its seed, and puts forth countless leaves of words," and goes on to describe the origin and function of poetry down to a critique of contemporary poets. Thereafter, in the middle of the Heian period, Fujiwara no Kintō's "Shinsen Zuinō" appeared, and the language of criticism was honed through the judges' comments (hanshi) rendered at poetry contests (utaawase) that assessed superior and inferior verses. Fujiwara no Shunzei held up the beauty of yūgen (mysterious depth) in "Korai Fūteishō," and his son Teika discussed the art of honkadori (allusive variation) and the relationship between feeling and diction in "Kindai Shūka" and "Maigetsushō." Kamo no Chōmei, author of "Hōjōki," also left a work of poetics recording his experience as a poet, "Mumyōshō." Waka poetics was carried forward into medieval treatises on renga and into Zeami's writings on noh, nurturing the very language used to discuss a work, and building a long tradition of literary criticism in Japan. When Masaoka Shiki, in 1898, rejected the "Kokin Wakashū" and looked instead to the "Man'yōshū" in "A Letter to Practitioners of Tanka," that too was a work of waka poetics standing in this thousand-year-old conversation.
