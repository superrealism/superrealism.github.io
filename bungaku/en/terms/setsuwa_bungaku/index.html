What is Setsuwa Bungaku (Anecdotal Literature)?

Category
Form / Genre

Literature that writes down and compiles myths, legends, folk tales, and other orally transmitted stories. The Buddhist monk Kyōkai's "Nihon Ryōiki," compiled in the early Heian period, stands as the oldest surviving anecdote collection, and from the late Heian into the Kamakura period numerous such collections were produced. Foremost among them, the "Konjaku Monogatarishū," thought to have been compiled in the early twelfth century, is the largest anecdote collection of all, gathering over a thousand tales in thirty-one scrolls divided into India, China, and Japan, with every tale opening "Ima wa mukashi" ("Now it is the past"), and conveying the figures of every class of person, from aristocrat to warrior, bandit, and commoner. The Kamakura period saw further collections such as "Uji Shūi Monogatari" and "Hosshinshū," compiled by Kamo no Chōmei, author of "Hōjōki," gathering tales of religious awakening and renunciation of the world. Each individual anecdote is short and plain, but the great value of these collections lies in the way they capture raw desire, comedy, and the lives of nameless people that the courtly tale literature never scooped up. It was Akutagawa Ryūnosuke who revived this vein of ore for modern literature: his series of court tales drawing on the "Konjaku Monogatarishū" and other sources — "Rashōmon," "Hana" ("The Nose"), "Imogayu" ("Yam Gruel") — are masterworks of retelling that breathe modern psychology into the framework of old anecdotes.
