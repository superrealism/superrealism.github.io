About this work

A novella representative of Izumi Kyōka's fantastic writing, published by Shun'yōdō in 1908. A single ball-bouncing song his dead mother sang to him as a child — searching for the rest of it, the young man Hagoshi Akira wanders the provinces, strays into a ruined mansion at Akiya on the Miura peninsula that is rumoured to be a haunted place, and is played with by weird and beautiful apparitions, all drawn in Kyōka's own gorgeous style. Fusing the theme of longing for a mother with the grotesque and fantastic, its labyrinthine world, in which the border of reality and dream collapses, is held to be the furthest point of his aesthetic.

Summary

The area around Hayama and Akiya, near the great landslide of Miura on the Sagami coast, has long been rumoured a "place of demons." A mansion there, feared as a haunted house whose occupants meet with one uncanny happening after another, now stands empty. To this mansion comes Hagoshi Akira, a young man on his travels, seeking a night's lodging. He is wandering aimlessly through the provinces in search of the forgotten rest of the words of a ball-bouncing song his mother, whom he lost as a small child, once sang to him. As he listens to the ghost stories told by the old servant who keeps the house, and by ascetics passing through, one strange vision after another rises up before Akira. A beautiful woman, a ball dancing through the air, apparitions night after night — the border between reality and dream dissolves, and the mansion itself turns into a single labyrinth, luring Akira ever deeper into the memory of his mother. The melody of the ball-bouncing song he has sought so long, and the face of his beloved dead mother, come together within the eerie, beautiful visions, and at last the young man steps into a world of illusion not of this earth.

The opening (original text)

They call the great landslide of Miura a place of demons. There the foot of Sakurayama, which shuts off the Hayama shoreline like a folding screen, plunges into the ocean like some unfamiliar beast; on one side lies the beach of Chōjaen, and in the season for sea-bathing, from Zushi through Morito to Hayama, it is hereabouts that most of the drownings occur.

Kusa meikyū by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

It sounds most dear to me now, I think, as a sudden wind roars through the thick of the trees, the leaves rushing like a swift green current... a trailing cloud — that, that trailing cloud.

Kusa meikyū by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)
