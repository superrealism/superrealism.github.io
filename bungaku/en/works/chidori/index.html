About this work

Suzuki Miekichi's first work, noticed by Natsume Sōseki and carried in Hototogisu on his recommendation. A young man who has spent a summer on a small island in the Inland Sea recalls his faint understanding with Fuji, the daughter of the inn where he stayed, in a delicate hand poised between dream and waking. A fine lyrical novel, crystallizing like a poem the feeling for days gone by and the faint pain of a young love.

Summary

"I" spent one summer on a small island in the Seto Inland Sea, partly to recuperate. Warmly welcomed by the family who ran the inn, I came to share, above all with Fuji-san, the seventeen- or eighteen-year-old daughter of the house, a bond of few words but unforgettable feeling. The seaside life, the people of the island, and each small moment spent with Fuji-san sank quietly into the young man's heart. Before long summer ended, and "I" left the island. Even after returning to the city, the memory of Fuji-san never faded, and late at night, alone, I would hold up to the lamplight the red sleeve she had given me on the island, and trace back again and again in my heart those days as "the tale of the plovers." It matters nothing where the real Fuji-san might now be, or how she fares — for the young Fuji-san and the young "I" are sealed forever, unaging, within this sleeve. In prose as beautiful as a dream, the work depicts the faint stirring of first love, and the young man's lyricism as he folds that love away into an eternal memory. This was Miekichi's memorable debut work, sent out into the literary world by Natsume Sōseki, who recognized and prized his talent.

Characters

  • I　The young narrator. Spends one summer on a Seto Inland Sea island partly to recuperate, and later recalls the faint bond he shared with Fuji-san, the innkeeper's daughter, as "the tale of the plovers"
  • Fuji-san　The seventeen- or eighteen-year-old daughter of the inn's family. A woman of few words, who nonetheless shares an unforgettable affection with "I"
  • Obasan and Hatsuya　People of the island inn. Welcome "I" warmly

The opening (original text)

The tale of the plovers begins with Ochō, a horse-trader's daughter. On the evening of a mild autumn day, pale-faced Ochō sits forlornly on a straw mat spread beneath the eaves. Not even a thread's width of sunlight now falls on the flattened stalks laid out to dry. It is "I" who comes climbing the hill in Western clothes. Ochō looks up at me with that same look, as though about to cry. Her thumb and little finger, and the gesture of tying a sash crosswise, mean Hatsuya. She waves her hand to say that all three of them are out. Pointing inward with her chin and miming a hand pillow — what that means, I cannot tell. The loosened strands of her hair, bound with straw, fall again across her face the moment she brushes them back.

Plovers by Suzuki Miekichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Since then I have long ceased to think of Fuji-san at all. I do think of her often, it's true — but it is not Fuji-san herself I think of. It is the Fuji-san within the tale of the plovers. Even now I sometimes take out that sleeve to look at it. On sleepless evenings I am sure to bring it out. This sleeve is no good to look at unless the night has grown late. Once it has, I pinch the two corners of the sleeve, raise my arm at a slant, and hang it before the lamp flame. When the lamplight soaks through the red of the sleeve and the flame clouds at its center, I drift, before I know it, into the tale of the plovers, and move together with Fuji-san as though in a moving picture. I watch my own play, performed by myself. Until I have seen the tale of the plovers through, in full detail, from beginning to end, I do not notice how tired my upraised arms have grown. As I fold the sleeve away, I think this: that within this sleeve, the seventeen- or eighteen-year-old Fuji-san and the twenty-year-old "I" are sealed away forever, never to grow old. It matters nothing where Fuji-san may be now, or how she fares. My Fuji-san is the Fuji-san within the sleeve. Fuji-san can always be seen there, vividly, whenever I wish. The reason people so often say "plover, plover" is the crest-pattern woven into that silk crepe — the plover design.

Plovers by Suzuki Miekichi (Aozora Bunko)
