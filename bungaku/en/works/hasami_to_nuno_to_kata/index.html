About this work

Written in December 1952, and finished about a week before Kusaka Yōko's death: a one-act play with only two characters, a famous designer and a mannequin. The whole piece is the argument in which the doll pronounces human beings pitiful and strips away honour, art and talent as so many poses. In her last manuscript, "The Death I Do Not Know How Many Times," the author wrote that she had meant this play to be her final work.

Summary

The studio of the designer Tanikawa Suwako. At the back a mannequin stands draped in cloth. Told by a customer that the buttons of a finished suit will not fasten, Suwako is indignant: it is the customer's fault for putting on weight, and in ten years she has not once made a mistake. Then the mannequin speaks — surely it was a miscalculation, since you did not reckon that people grow fat.

The quarrel spreads in time to human beings themselves. They grow fat, they grow thin, they grow old, they wrinkle and stoop; they love and suffer and weep; and after every height there must come death. That is why human beings are pitiable, says the doll. Shaken, Suwako telephones her husband without meaning to, and is cut off and comes back. If she tries to sink into her memories she is mocked with "you were young then"; she is told that art which imitates nature cannot possibly be more beautiful than nature; and she is laughed at with the remark that honor is only the pose of one who wants a bronze statue put up.

Made to stand side by side before the great mirror, Suwako looks at herself. Against the doll, whose face and eyes never change because it has no heart, she has gone on making poses in every situation of her life — "please, I beg you, make me into a doll." That is impossible, the doll answers, and urges her to begin her work.

Characters

  • Tanikawa Suwako　A designer who has won a name, with a dozen or more pupils
  • The mannequin　Standing at the back of the studio, which speaks to Suwako

The opening (original text)

The mannequin Tanikawa Suwako Desks, tables, chairs and the rest scattered about; from the center toward stage right, dress forms and bolts of cloth scattered; in a recess at the back a mannequin, draped in cloth, holding a pose. (Note: the mannequin's makeup and movements are to be inhuman. The speech as it is.) In the center, one large full-length mirror. Strange music. Curtain

The ending (original text)

SUWAKO: Say nothing more; be quiet, I beg you. I cannot become a doll. MANNEQUIN: Yes, that is so. And therefore, come. The scissors and the cloth, and make a shape, if you please. A beautiful shape. Strange music. The mannequin begins to dance again. — Curtain —
