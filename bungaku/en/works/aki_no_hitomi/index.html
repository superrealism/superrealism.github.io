About this work

The only collection Yagi Jūkichi published in his lifetime. It opens with a short preface — that he cannot bear to be without a friend, and that he has none — and sets out poems stripped of every ornament, as clear as prayer. Faith and solitude, love for a small child, and a thirst for what is beautiful, set down in plain words with much hiragana. Two years after it appeared, the poet died at twenty-nine.

Summary

"I cannot bear to be without a friend. But I have none. I offer these poor poems to the breast of whoever will read them. And please make me your friend" — the preface is only these few lines. The poems collected are mostly as short, a few lines each. "hold your breath / hold your breath / the baby is looking at the sky" ("Hold Your Breath"). "a white branch / a thin, painful branch / in my heart / a white branch" ("A White Branch"). He asks where beautiful things are ("Beautiful Things"), crouches on the hills of his home country and watches his own remorse burning ("The Hills of Home"), and feels autumn at the sight of the red pouch hung on an infant's back. The smallest things of daily life — the sound of a gardener's shears, a willow, a festival float, a baby's pouch — turn as they are into thanks and prayer to God. "Don't cry, child / don't wail, child / look at this father of yours / he does not weep / and he does not laugh." The words are few beyond measure, and for that each single word rings clear. A book strong because it is unadorned, holding a place of its own in modern Japanese poetry.
