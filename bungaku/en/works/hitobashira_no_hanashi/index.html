About this work

A folklore essay in which Minakata Kumagusu discussed the custom of the human pillar — burying a person alive to secure a building or engineering work — searching out legends from all over the world. Published in Hentai Shinri in 1925. Citing countless legends of human pillars in bridges, castles, embankments and ponds from India and Burma to Europe and Japan, it goes as far as the difference between the human pillar and the punishment of walling a criminal up: a piece overflowing with Minakata's learning.

Summary

The custom of setting up a human pillar is still practiced among some peoples even now, and its legends and old sites are numerous even among the civilized nations, Minakata begins. In India there are countless examples of girls and infants buried alive when building forts, bridges, and ponds; in Burma, people were buried beneath castle gates as guardians, and an empress, it is said, was sunk in a pond to secure an embankment. In Japan too, the Genpei Seisuiki records that Kiyomori made a human pillar of a child boy when building Kyō no Shima, and legends of human pillars remain in various regions. While ranging widely across these examples from East and West, Minakata further introduces the cruel punishment, found in Persia and France, of walling up a criminal alive, leaving only the head exposed, to await death — quite apart from the human pillar proper. At the end, he traces the etymology surrounding the yagura (valley storehouses, rock storehouses) common at Kamakura, and extends his pen so far as to caution against the popular notion that hastily links these to human pillars and stone dungeons.

The opening (original text)

The setting up of a human pillar to secure a building or an engineering work is still practiced among certain tribal peoples even now, and its legends and old sites are not few among the civilized nations. For example, it is reported from time to time in the newspapers that native tribes of India still practice this today, and in Bompas's collection of folktales of the Santal Parganas there is a story in which a king, resenting the strength of his son-in-law, has him mount his horse and stand in a dried-up pond that yields no water even when animals are offered to it — and, worthy warrior that he is, the son-in-law does not retreat even as the water begins to well up, but sings, as it rises, 'now to the horse's knees, now to my own knees, now to my back,' and at last is submerged entirely. His wife, following after him, is also said to have drowned herself in that same pond.

Hitobashira no hanashi by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In Higashimuro county in Kii there are many shrines to the god Yagura Myōjin. In the local dialect, a steep mountain is called a kura, and in various estates a god enshrined on a steep, rocky mountain is often called Yagura Myōjin. In most cases these enshrine the spirit of the rock itself and have no separate shrine building. The theory in the Kii Zoku Fudoki, volume 81, is that the ya of yagura is a contraction of iwa, so that the meaning is "rock storehouse." The Karaito Sōshi records that Lady Karaito, having been caught in an attempt to stab Yoritomo, was put into a "stone prison" — so it may be that "rock storehouse" is the more correct reading than "valley storehouse." In any case, this yagura, though read the same as the word for a turret or watchtower, is a different thing entirely.
