About this work

Yamate in Yokohama, two years after the Great Kantō Earthquake. Among the pigweed on the site of a fallen house stood a piano with its lid still open — and though no one has touched it, it gives out a faint sound in the moonlight. A piece of some three pages Akutagawa set down among the ruins after the earthquake. What is left is neither quite the uncanny nor quite a poem.

Summary

On a rainy autumn day, "I" was walking through the Yamate district of Yokohama to call on someone. The devastation of this area had scarcely changed since the time of the earthquake. On the site of one collapsed house, a bow-shaped piano, its lid left open, stood half-crushed against a wall, its keys still glistening and wet. In the goosefoot weeds around it lay scattered sheets of music, their covers pink and pale blue. My business ran long, and I did not take my leave until after nightfall. The rain had cleared, and the moon let its light spill through a wind-swept sky. Hurrying my steps so as not to miss my train, I suddenly heard the sound of someone touching the piano. I turned, but there was no one to be seen anywhere. The piano only let its long, narrow keyboard glimmer faintly in the moonlight. Unnerved, I quickened my pace, and behind me the sound came again, faint, once more. Some days later, "I" passed that same road again, and in the bright light of day walked up to the piano. Its ivory gloss was gone, the lacquer of its lid had flaked away, and vines had wound themselves around its legs. "I wonder if this thing can even still make a sound." No sooner had I murmured this to myself than the piano at once let out a faint note. What rose in me was less astonishment than a smile. On the keyboard, a fallen chestnut had somehow come to rest. Looking up, I saw that a chestnut tree, pressed askew by the slate roof, had been leaning over the piano all along. But, I write, it made no difference either way. I only kept my eyes fixed on the bow-shaped piano among the goosefoot weeds — the piano that, ever since last year's earthquake, had gone on keeping a sound no one else knew.

The opening (original text)

On a rainy autumn day, I was walking through the Yamate district of Yokohama to call on a certain person. The devastation of this area had scarcely changed since the time of the earthquake. If anything at all had changed, it was only that goosefoot weeds had grown up thick among the slate roofs and brick walls piled one upon another. On the site of one collapsed house, even a bow-shaped piano, its lid left open, stood half-crushed against a wall, its keys still glistening and wet.

The ending (original text)

After I returned to the road, I turned to look back once more at these ruins. The chestnut tree, which I had only now noticed, leaned slantwise over the piano, pressed down by the slate roof. But it made no difference either way. I only kept my eyes fixed on the bow-shaped piano among the goosefoot weeds — the piano that, ever since last year's earthquake, had gone on keeping a sound no one else knew.
