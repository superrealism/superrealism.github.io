What is Engo (Associative Words)?

Category
Style & Technique

A rhetorical technique in waka poetry that scatters through a single poem several words closely related in meaning, so that they resonate with one another. For example, against the word "ito" ("thread"), a poet would place words naturally associated with it, such as "yoru" ("to twist"), "haru" ("to stretch"), "midaru" ("to tangle"), or "hokorobu" ("to fray"), weaving into the poem a layer of association distinct from its surface meaning. Engo are often combined with kakekotoba (pivot words), quietly supporting the second, hidden meaning that a kakekotoba creates. The sets of words that function as engo for a given term — "tsuyu" ("dew"), "sode" ("sleeve"), and "nuru" ("to grow wet") for "namida" ("tears"); "haru" ("to draw a bow"), "iru" ("to shoot"), and "kaeru" ("to return") for "yumi" ("bow") — were more or less fixed, and poets drew on them as shared cultural knowledge. The technique became established during the age of the "Kokin Wakashū" and represents the ornate diction characteristic of court waka. The reader, following the surface narrative, comes to notice the connections quietly forged beneath it between word and word, and so savors the poem twice over. How to weave a rich world into the limited vessel of thirty-one syllables was the great ingenuity of court poets, and it is concentrated here, together with kakekotoba and jokotoba (prefatory verses).
