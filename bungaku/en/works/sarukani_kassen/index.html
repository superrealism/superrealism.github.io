About this work

A satirical story Akutagawa wrote as a sequel to the old tale of the crab and the monkey. With the mortar, the wasp and the egg, the crab duly avenges itself on the monkey — and afterwards they are all arrested, and the crab, as ringleader, is put to death. A piece that strikes with sharp irony at public opinion and at what passes for justice.

Summary

The monkey who stole the crab's rice ball was at last paid out by the crab. Together with the mortar, the bee and the egg, the crab killed his hated enemy the monkey — but the fairy tale does not tell what came after. In fact, after taking their revenge they were arrested by the police and every one of them thrown into prison. At the trial the crab, as principal, was sentenced to death, and the mortar, the bee and the egg, as accomplices, to penal servitude for life. Public opinion at first sympathized with the crab, but soon turned its hand over: killing the monkey showed a want of common sense, it said; and men of learning preached that it was the tragedy of the uneducated ruled by instinct. And the crab's family: the wife became a prostitute, the eldest son turned his coat, the second son became an ironical novelist.... "In any case, this much is a fact: once he has fought the monkey, the crab is bound to be killed for the good of the world. A word to the readers of the world: most of you are crabs too." A satire of Akutagawa's, striking with sharp irony at the hypocrisy of justice and public opinion and at the heartlessness of society.

The opening (original text)

The monkey who stole the crab's rice ball was at last paid out by the crab. Together with the mortar, the bee and the egg, the crab killed his hated enemy the monkey. — There is no need to tell that story over again now. But what became of the crab and his confederates after they had finished off the monkey — that it is necessary to tell. For the fairy tale says nothing whatever about it.

Sarukani kassen by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The third son of the crab, being a dullard, could not become anything but a crab. As he was crawling sideways along, there lay a rice ball. (...) And then, up in the top of a tall persimmon tree, a monkey picking off its lice — there is no need to tell the rest. In any case, this much is a fact: once he has fought the monkey, the crab is bound to be killed for the good of the world. A word to the readers of the world: most of you are crabs too.
