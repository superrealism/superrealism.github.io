About this work

A short story Arishima Takeo published in Fujin Kōron in 1921. The narrator recalls, with sharp remorse, a day in boyhood when he, his friend M and his small sister went swimming and were nearly drowned by a heavy swell. In the moment his sister was going under, he found himself wishing that he alone might be saved — a piece in which Arishima looks with an honest pen at the selfishness of a child and the sense of guilt it left.

Summary

At the start of September, when the towering swells of the late-summer heat come rolling in, all the city people who had come for sea-bathing had already shut up their villas and gone home, and not a soul remained on the beach. "I," thirteen, together with my friend M, fourteen, and my young sister, eleven, went out for one last swim, ignoring Grandmother's warning not to go, since the waves were growing rough. Forgetting the danger of the late-summer swells, the three of us grew absorbed in the game of "jumping the waves," until at last an enormous wave swept us up, and before we knew it we had been carried out beyond our depth. M, accustomed to swimming, was one thing, but neither "I" nor my sister could swim well at all. The three of us were pulled apart, and my sister, beginning to sink, called out to me with all her strength: "Brother, come — I'm going under — it hurts." My heart was drawn toward my sister, and yet — in that moment, I wanted, above all, to save my own life. For I knew full well that if I went to her, both of us would be swept away and drown together. Without so much as looking back, I swam for the shore in a desperate frenzy, and when at last I reached it, I ran up and down the beach, weeping, like a madman, crying for help. Before long M brought back a young man who happened to be passing by; the young man untied his sash and plunged straight into the sea, and saved my sister. After the danger had passed, Grandmother, ordinarily so gentle, turned on me sternly and scolded me. I, who had wished, even for a moment, to save myself alone, shrank into myself, body and soul. ...Years passed after that. Grandmother and M are both gone from this world now, and only my sister and I remain. Whenever the subject comes up, my sister still says, even now, "That one time, I truly resented you, Brother, from the bottom of my heart." An autobiographical piece in which Arishima, with an honest pen, looks steadily at a childhood selfishness and a sense of guilt that has never faded.

Characters

  • I　A thirteen-year-old boy. Faced with his sister nearly drowning in the late-summer swells, wishes to save himself alone, and carries a sense of guilt that never fades for the rest of his life
  • My sister　Eleven years old. Swept out to sea and beginning to sink, calls out to "me" for help, crying "Brother, come"
  • M　A fourteen-year-old friend. Accustomed to swimming, brings back a young man passing by to save my sister
  • Grandmother　Ordinarily gentle, but scolds "me" sternly for trying to save only myself

The opening (original text)

By the time those tall swells called doyō-nami come rolling in against the shore even without a breath of wind, the city people who had come for sea-bathing gradually begin shutting up their villas and going home. Until then, from morning till night, so many people would gather on the sand of the beach and in the water alike that, watching from the dunes, one might wonder in astonishment where on earth so vast a crowd of human beings could possibly have come from — but on that day, the third day of September, there was not a single soul to be seen anywhere along the beach, as far as the eye could reach. My friend M, my sister, and I decided to go for one last, farewell swim in the sea.

Oborekaketa kyōdai by Arishima Takeo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Before long, Grandmother turned sternly to face me and spoke these words. She was ordinarily such a gentle grandmother, but at her words then, I shrank, body and soul. That I had wished, even for a moment, to save myself alone — the thought felt as though my very heart were being pricked all over with needles. I could not even cry, and sat there stiffly before Grandmother, head bowed, unable to move. Outside, beyond the veranda, the hot sun beat mercilessly down upon the sand. For five or six years after that, we knew where that young man was living, but now I no longer know where he is or how he fares. Our dear grandmother is no longer of this world. My friend M was killed by someone, over some strange matter, and died. Only my sister and I are still living, even now. Whenever I bring up that story with her, my sister always says that, just that once, she truly resented me from the bottom of her heart. Even now, whenever I recall that moment when the waves rose high and my sister's figure vanished from sight, my heart begins to pound, and I am filled with a nameless dread.
