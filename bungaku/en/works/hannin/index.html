About this work

A short story following the flight of a young man who has committed murder, published in Chūō Kōron in January 1948. It carries a passage from Pushkin's "The Blizzard" as an epigraph and begins from the ordinary whisperings of love. For no better reason than wanting somewhere to meet, a young office worker turns a blade on his sister. That it never appears in the papers drives him further into a corner, and at the end of his flight he tries to write a poem — a crime story from Dazai's last years.

Summary

Tsuruta Keisuke, twenty-six, and Komori Hide, twenty-one, work at the same company and are in love. But the two of them have no money, and nowhere they can be alone together. When Tsuruta tries to rent a room at his elder sister's house in Mitaka and is refused, he flies into a rage, turns a blade on her, snatches several thousand yen from the cash box holding the day's takings, and flees. Wiping the blood from his face, he boards a train and spends time with a geisha named Suzume at a Nihonbashi teahouse. He buys three different evening papers and checks each one, but the incident appears in none of them. That it does not appear only drives him further into a corner. Convinced that the whole world is chasing after him alone, he boards a train bound for Osaka and, for the first time in his life, tries his hand at writing a poem.

Characters

  • Tsuruta Keisuke　A twenty-six-year-old company employee. Turns a blade on his sister after failing to find a place to meet his lover
  • Komori Hide　Tsuruta's lover. Twenty-one, works at the same company
  • The sister　Tsuruta's elder sister, who runs a shop in Mitaka. Refuses to lend her brother a room
  • Suzume　A geisha at a Nihonbashi teahouse. Keeps Tsuruta company while he is on the run

The opening (original text)

"I love you," said Burmin. "I love you with all my heart." Marya Gavrilovna flushed crimson and bowed her head still lower. — Pushkin, "The Blizzard" What utter banality. The talk of love between a young man and woman — no, even the talk of love between grown adults, come to that — heard from the outside, is trite and mawkish enough to raise gooseflesh over one's entire body. But this is no matter for laughter alone. A dreadful incident took place.

Hannin by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Tsuruta's murder never appeared in a single newspaper, but his suicide was reported, in a small corner of a Kansai paper. A young man named Kitagawa, employed at a certain firm in Kyoto, was startled and hurried to Ōtsu. After conferring with the people at the inn, he sent a telegram to Tsuruta's dormitory in Tokyo. From the dormitory, someone rushed to the home of the brother-in-law in Mitaka. The stitches had not yet been removed from the wound on the sister's left arm, which she carried in a white sling about her neck. Her husband, drunk as ever, said, "It was wrong of me to spend all this time searching around here and there just because I didn't want to make it public." The sister only wept, and came to understand that a young couple's foolish romance was, after all, nothing to make light of.

Hannin by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)
