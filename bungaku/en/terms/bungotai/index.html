What is Bungotai (Classical Written Style)?

Category
Style & Technique

A written style based on the grammar of the language of the Heian period (classical Japanese), as opposed to kōgotai, the colloquial style based on the spoken language. Using classical auxiliary verbs such as "keri" and "nari," it was long the standard for written Japanese. It includes several lineages: the elegant gabuntai (pseudo-classical prose) in the native Japanese vein, kanbun kundokutai modeled on the cadence of classical Chinese read in Japanese, and wakan konkōbun, which fused the two; in the Meiji era a plain "futsūbun," which blended these elements with kanbun kundokutai at its core, became the standard prose of newspapers, laws, and textbooks. Even in the 1880s and 1890s, when experiments in genbun'itchi had begun, the classical style still dominated literature: Mori Ōgai wrote "Maihime" ("The Dancing Girl," 1890) and the other stories of his so-called German trilogy in a fresh gabuntai, and made his translation of Andersen's "Sokkyō Shijin" ("The Improvisatore"), which he worked on from 1892 and published in book form in 1902, a celebrated rendering in flowing classical prose. Takayama Chogyū's "Takiguchi Nyūdō" (1894), too, was widely read for its ornate pseudo-classical style. Even after the genbun'itchi style had become the standard for fiction, the classical style lived on as the style of solemn historical narrative and of poetry, as when Kōda Rohan wrote "Unmei" ("Fate," 1919) in a dignified style steeped in classical Chinese. It also remained long in use in laws and official documents, but after the Constitution of Japan was promulgated in 1946, written in hiragana and the colloquial style, the colloquialization of legislation advanced, and the classical style all but vanished from everyday writing. It is still used today in tanka and haiku.
