About this work

A novella representative of Kyōka's middle period, published in Shinshōsetsu in January 1910. Set in Kuwana, a post town on the Tōkaidō, it draws the atonement of a nō actor who drove a man to his death in a contest of chanting, and the saving of a soul by the power of art. Known as the central work among his stories of the artistic disciplines, built on a deep knowledge of nō, and warmly praised by Tsubouchi Shōyō.

Summary

Onchi Kitahachi, born into the Onchi family of Noh masters, challenges Sōzan — an old man who, though an amateur, chants skillfully — to a contest of skill for amusement, shames him, and drives him to die of mortification. For this his uncle Genzaburō disowns him, and Kitahachi gives up the Noh and goes from province to province as a street performer, wandering with diseased lungs. At the post town of Kuwana Kitahachi meets O-Mie, Sōzan's orphaned daughter, who has become a geisha. By way of atonement for his old crime, Kitahachi breaks the prohibition upon him and in only seven days teaches O-Mie the dance and the chant. On a night when his uncle Genzaburō comes to Kuwana, O-Mie dances the "Tama no dan" from the play Ama, and Genzaburō and Henmi, who is with him, answer her with the chant and the hand drum. And outside the gate, drawn by the sound, Kitahachi too joined his voice and chanted.

Characters

  • Onchi Kitahachi　The hero, born into a family of Noh masters and yet disowned, who wanders from province to province
  • Sōzan　An old man who, though an amateur, chants skillfully. Shamed in the contest with Kitahachi, he dies of mortification
  • O-Mie　Sōzan's daughter. After her father's death she becomes a geisha, and is taught the dance and the chant by Kitahachi
  • Onchi Genzaburō　Kitahachi's uncle, who once disowned him

The opening (original text)

"The pillars of the shrine, set thick and firm as a Miyashige radish, are looked upon by the god of Atsuta, who is boiled with the turnip; the seven-league crossing lies rich with waves, and the ferry passes over without trouble, and in our joy at coming to Kuwana..." — so, as if reciting, he muttered to himself the opening lines of the fifth part of the Hizakurige, on the first watch of a night past the tenth of the eleventh month. The middle sky was cold and clear, and under a moon bright enough to make the stars perform cold-water ablutions, his shadow rose tall as he crossed the plank bridge of the crossing; and, watching the lights twinkling below him and the bare bones of the trees near and far, a traveler got down at the station of Kuwana.

The ending (original text)

"Lend me your back, Sōzan." And with the words, Onchi Kitahachi, looking wearily, took hold of the shapeless shadow of some large thing that had been crouching at his skirts for some time, and drew it under him and sat upon it, as one sits on a stool. The road ran white; along the hanging lanterns of the late hour, on this side and that, people gathered here and there in twos and threes, among them a masseur leaning on his staff.

Famous passages

The stout shrine pillars, thick as Miyashige daikon radishes, that the god of Atsuta, boiled soft as furofuki, watches over — the seven-league crossing rich with waves, and in the joy of arriving safely by ferry at Kuwana...

An opening that begins with a voice humming a passage from “Shanks' Mare.” Set in Kuwana, it opens a representative work of Kyōka's mature period, depicting the karma bound up with noh and the arts.
