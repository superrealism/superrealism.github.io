About this work

A collection of verse translations Nagai Kafū published in 1913. As its subtitle — a selection of modern French lyric poetry — indicates, it holds thirty-eight poems chosen from Baudelaire, Verlaine, Régnier, Samain, Mallarmé and other modern French poets. Kafū, back from America and France, gathered into one volume the translations he had published a few at a time in various magazines from about 1909. In contrast to the ornate seven-five metre of Ueda Bin's Kaichōon, he took a gloomy, prose-like measure with the punctuation pared down. It answers to the style of his own fiction, and opened a second current in the Japanese reception of symbolist poetry.

Summary

At the head stands Baudelaire's "The Joyous Dead" — a violent piece in which the poet digs himself a bottomless pit in the snail-crawling earth, hates wills and hates tombs, and invites the crows and the worms to question his rotting flesh. In "Spleen," which follows, the sky hangs down like a lid, the threads of the rain become the bars of a prison, and silent spiders spin their webs in the brain, while the bells cry out like departed souls and Anguish, planting its black flag, passes over the bowed head. "Darkness," "The Enemy" — every poem Kafū has chosen leans toward melancholy and languor and the beauty of ruin. Besides Baudelaire there are Verlaine, Henri de Régnier, Albert Samain, Mallarmé, Verhaeren — the poets of the end of the century who follow Baudelaire. Evening, an abandoned garden, a fountain, autumn light, a distant sea — into these blurred landscapes are sunk the memory of what is lost and the life of the senses. Kafū's diction sets Chinese and Japanese words hard against each other and holds down the exclamation, laying the words side by side, so that what comes forward is less the music of the original than a chain of images. The long poem placed at the end takes "Luxury" for its subject and heaps up one vision upon another — a lake of sulphur, the tears of martyrs, a dark tower where the king of the devils gathers his prayers — calling out, "The end of the world. Luxury, the last term of the turning of the spheres," "Saint of corruption. NOTRE-DAME of hell," and closing, "this Luxury, which may be likened to the imperishable, undying queen of this world." The longing for the decadence of Europe that runs through this one book remained, and did not vanish, in Kafū's prose thereafter.

The opening (original text)

The Joyous Dead Charles Baudelaire In a rich earth, full of snails, I wish to dig myself a deep pit, where I may spread out my old bones at leisure and sleep in oblivion like a shark in the wave. I hate wills, and I hate tombs; rather than beg one tear of the world, living, I would sooner invite the crows to bleed the ends of my filthy carcass.

Sangoshū by Nagai Kafū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

A convulsion that longs for wholeness. A banquet within the absolute. The end of the world. Luxury, the last term of the turning of the spheres. Saint of entreaty and of mercy. Saint of golden blood. Saint of greed and of pitilessness. Saint holy forever. City of flames. Drug of forgetting. Awl of black iron. Saint of corruption. NOTRE-DAME of hell. So would I liken it. Luxury, remote and mysterious. Ah, how great a thing. This Luxury, which may be likened to the imperishable, undying queen of this world.

Sangoshū by Nagai Kafū (Aozora Bunko)
