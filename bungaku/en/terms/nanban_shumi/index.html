What is Nanban Shumi (Nanban Exoticism)?

Category
Other

A literary fashion of the late Meiji and Taishō eras that found exotic charm and mystical beauty in the goods brought from Portugal and Spain between the late Muromachi and early Edo periods and in the Christian faith of that time (the "Nanban" culture), and took them up as subjects for poetry and fiction. In the summer of 1907, Yosano Hiroshi (Tekkan) traveled through Kyushu, including Amakusa and Nagasaki, with Kitahara Hakushū, Kinoshita Mokutarō, Yoshii Isamu, and Hirano Banri, and the travel sketch "Gosoku no Kutsu" ("Five Pairs of Shoes") they serialized in the "Tōkyō Niroku Shimbun" sparked interest in the remains of the Christian era. Hakushū sang of Christian legends and objects brought from the Nanban lands in dense, sensual, richly colored language in his first collection, "Jashūmon" ("Heretical Faith," 1909), and in the same year Mokutarō published the play "Nanbanji Monzen" in "Subaru," representing the Nanban taste of the young aesthetes of the Pan no Kai. In the Taishō era Akutagawa Ryūnosuke, beginning with "Tabako to Akuma" ("Tobacco and the Devil," 1916), wrote a series of stories known as his "Kirishitan-mono" (Christian pieces), culminating in "Hōkyōnin no Shi" ("The Martyr," 1918), which cites as its source a fictitious book purportedly printed by the Jesuits in Nagasaki; "Kamigami no Bishō" ("The Smile of the Gods"), which tells of the power of Japan's native soil to remake a foreign faith; and "Ogin" (both 1922), about a girl who chooses love for her parents over her faith. The term "Nanban bungaku" (Nanban literature), however, is another name for Kirishitan literature centered on the Jesuit mission press editions of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries (such as the Amakusa edition of "The Tale of the Heike"), and is distinguished from this modern fashion.
