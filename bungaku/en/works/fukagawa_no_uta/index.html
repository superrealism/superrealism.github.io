About this work

"I," back from abroad, boards a streetcar with nowhere to go, and, worn out by the crowding and the noise, escapes at last to Fukagawa. The first half sets down in detail the inside of a Meiji Tokyo streetcar; the second listens to the singing of a blind man performing utazawa below the stone wall of the Fudō temple. Anger at a city being filled up with flimsy Western-style buildings and longing for the Edo that has been lost run through each other, and the piece became the starting point of Kafū's work after his return to Japan.

Summary

On an afternoon past the twentieth of December, "I" get on a streetcar at Yotsuya Mitsuke. I have nowhere to go; I only like being shaken about by something in motion. Inside the car a woman with a baby on her back has trouble getting on and off, and the passengers look at one another with eyes that have nowhere to rest. Gazing at the view along the moat, passing through a crowded Ginza, meeting an uproar over a man who gets off without paying at the Kobikichō bank, and listening to two women in married-women's coiffure delighting in a chance meeting, I find the car stopped at Sakamoto Park by a failure of the power. Getting off with a transfer ticket in my hand, I look at a street of Western-style buildings that are all roof and window, of raw timber telegraph poles and advertisements in badly colored paint, and am filled with anger and with an irresistible desire to escape to the Fukagawa of long ago. Crossing the Eitai bridge while I think back to the watery Fukagawa of the days when one went over by ferry, I see beyond Tsukudajima an unfamiliar long bridge. Walking down the main street to the Fudō temple, I find at the foot of the stone steps an old man beating out an ahodara-kyō, and beside him a blind man with his hair grown wild, crouching with a shamisen. The utazawa he sings is in a phrasing so exact that no geisha of the Yamanote could produce it, and I am struck less by nostalgia than by respect. Looking back at the crimson sun going down in the west, I even think I should like to perish as Saitō Ryokuu did, that poet of longing for Edo. And yet I must go home. In my study at Ōkubo, under a portrait of Wagner, a volume of Nietzsche lies open and waiting for me.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, back from abroad and disappointed at how Tokyo has changed
  • The blind man　The man who crouches with a shamisen below the stone wall of the Fukagawa Fudō and sings utazawa

The opening (original text)

I got on a Tsukiji–Ryōgoku streetcar at Yotsuya Mitsuke. I had no particular place to go. Riding on something in motion, boat or carriage, and being shaken about, gives me a kind of pleasure; it became a curious habit of mine without my noticing it, on the elevated railway of New York, on the top of the omnibuses of Paris, on the river boats of the Seine. It is a fine day. It is warm. No wind blows. Since it is past the twentieth of December already, the main street of Kōjimachi along which the car runs is hung with pine and bamboo decorations, ground-cherry lanterns, curtains, tall standards, banners and flags of every kind, all of them out of all keeping with the dirty tiled roofs and the raw new boards of the newly built houses; and the sunlight after three o'clock, slanting and dazzling, makes the confusion look crueler still. Advertising bands out of tune are striking up noisily here and there. There are a great many people about.

The ending (original text)

"The cares of a night we meet in the small hours —" he sings on. I wanted to stand there forever and forever, bathed in this Fukagawa sunset as the day went down, below the stone wall of the main hall in its holy ground of superstition, listening to the short songs of the utazawa. To cross the Eitai bridge and go home seemed more than I could bear. I felt that I would rather perish, like Saitō Ryokuu, the poet of longing for Edo that Meiji brought forth. Ah, and yet I must go home after all. That is my fate. Across the river, over the canals, up the slope and far away, in the shade of the woods of Ōkubo, on the desk of my study, under the portrait of Wagner a volume of Nietzsche's "Zarathustra" lies open and waiting for me….
