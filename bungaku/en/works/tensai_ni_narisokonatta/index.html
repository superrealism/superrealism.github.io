About this work

When he tells people that he was hit by a car and cracked his skull, most of them put on a face of condolence. Only one man let out a long sigh of pure envy — a short piece in which Ango sets down, with a great deal of comedy, the eccentricities of a character modelled on the poet Hishiyama Shūzō. From the story of sitting the entrance examination for a newspaper company without ever having read a newspaper, to the last scene in which he opens the shutters saying he will show him the moon, something sad remains inside the laughter.

Summary

When he was a student at Tōyō University he was knocked down by a motorcar in front of the gate in the middle of the year's examinations. He flew two or three ken into the air, turned a somersault on the way and came down, striking the left side of his head against the concrete so that his skull was cracked, and for two years afterward he went on taking medicine. He was gloomy with worrying whether he would become an invalid — and when he tells this story most people put on much the same face and say something like "how very sad for you"; but there was one man, and one only, who was struck dumb like a pigeon hit by a peashooter, and then let out a long sigh of envy. He was a poet named Hishiyama Shūzō. ... When this poet came out of the school of foreign languages and went to sit the entrance examination for the Asahi Shimbun, he had in all his student days never once read a newspaper properly, and knew nothing at all outside his study and his school. Since the Manchurian Incident had just broken out and women stood in the streets with thousand-stitch belts, and the whole town was in an uproar with records of the Three Human Bullets, the master had at least gathered that a war seemed to be going on somewhere near at hand. From then on the anecdotes of this unworldly poet are told one after another. In the last scene the master says he will show them the moon, and opens the shutters. But there is no moon. It is pitch dark. He gropes to the left and the right, and searches at last even under the floor of the veranda; but the moon is still not there.

Characters

  • I　A student whose skull was cracked when a motorcar knocked him down
  • Hishiyama Shūzō　The poet who envied him that story

The opening (original text)

When I was a student at Tōyō University, in the middle of the year's examinations, I was once knocked down by a motorcar as I got off the tram in front of the gate. Since my reflexes are fairly well developed, I flew two or three ken into the air and turned a somersault on the way and came down; but even so I struck the left side of my head against the concrete.

The ending (original text)

Saying so, he opened the shutters. And there was no moon. It was pitch dark. He groped to the left and the right, and at last the master searched even under the floor of the veranda. Still the moon was not there. "Ah, the moon is not out today. Look at the moon for me another time." So the master murmured sadly, and came quietly back.
