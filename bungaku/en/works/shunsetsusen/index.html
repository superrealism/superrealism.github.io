About this work

A short story published in Bungei Sensen in 1926. A seaman whose leg has been crushed in an accident aboard is put ashore under discipline with neither treatment nor compensation, and the story draws the few dozen minutes at dawn in which he goes to the maritime office accompanied by the second mate. A great company with a hundred million yen of capital, and one labourer carrying a single wicker trunk. As the man throws the trunk, now useless, into the canal from the bridge, a dredger begins to raise steam in the morning light. The steamer of the setting bears the same name, Manjumaru, as the ship in The People Who Live on the Sea.

Summary

The old wicker trunk 'I' carried was stuffed full of things 'no longer of any use,' like the entrails of a dead man. My left ankle had been crushed in a shipboard accident, and left with nothing more than a wad of cotton stuffed against it, the wound had turned septic. Even so, the company paid neither medical costs nor injury compensation, and only saw to the paperwork for my disciplinary discharge. 'If you just plead your case, even the captain will hand over a bit of condolence money,' the second mate said soothingly, and with him 'I' set off, snapping back at him the whole way, for the maritime affairs office. Unable to bear the pain that shot through me with every step, I set the trunk down on a bridge over the canal and sat. Below, a dredger sat moored, coal smoke rising from a charcoal brazier on its deck. At last, 'I' hurled the trunk into the canal with all my strength — 'I just threw in a headless corpse,' I thought. When I turned, the second mate had vanished. And of course there was no novelist's heroine waiting to sweep me up in her car. The dawn wind was crisp and fresh; the dredger's boatman and his wife were eating breakfast, and the fireman called out, laughing, 'Have a good time last night?' Steam rose, a roar tore through the morning, and the operator and the fireman alike were swallowed up into the machine. If you laze about you can't eat, so you work. And if you get hurt working, and can no longer work, you can't eat either — 'I' shouldered one heavy plan in place of the trunk, and turned back toward the pier.

Characters

  • I　A sailor whose foot was crushed aboard the Manju Maru, discharged in disgrace without any compensation
  • The second mate　The officer who accompanies 'me,' urging a quiet settlement on behalf of the company and the captain
  • The dredger's crew　Men who greet the morning on the canal and set to work, raising steam

The opening (original text)

I was carrying a single wicker trunk. Inside that trunk, like the entrails of a dead man, were packed only things 'no longer of any use.' The shin-piece alone of a pair of rubber boots. An English translation of the Arabian Nights, its cover and more than half its pages gone, its type packed as fine as millet seed. A knitted cap, the kind French sailors wear, with the pompom torn off.

Shunsetsusen by Hayama Yoshiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The operator gripped the controls. The stillness broke, and a roar tore through the morning. The operator and the fireman alike, their faces gone sharp and intent, were swallowed up into the machine. — If you laze about you can't eat. So you work. And if you get hurt working, and can no longer work, you can't eat either! — I turned back toward the pier, shouldering one heavy plan in place of the trunk, my foot throbbing like a broken tooth.
