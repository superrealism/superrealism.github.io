What is Botsurisō Debate?

Category
Other

A debate waged between 1891 and the following year by Tsubouchi Shōyō and Mori Ōgai over whether art requires ideals. In beginning a serialized commentary on Shakespeare's plays in "Waseda Bungaku," Shōyō set out a position of wishing to assess the work as it was, without importing his own ideals or doctrine, calling this stance "botsurisō" ("the absence of ideals"). Against this view — that Shakespeare's plays presented phenomena as they were without directly setting forth any ideal — Ōgai, writing in the magazine he had himself founded, "Shigarami Zōshi," wielded the aesthetics of the German philosopher Hartmann to argue that art cannot do without ideals, and that criticism must judge value in the light of an idea of beauty. Shōyō responded in turn in the pages of "Waseda Bungaku," and the exchange across the two magazines ran on for more than a year. It ended inconclusively, but as the first debate to ground criticism in theory, rather than simply relaying impressions of a work, it is regarded as the starting point of modern Japanese literary criticism. Shōyō, notably, turned away from creative writing after this debate and devoted himself instead to translating Shakespeare and reforming the theater.
