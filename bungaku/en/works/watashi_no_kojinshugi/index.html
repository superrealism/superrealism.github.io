About this work

A record of the lecture Sōseki gave to the Hōjinkai at the Gakushūin in November 1914, and widely read as the plainest and frankest statement of his late thought. He describes his young days, absorbed in English literature yet suffering emptiness and unease, and how in the loneliness of his time in London he seized on the phrase "self-centred" — meaning to stand on one's own ground — and from there explains to the students what individualism is: developing one's own character while respecting the character of others. Still read as a guide to how to live as a modern Japanese.

Summary

The record of a lecture Natsume Sōseki delivered to the Hōjinkai society of the Gakushūin in November 1914 (Taishō 3). Sōseki begins by speaking frankly of his own wanderings: the years of his youth when, for all his study of English literature, he suffered an emptiness and unease, as though he "did not hold a single card in his hand"; and then, in the loneliness of his years of study in London, how he came at last upon a single phrase, jiko hon'i, "the self as one's own ground" — the conviction that a man must live by his own lights and not on things borrowed from others — and how that conviction saved him and lit the road he was to take. From there Sōseki turns to the young men before him and tells them what individualism is. It is to develop one's own individuality and at the same time to respect the individuality of others; it is, in the use of one's wealth and one's power, to be conscious of the duty and the responsibility that come with them. An individualism founded on ethics, so like egoism and yet wholly unlike it, is the ground of individual happiness. He goes on to touch upon its relation to the nationalism then in full flood, arguing that in a time when the state is at peace it is individualism, with its high sense of moral duty, that ought to be given the greater weight. Plainspoken and candid, backed at every turn by his own experience, it is a famous lecture on the independence and the ethics of the modern person, and is read to this day.

The opening (original text)

— Delivered before the Hōjinkai of the Gakushūin, the twenty-fifth of November, Taishō 3 — Today is the first time I have ever set foot inside this thing called the Gakushūin. I had long had some notion, it is true, that the Gakushūin must lie somewhere about this quarter, but I did not know it for certain. As for coming inside, today is of course my first time.

My Individualism by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Since I had been so good as to be invited, I came out here today and did what I could to set forth the necessity of individualism to you, who are to lead lives as individuals so far as it is possible to do so. I did it because I think it may be of some use to you after you have gone out into the world. And if, without my taking all that trouble, my real meaning has been fully grasped by you, then no satisfaction of mine could go beyond that. As I am running on too long, I shall beg your leave and stop here.

Famous passages

Once I had this phrase, “self-centeredness,” firmly in my own grasp, I became a much stronger man.

A line from a lecture given at the Peers' School. Sōseki, who had reached an impasse measuring literature by Western standards, speaks of the “self-centeredness” he grasped only after long anguish while studying abroad. It is the very core of a lecture that expounds individualism in modern Japan.

First, if you wish to achieve the development of your own individuality, you must at the same time respect the individuality of others.

The first of three principles summarizing the lecture. By joining the establishment of the self to respect for others, these words distinguish Sōseki's individualism from selfishness.
