About this work

A rough but honest earth god and a refined but lying fox contend over a beautiful birch tree — a piece leaving a particularly bitter aftertaste even among Miyazawa Kenji's children's stories. The ending, in which the earth god, mad with jealousy, kills the fox and then breaks down weeping at what he finds in its breast, is unforgettable.

Summary

At the northern edge of Ippongi Field, on a small rise of ground, stood a beautiful birch tree, glossy black-trunked and feminine in form. This tree had two friends: one was the earth god who lived in the marshland, and the other was a brown fox who came from the south. If anything, the birch tree preferred the fox. The earth god, though called a god, was thoroughly uncouth — his hair unkempt, his eyes red, always barefoot, his nails black and long. The fox, by contrast, had a most refined manner, and rarely angered anyone. The fox would visit the birch tree and tell her stories of telescopes, of poetry collections, of Italian sculpture — but all of it was lies, for the fox did not own a single book. The earth god, knowing this, could not help but be galled that the birch tree's heart leaned toward the fox. Tormented by the thought of why he, a god, should feel jealousy over a mere fox, he could not master his own heart, and raged. On a certain moonlit night, the earth god at last cornered the fox, seized him as he tried to flee, and, with all his strength, wrung his neck and killed him. The fox, his mouth pursed as though in the faintest smile, hung limp, his head drooping, in the earth god's hands. The earth god flung the fox's body to the ground, trampled it four or five times, and then leapt into the fox's burrow. Inside it was hollow and dark, nothing there but neatly packed red clay. Coming back out, the earth god reached his hand into the pocket of the dead fox's raincoat — and found inside it two brown timothy-grass seed heads. The earth god, his mouth still twisted wide open, burst into a strange, enormous wail. His tears fell upon the fox like rain, and the fox, his head hanging ever more limply, lay dead with that same faint, wan smile upon his face.

The opening (original text)

At the northern edge of Ippongi Field there was a place that rose up a little, mounded higher than the rest. Foxtail grass grew there thick, and in the very middle of it stood a single beautiful, feminine birch tree. It was not a very large tree, but its trunk gleamed glossy black, and its branches spread out beautifully, bearing white blossoms like clouds in May, and in autumn letting fall leaves of gold, crimson, and every other color.

Tsuchigami to kitsune by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Then he reached his hand into the pocket of the raincoat worn by the fox's corpse, which lay slumped there. Inside that pocket were two brown timothy-grass seed heads. The earth god, his mouth still hanging open from before, burst out into a strange, enormous wail. His tears fell upon the fox like rain, and the fox, his head hanging ever more limply, lay dead with that same faint, wan smile upon his face.
