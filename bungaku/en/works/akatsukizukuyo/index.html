About this work

A short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the magazine Miyako no Hana in February 1893. A beautiful young lady who refuses every offer of marriage and lives alone is approached by a literary student who disguises himself as a gardener to learn her reason. Around the secret of her birth, which she at last discloses — the tragedy of a mother who died in a forbidden love across ranks — it draws an inherited fate and a resignation, laid over a parting under the dawn moon.

Summary

Kayama Hitoe, the daughter of the noble Kayama family with an estate along the Edogawa River, is a young woman of twenty of matchless beauty, who has turned down every marriage proposal and insists on living alone — earning her a reputation in society as an eccentric. Morino Satoshi, a student of literature lodging in Ushigome, wanting above all to know the reason for her solitary life, hides his true identity and takes work as the Kayama family's gardener, under the name 'Gosuke,' in order to get close to her. Able only to gaze up at the young lady across the garden, Satoshi wins over her nine-year-old younger brother Jin'nosuke, who adores his elder sister, and uses the boy as a messenger, delivering love letters to her disguised as 'poems.' In time Satoshi learns why Hitoe's stepmother treats her so coldly: Hitoe is the child born of a forbidden love between a young lady who was the sister of a viscount and Rokuzō, a mounted attendant of far lower rank than herself, a union across an unbridgeable difference in status. When the affair came to light before the viscount, Hitoe's brother, Rokuzō was driven from the household, and the young lady, pregnant and despairing of the world, died giving birth to Hitoe; Rokuzō too, wrapping himself in a scarlet crepe kimono left behind by his beloved, threw himself into the Ōkawa River. Knowing the shame of her birth, Hitoe has borne the stain on her family name alone, and has lived in solitude ever since. As the young lady prepares to leave the world behind and move to a villa in Kamakura, Satoshi steals into the house the night before her departure and confesses his feelings. Hitoe, as a single act of confession she will make but once in her life, tells him the truth of her birth, and beneath the moon at dawn, they part — you to the capital, and I to Kamakura — each going their separate way.

Characters

  • Kayama Hitoe　The daughter of the noble Kayama family. A young woman of matchless beauty who refuses every marriage proposal and lives alone, carrying the secret of her birth as the child of a mother who died for a love across an unbridgeable difference in status
  • Morino Satoshi　A student of literature lodging in Ushigome. Twenty-four years old. Disguises himself as the gardener "Gosuke" to get close to the young lady
  • Jin'nosuke　The nine-year-old second son of the Kayama family. Adores his elder sister Hitoe, and serves as the go-between carrying letters from Satoshi to her
  • Rokuzō　Hitoe's true father, a mounted attendant of low rank. After a forbidden love with the young lady of the house, across an unbridgeable gap in status, he throws himself into the Ōkawa River clutching a keepsake left by his beloved

The opening (original text)

Cherry blossoms scented with plum, blooming, it is said, even on willow branches — merely to hear tell of such a thing is enough to stir the heart, and so too the rumor of her cruel, solitary life stirred the hearts of refined young men, until some found themselves, like reflections floating on a mountain well, falling into a kind of love for her they had never even seen. The house known for its blossoms, the Kayama family — one need not even read the Junior Third Rank inscribed upon its gate to know that theirs was a name well known among the noble houses — stood along the clear-flowing western bank of the Edogawa River, its buildings mixing Japanese and Western style, not the very height of beauty, but—

The ending (original text)

That my whole life should be so sorrowful, that I should carry a grief I can confess to no one — this too is only because of a shameful love. The story of the parents I never truly had is a thing I too have kept secret, unfit to be told; even my own father, let alone my mother, hid it from the world as a shame upon our house, and I do not tell you this so that you might spread it further, but as a confession I will make only once in my life — hear me now, hear the truth of this unhappy birth — and there, with tears spent to the very last, she tells him everything, as the night, which may as well have been a dream, draws toward the hour of dawn in spring, and the cries of birds are heard in the sky, and everything grows hurried and urgent. You to the capital, I to Kamakura — parted from each other, when shall we ever meet again? Seeing here an example of the inevitable parting that comes to all things, she thinks that love is safest kept to oneself alone, and says: let nothing more be said, let nothing more be thought, and do not ask it of me again — and so, beneath the moon at dawn, their shadows part ways. What has become of the young lady since, and what of Satoshi — no one can say; only that, gazing up into the sky at the fading moon of early dawn, that indifferent keepsake, one of them may have cried out, "Nothing but the dawn" — though whether it was truly cried at all, no one can say.
