About this work

"Of the old Musashino, traces now remain only in Iruma district" — taking a line from an old map as his cue, the author walks the coppice woods still left on the edge of Tokyo. Light and wind through the seasons, the stir of the fallen leaves, the character of the small paths: all recorded minutely, with passages from his own diary worked in. Rather than famous sites, it finds a new beauty in the ordinary nature of Musashino itself, and Kunikida Doppo's best-known essay has had a lasting effect on how Japanese see landscape.

Summary

The author conceives a wish to learn what has become of Musashino — a place by then imagined only in paintings and poems — and where any of it still remains. Quoting a line about an old battlefield inscribed on an old map, and the scene of a wood of fallen leaves in Turgenev's "The Tryst," he opens the diary he kept while living out near Shibuya. What is set down there is nothing in the way of famous sites or historic ruins. It is the stir of the oak woods, the sound of a passing shower, morning mist and evening sun, moonlight, the songs of small birds, and the voices of people coming and going along the paths between the trees — the plain scenery of a coppice wood itself, shifting with the four seasons and with morning and evening. Here the author discovers a new beauty in the woods of falling leaves, which the Japanese, seeing beauty in pine groves alone, had passed over without a glance. He walks from wood to wood and field to field with no destination, takes his pleasure in losing the way, and listens to the sun going down deep among the trees and to a steam whistle sounding far beyond the fields. Catching, with delicate observation and lyric feeling, the quiet and freshly seen character of nature that lies along the border of city and country, the work is famous as a monument that brought "the discovery of landscape" to modern Japanese prose.

The opening (original text)

"Of the old Musashino, traces now remain only in Iruma district" — so I once saw it written on a map made in the Bunsei years. And on that map, in Iruma district, I read this written in: "Kotesashigahara and Kumegawa are an old battlefield. The Taiheiki records that on the eleventh day of the fifth month of the third year of Genkō the Minamoto and the Taira fought at Kotesashigahara, closing more than thirty times in the course of a single day, and that at nightfall the Taira drew back three ri and made camp at Kumegawa, and that at daybreak the Minamoto bore down upon the camp at Kumegawa; this must be hereabouts." It came to me that the little of Musashino that survives must surely lie somewhere about that old battlefield, and I have been meaning to go and see it, though I have not gone yet, and I have my doubts whether things are in fact still as they were. In any case, it cannot be my wish alone to see the Musashino one imagines only in paintings and poems, if only the shadow of it. What, then, has become of that Musashino now? It was a year ago that the desire rose in me to answer the question in detail and satisfy myself, and the desire has only grown larger since. But can such a wish be accomplished by my own powers? I do not say it cannot be done. I believe it is not easy — and by that much do I feel the interest of the Musashino of today. There are, I imagine, not a few who feel as I do.

Musashino by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Black soot is rising from the oil lamps, and among them a dozen or more people from the village and from the town run about shouting. Vegetables of every kind lie piled here and there. This is a small vegetable market, a small auction ground. There are houses that go to bed the moment the sun is down, and others that keep the lamplight showing on the paper doors of the shop until two in the morning. Behind the barber's is a farmhouse, and the lowing of the cow carries out into the road; next door to the wine shop lives the old man who sells nattō, and every morning early he sets off toward the city calling nattō, nattō in his hoarse voice. The short summer night is hardly over before the carts begin to pass. The rumbling and clattering never stops. By nine or ten the cicadas start up in the high branches that can be seen from the road, and it grows hotter and hotter. Sand and dust, driven up by the hooves of horses and the wheels of carts, whirl into the empty air. Swarms of flies cross the road, flying from house to house and from horse to horse. And still the noon gun sounds faintly, and somewhere far off beyond the sky of the city comes the sound of a steam whistle.

Musashino by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

“The vestiges of Musashino now remain, but faintly, in Iruma District” — so I once read on a map made in the Bunsei era.

An opening that takes as its clue a line inscribed on an Edo-period map. It begins a landmark of modern Japanese essay writing, in which the author walks out on his own two feet to confirm the vanishing face of Musashino.

It seems that, until now, the Japanese have known little of the beauty of deciduous forests, oak and its kin.

A passage that objects to a Japanese literary tradition that had loved only pine forests. It was the first to put into words the beauty of the mixed woodlands of Musashino.

One who strolls in Musashino must not mind losing the way. Whichever path you take, if you go where your feet lead, there will always be something to see, to hear, and to feel.

A passage arguing that the beauty of Musashino is found by walking its paths without aim. It shows Doppo's new eye, which found beauty not in famous sights but in the woods and field paths themselves.
