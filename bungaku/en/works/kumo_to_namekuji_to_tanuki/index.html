About this work

The spider, the slug and the badger were all fine champions, though nobody knew what they were competing in. An early children's story by Miyazawa Kenji, setting side by side how each of them devoured and deceived and grew fat, and the end that came to each. Beneath an exaggeration and an absurdity that verge on the cruel lies a thoroughgoing warning against greed.

Summary

The spider, the silver slug, and the tanuki who had never once washed his face — all three were fine champions, though of just what contest, the narrator does not rightly know. So begins this tale, which tells each of their stories in turn. The red, long-legged spider is blown to an oak tree at the edge of the forest, and, enduring his hunger, spins a small web. He gorges on the mosquitoes he catches, spreads his web wider and wider by degrees, and at last even catches his own companions, his wife, and his children in it, growing fatter and fatter all the while. The silver slug, with courteous and ingratiating manners, deceives those around him, calling frogs and lizards his "dear friends" even as he dissolves and swallows their bodies. The tanuki, chanting "raw cat, raw cat" like a prayer, preaches the importance of patience and forbearance even as he devours rabbits and wolves, one bite after another. Each of the three believes, all the while, that he alone is in the right. And in the end, all three die. The spider melts away and dissolves; the slug is gulped down in a single bite; and the tanuki swells up round as a globe, mud and water pooling inside his body, until he turns entirely black and, feverish and delirious, cries out in agony before at last burning up and dying. So it seems, after all, that the three of them had been running a marathon bound for hell, every one of them.

The opening (original text)

The spider, and the silver slug, and the tanuki who had never once washed his face — all three were fine champions. But just what they were champions of, I don't rightly know. The wildcat told me that the three of them were competing in dead earnest, in a contest of the most serious kind.

The ending (original text)

And turning entirely black, feverish and delirious, he cried, "Ooh, how frightening, how frightening. I've been running a marathon bound for hell. Ooh, how it hurts" — and at last he burned up and died. So it seems, after all, that the three of them had been running a marathon bound for hell, every one of them.
