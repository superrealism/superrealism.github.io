About this work

A novel serialized in the Asahi Shimbun from June to September 1915, and the only avowedly autobiographical one Sōseki wrote. Using his own life around the time of I Am a Cat, it follows Kenzō, a scholar back from studying abroad, and the entanglement over money with Shimada, the foster father who raised him as a child. The closing line — that almost nothing in this world is ever settled — holds Sōseki's sense of a life that will not come out even.

Summary

Back from his studies abroad and set up in a house deep in Komagome, Kenzō teaches at the university and is driven by his writing. One day he passes in the street his foster father Shimada, with whom he broke off relations fifteen or sixteen years before. As a small child Kenzō was raised as the adopted son of the Shimadas, and even after he was returned to his own family the money side of it dragged on. Before long Shimada begins coming to the house and asking for money again and again. Kenzō loathes it and cannot bring himself to cut the man off, and hands over money out of his own scant income. O-Tsune, his former foster mother, his sister and her husband, and even his wife's father all count on his purse, each for reasons of their own. Between him and his wife O-Sumi the misunderstandings pile up, and Kenzō wears his nerves away between his impatience over his work and his obligations to his relatives. In the end, through the good offices of his wife's family, he pays a hundred yen as a final settlement, and on paper his connection with Shimada is "settled." To his relieved wife Kenzō mutters bitterly that hardly anything in this world is ever settled — that what has once happened goes on for ever.

Characters

  • Kenzō　A scholar home from abroad, wearing away his nerves between his old tie with his foster father and the strain of making a living
  • O-Sumi　Kenzō's wife, who keeps the household going even though she and her husband do not understand each other
  • Shimada　Kenzō's former foster father, who keeps turning up to ask for money long after the tie was broken
  • O-Tsune　Shimada's former wife and Kenzō's former foster mother, who also comes to rely on him

The opening (original text)

How many years was it since he had left Tokyo, when Kenzō came home from far away and set up house deep in Komagome? In the strangeness of treading his native soil he felt even a kind of loneliness. About his body there still clung the smell of the distant country he had newly left behind him. He disliked it. He must shake that smell off, he thought, at the earliest possible day. And he never noticed the pride and satisfaction that lay hidden within that very smell.

Michikusa by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Hardly anything in this world is ever settled. What has once happened goes on for ever. It only changes into all sorts of shapes, so that neither other people nor oneself can make it out any longer." Kenzō's tone was bitter, as though he were spitting the words out. His wife said nothing and picked up the baby. "There's a good child, there's a good child. We can't make out a word of what your father says, can we?" And saying it over and over, she kissed the red cheeks again and again.

Michikusa by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Hardly anything in this world ever gets settled. Once a thing has happened, it goes on forever. It's just that it changes into all sorts of shapes, so that neither other people nor you yourself can recognize it anymore.

Kenzō's reply at the end of the novel to his wife, who is relieved that the matter with his former foster father is settled. The bitter view of life in this autobiographical novel, drawn from Sōseki's own experience, is distilled into this remark.
