About this work

Chekhov's last play, finished in the year of his death (first performed 1904), translated by Jinzai Kiyoshi, known for his renderings of Russian literature. Before an orchard and a house that are about to be put up for auction, it shows a family of declining landowners who let the days pass without taking a single step, and Lopakhin, a merchant risen from a serf's son, who buys the land. The author himself called it a comedy, but ever since the first performance it has also been read as an elegy for a class that is passing. The sound of a breaking string at the close, and the sound of an axe in the garden, are famous as symbolic moments of modern theater.

Summary

At dawn in May, Ranevskaya, the mistress of the estate, comes home from Paris after five years. Her daughter Anya, her adopted daughter Varya, her brother Gayev, and Lopakhin, a merchant who as a child was the son of a serf on the estate, are there to meet her. The cherry orchard, whose name was once known all over the country, has been taken as security for debt, and in August it is to be sold at auction with the house. Lopakhin offers the practical proposal of cutting down the orchard and letting the land out for summer cottages, at an income of twenty-five thousand a year; but neither Ranevskaya nor Gayev will hear of it — to cut down the orchard their ancestors left them is unthinkable. Unable to give up the habits of extravagance bred into them, they count on money from an aunt, or say that a general is sure to lend them something, and let the days go by without doing anything at all. The student Trofimov tells Anya that from every single tree in the garden the faces of serfs are looking out at her. On the day of the auction a ball is held at the house, and Lopakhin comes back to announce that it is he who has bought the cherry orchard. The family scatter and leave the house, and Firs, the old servant of eighty-seven, is left behind in the locked house without anyone noticing.

Characters

  • Ranevskaya　The mistress of the estate, come home from Paris, who cannot bring herself to give up the cherry orchard
  • Gayev　Her brother. He does nothing but recite billiard phrases and takes no step at all
  • Anya　Ranevskaya's daughter, seventeen years old
  • Lopakhin　A merchant risen from a serf's son, who buys the cherry orchard
  • Firs　The old servant of eighty-seven, left behind in the house at the last

The opening (original text)

Act One A room which is still called the nursery. One of the doors leads to Anya's room. Daybreak; the sun will soon be up. It is May already and the cherry trees are in blossom, but the garden is cold: there is a frost in the early morning. The windows of the room are all shut. Enter Dunyasha with a candle, and Lopakhin with a book in his hand. LOPAKHIN. The train has come in at last, thank God. What is the time? DUNYASHA. Nearly two. (Blows out the candle.) It is light already. LOPAKHIN. And how late was the train, then? Two hours at the very least. (Yawns and stretches.) A fine fellow I am — what a fool I have made of myself! I came here on purpose to meet them at the station, and then went and overslept…. Fell asleep sitting up. It is annoying.

The ending (original text)

…But it seems the master went without his fur coat, and traveled in nothing but an overcoat. … (Sighs anxiously.) I never looked to it…. Young and green, that's what they are! (Mutters something that cannot be made out.) My life has gone by as though I had never lived at all…. (Lies down.) I'll lie down a while…. You've no strength left in you, nothing left, nothing…. Ah, you … good-for-nothing!… (Lies motionless.) A distant sound is heard, as though out of the sky: the sound of a breaking string, dying away, mournful. Silence falls, and there is nothing to be heard but the stroke of an axe on a tree, far off in the orchard. — Curtain —
