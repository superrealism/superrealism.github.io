About this work

A short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the magazine Musashino in July 1892. The daughter of a wealthy house and her faithful maid, who is also her foster sister, love the same man without knowing it. The maid's devotion in giving up her own love to act as go-between for the mistress she is indebted to, and the two women's hearts grieving together, unrewarded, in the early-summer rains, drawn in a moist and quiet feeling.

Summary

Yūko, daughter of the wealthy Nashimoto family, is a gentle, intelligent girl of nineteen. She and her maid Oyae, one year her junior and also her milk-sister, share a closeness that surpasses even that of true sisters. Worried by Yūko's brooding, Oyae presses her to confess the cause, and Yūko admits that she has fallen desperately in love with a handsome man called 'Lord Mitsuuji' — Sugihara Saburō, a close acquaintance of her father — and begs Oyae, saying she would as soon die as have her feelings go unanswered, to act as go-between. But unknown to Yūko, Saburō is none other than Oyae's own childhood sweetheart, the very man she came up to Tokyo searching for after he fell into poverty and left his home province. Reunited with Yūko by pure chance after being taken in by the Nashimoto family, Oyae, owing her life to Yūko's kindness, resolves to bury her own love and give herself wholly to serving as go-between. She delivers Yūko's letter to Saburō, but his only reply is a single poem-slip whose meaning cannot be fathomed, and before long all word from him ceases entirely. Whether to resent him or to go on waiting — through the endless rains of the fifth month, at their villa, both Yūko and Oyae sink into their own separate, unanswered griefs. At last Yūko, giving up on her love, clings to Oyae's knees and weeps, and the two share their sorrow together. Led out into the fields after the rain clears, the two watch a wandering monk knocking at a gate beneath the moon, and cry out together as one.

Characters

  • Yūko　The daughter of the Nashimoto family. A gentle, intelligent nineteen-year-old girl. Falls in love with the handsome Sugihara Saburō, a close acquaintance of her father, and asks her maid Oyae to act as go-between
  • Oyae　Yūko's maid and milk-sister. One year younger than Yūko. Though Saburō is in truth the childhood sweetheart she herself came to the city searching for, she casts aside her own love to serve faithfully as go-between, out of gratitude to Yūko
  • Sugihara Saburō　The handsome man both Yūko and Oyae love. A close acquaintance of Yūko's father. Cuts off contact after leaving behind only an ambiguous poem-slip

The opening (original text)

Two irises bloom by the pond, mirrored double in the water — one a pale violet, one deep, or perhaps not violet at all but white; their sashes tied the same way, their high formal coiffures dressed alike, their long-sleeved cherry-blossom patterns restrained yet rich in color, with no difference of rank between them and no barrier between their hearts — two who might almost be embarrassed to call each other sisters, so alike are they in devotion, needing each other as fish need water: without you, what would become of me; no, it is you who matters most — trusting and trusted in turn, like wisteria clusters hanging from a pine bough. Is there another bond like this, between a mistress and her maid? Of the wealthy Nashimoto family there was a daughter called Yūko, fine of countenance, fair-skinned, with a slender face and brows like distant mountains veiled in mist — to compare her to a flower would be trite, and yet: a pale plum blossom of the second month, or perhaps light snow, something not too deeply colored, wearing faint white powder and iridescent lip rouge that people praised as tasteful. Though nineteen, raised deep within the house like a hothouse flower, not knowing the world's winds, yet the sound of wind in the pines reached her all the same, and she passed the long spring days, which felt all too short, at her koto — how peaceful must her heart have been. It was the season when the blossoms fall at the end of the third month; a morning storm invited them down, and the garden turned to a snowstorm of white — and yet her sleeves were not cold, for it was the season of butterfly wings, of clear skies after rain and glistening dew at the veranda's edge. There, lightly holding the pet cat Tama and loosening its collar to retie it, was the maid Oyae, one year younger than Yūko but her equal in charm, with a single dimple — for whose sake did she turn those alluring eyes and smile so? — she let the cat go, glanced back, knit her brow, then laughed and called out, "Young mistress, do come look — how funny this is," and, amused, drew Yūko out to see. "Oyae, why do you find such a thing amusing? To me it is nothing at all," Yūko said, looking troubled, and without so much as a glance at the kitten at play, stared vacantly out at the garden. "Are you unwell again today, my lady?" "No, it is nothing like that, but—" and though pressed further, whatever lay in her heart she seemed unwilling to reveal, and she changed the subject instead. "Oyae, I have something to ask you. Between the flowers and the birds of spring, which do you prefer?" "What a strange thing to ask. It would depend on the person, but for my part, I find the returning wild geese more moving." "How odd, that you should prefer such heartless birds, abandoning the capital's spring to fly away." "If it comes to being moved — surely the heart of a blossom hidden deep in the mountains is moved too, wondering if its fate is only to bloom and scatter, unknown to the world, unknown to anyone. Even tempted by the very same storm as other blossoms, if it could bloom at the home of someone it loved, and be loved by that person in turn, it would have no regret even in scattering. But with no word of it, like a mountain stream carrying no message down to the world, there would be nothing even to hope for. How sad that must be, I can't help thinking, though it is none of my business," she said, laughing at herself. "My, Oyae, how well you understand a blossom's heart." "As for why I myself favor the returning geese, I hardly know — but in the moonlit dawn over blossoming mountains, or in the small hours of a spring rain, when I hear their cries passing overhead as they take their leave, it pierces me somehow, sad and lonely at once. And yet, thinking of their promise to return again in autumn, there is something reassuring in it too. And because they speak of 'returning home,' it makes me think of my late parents," she said, her voice trailing into sorrow. "That is only natural. Even I have never forgotten my old nurse — if she were alive today, how I would want to be spoiled by her," Yūko said, moved by the thought. "But how much lonelier, how much sadder it must be for you, who lost your own parents. I can do little, but I want you to think of me as your strength — think of me as an elder sister." "It is strange of you to ask, but since you are older, that has always been our understanding. Just as you always say, I do think of you as a true sister," Oyae said, comforted, and looked glad. "It must be some bond between us that has led not only my parents but myself, round and round, to this house, and I could never put into words how deep my gratitude runs, vast as the sea, high as the mountains. Your kindness has sunk into me, and I will never forget it. It's presumptuous of me, forgetting that you are my mistress and I only a newcomer, to go on saying whatever I please out of my own selfishness. Being kept as near to you as I once was to my own parents, I could ask for nothing more. And yet what I regret is that, being dull-witted by nature, I could surely never be the one you would choose to confide in — I know you must set me apart, and though I have no right to resent it, I cannot help feeling bitter over my own inadequacy," she said, and tears fell, wetting her knees. Yūko looked at her doubtfully, unable to guess what had upset her so, thinking: what is it that has made Oyae say such unexpected, wounding things? "What is it that I keep from you, that you should say I hide things? There has never been a time I didn't tell you what I wouldn't even tell my own mother — I can think of nothing today that would make you say such a thing. But tell me, what is it you think?" she asked, looking straight up into Oyae's face. "That, precisely, is you keeping something from me. Why do you hide it so? Didn't you call me your sister — was that a lie?"

The ending (original text)

"Staying shut indoors all the time is bad for your health too," Oyae said, coaxing her mistress out. "And the fields nearby, with their thatched hut among the rice paddies, have their own quiet charm — won't you come see?" And so, quite against her will, Yūko was drawn out through the brushwood gate, a rare outing for her. Who could say what unspoken thing lay hidden in each other's hearts? The thick groves were cool, the wind in their sleeves just what the heart could wish for; the new rice shoots stood green in the little paddies, and frogs cried out here and there in every different voice. "Even they must be singing — how funny," Yūko said, laughing, and Oyae, glad to see her mistress smile, said she wanted that thatched roof, that hedge, right inside their own garden — "and what flower is that, I wonder" — and ran ahead to it, broke off a spray, and held one blossom out for her mistress and kept one for herself, each adorning the other to please her, neither knowing what lay in the other's heart. Along the footpaths between the fields they wandered back and forth, keeping no particular company, until the day drew to its close; in the evening sky, where even the birds were returning to roost, a single wandering monk passed by, knocking at some gate beneath the moon. "How enviable, to live such a life," they thought, watching him go — when, at the edge of his sedge hat, he turned to glance back, and the two women, as one, cried out, "Oh—!"
