About this work

Sakaguchi Ango's essay on love, written the year after "Discourse on Decadence." Saying honestly at the outset that he does not really know what love is, he goes on to the difference between the Japanese words for longing and for love, the pains the Christians took in translating caritas as what is precious, and the dubiousness of platonic love. And he arrives at the famous line that love is the flower of a life, and that however tedious it may be there is no other flower.

Summary

'I do not really know what love is. It is as though the whole of my literary life has been one long search for whatever it may be' — so Ango begins. Everyone runs up against love at some point. Or else, without ever running up against it, one marries, and comes in time to love one's husband, one's wife, one's children, one's household, one's money, one's clothes. Japanese has two words, koi and ai, and it seems that koi carries the fierce longing for something not yet possessed, while ai carries the settled tenderness of cherishing something already one's own. When the Christian missionaries first came to Japan, they struggled to find a word for 'love,' since under the code of bushidō, romantic passion led straight to adultery, and the character for ai carried no sense of anything pure or chaste. After much struggle, they hit upon the word taisetsu — 'precious' — so that 'I love thee' was rendered as 'I hold thee precious.' From there the discussion turns to the difference between love and marriage, to whether love can endure, to the relation between poetry and everyday life; Ango finds it strange that Platonic love should be prized as something noble, though one need not despise the body either — both spirit and flesh, he says flatly, are equally slipshod, unreliable things. And so Ango arrives at his conclusion. A person is never satisfied by love alone. No matter how many times one falls in love, one gains nothing from it but the fresh discovery, each time, of how tedious it is. And yet life cannot be built without love. Do not speak of loneliness — loneliness is the home we all return to. 'Love is the flower of a life. However tedious it may be, there is no other flower besides it.'

The opening (original text)

I do not really know what love is. It is as though the whole of my literary life has been one long search for whatever it may be. Everyone runs up against love at some point. Or perhaps there are those who marry without ever running up against it at all. But in time they come to love their husband, their wife. Or they love the child that is born to them. They love their household itself. They love money. They love their clothes. I am not joking.

Ren'ai ron by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In life, what is it that comforts a person most? Suffering, sorrow, heartache. Then do not be afraid of being a fool. Through suffering, through sorrow, through heartache, there will surely come moments of some small fulfillment. Would you say that even that is not enough to satisfy the soul? Ah, loneliness. Do not speak of it. Loneliness is the home we all return to. Love is the flower of a life. However tedious it may be, there is no other flower besides it.

Ren'ai ron by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)
