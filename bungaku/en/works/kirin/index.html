About this work

An early short story Tanizaki published in the coterie magazine Shinshichō in 1910, shortly after his debut. Drawing on episodes from the Analects and the Shiji, it sets Confucius, visiting the state of Wei on his travels, against Nanzi, the consort of Duke Ling. The duke, beginning to wake to the way of virtue the sage teaches, at last gives way before the bewitching power of his wife — all told in a gorgeous style with Chinese diction woven in, and closing with the line that he has never yet seen one who loved virtue as he loves beauty. With "The Tattooer," a central aesthetic piece of his debut period.

Summary

In the spring of 493 BCE, Confucius sets out with his disciples from his native state of Lu, and in the course of his travels to spread his teachings arrives at last in the state of Wei. Duke Ling of Wei looks up to Confucius as his teacher and aspires, under his guidance, to govern with virtue — but the duke's heart remains captive to his consort, Lady Nanzi, a woman of peerless beauty and a cruel, ruthless spirit. Determined to win back a husband who has begun to heed the sage's words more than her own, Nanzi resorts to every seductive art and scheme she commands, and at last has Confucius's carriage made to follow behind her own as she parades through the great avenues of the capital, showing the whole populace that her own beauty outweighs the sage's virtue. That night, trembling, Duke Ling tells her, "You are the demon who will be my ruin," even as he lets himself once more be taken into Nanzi's arms. The next morning, Confucius and his disciples depart the state of Wei. "I have never yet seen one who loves virtue as he loves beauty" — this final utterance of the sage alone survives, recorded to this day in the Analects.

Characters

  • Confucius　The sage who travels the states preaching the Way. Teaches Duke Ling of Wei the path of virtue
  • Lady Nanzi　Duke Ling's consort. Rules the state at will through her peerless beauty and cruel heart
  • Duke Ling　Ruler of the state of Wei. Torn between the sage's virtue and his consort's seductive beauty
  • Zilu　Confucius's foremost disciple. A bold and valiant follower who leads the party

The opening (original text)

Phoenix, oh phoenix — how has your virtue so declined! What is past cannot be reproved, but what is yet to come may still be pursued. Enough, enough! Perilous indeed are those who now hold office. In the year 493 before the Christian era — according to the records of Zuo Qiuming, Meng Ke, Sima Qian, and others — in the early spring when Duke Ding of Lu performed the suburban sacrifice for the thirteenth time, Confucius, with a handful of disciples attending his carriage on either side, set out from his native state of Lu upon the road to spread his teaching. Along the banks of the Si River the fragrant grasses had already begun to put out their first green shoots, and though the snows atop Mount Fang, Mount Ni, and the Five Peaks had melted away, a north wind, as fierce as any raised by the Xiongnu sweeping in with fistfuls of desert sand, still carried with it the last, bitter traces of winter.

The ending (original text)

That evening, the lady, adorned with unusual care, lay waiting upon the brocade coverlets of her own chamber long into the night, until at last there came the soft sound of footsteps, and someone knocked, ever so quietly, at the door. "Ah — so you have come back to me at last. Never again may you flee from my embrace — not now, and not ever." And Lady Nanzi spread wide her arms and gathered Duke Ling in beneath her long, trailing sleeves. Her supple arms, hot with wine, wound about his body like bonds that, once tied, could never be undone. "I hate you. You are a fearsome woman. You are the demon who will be my ruin. And yet, whatever I do, I cannot tear myself away from you." Duke Ling's voice trembled as he spoke. In the lady's eyes shone the pride of triumphant evil. The next morning, Confucius and his party set out once more upon the road to spread their teaching, this time bound for the state of Cao. "I have never yet seen one who loves virtue as he loves beauty." These were the sage's last words on leaving the state of Wei. And it is by these words, set down in that precious book, the Analects, that they are handed down to us even today.
