About this work

Hagiwara Sakutarō's second collection, published by Shinchōsha in 1923. Turning from the sharp dread of Howling at the Moon, it sings the languor and melancholy that drift through the nights of a great city, and an endless longing for the real, in a rhythm of colloquial Japanese at once listless and beautiful. The poet likens his own feeling to the sound of a flute heard on a spring night, and sets out to sing not sensation or passion but a nostalgia like cloud moving across the shadow of the soul. In sections such as "The Phantom Bed" and "Melancholy Cherry," besides the title poem, it crystallizes the loneliness and decadence of life in a modern city. A collection that announced the completion of free colloquial verse.

Summary

The book is made up of six sections: "The Phantom Bed," "Melancholy Cherry Blossoms," "The Lonely Blue Cat," "An Elegant Appetite," "Will and Ignorance," and "The Voluptuous Soul." In the opening poem, "Room at Dusk," the narrator, owner of a tired heart, grieves over the dim light inside the room and the powerless rhythm of life, and looks for comfort in the presence of his lover. What runs through the whole collection is not fierce passion but a "homesickness of the soul for no reason one can name" that has followed the poet since childhood, and the sorrow of longing for the world of the real. In the title poem, "The Blue Cat," he sings the joy of loving a lively city, and then declares that the only thing that can sleep in the night of that great city is the shadow of a single blue cat — a shadow that tells the sad history of mankind, the blue shadow of the happiness he never ceases to seek. Tedium, decadence, the gloom of moonlit nights, an elegant appetite, and the fatalistic darkness that thinking brings: these run in two streams, a melancholy of the senses and a melancholy of thought, and melt together in a languid, sensuous rhythm. If Howling at the Moon drew the shudder of modern nerves, The Blue Cat draws the world in which those nerves settle into the tedium and the homesickness that float through a city at night; it is one of the summits of free verse in the spoken language.

The opening (original text)

Room at Dusk A tired heart sleeps well at night I sleep well I am the owner of a lonely heart dressed in flannel Something — a nursing infant inside a dream — is moving quietly there The crying of a fly numbed with cold boom boom boom boom boom boom. I grieve for this whitish light inside the room I feel the loneliness of this powerless rhythm of life.

The Blue Cat by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The Blue Cat It is a good thing to love this beautiful city It is a good thing to love the buildings of this beautiful city in order to seek out all the gentle women in order to seek out all the noble ways of living it is a good thing to come to this capital and walk its busy streets The rows of cherry trees standing along the streets and are there not numberless sparrows chirping in them too? Ah — the only thing that can sleep in the night of this great city is the shadow of a single blue cat the shadow of a cat that tells the sad history of mankind the blue shadow of the happiness I never cease to seek.
