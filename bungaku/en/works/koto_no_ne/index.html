About this work

A short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the magazine Bungakukai in December 1893. Kingo, a boy left by his mother at four and orphaned when drink took his father, has been given up by the world and is sliding into crime — until, on an autumn night, the sound of a koto over a hedge strikes him, and he weeps at a gentleness he had forgotten and at a longing for his mother that comes back. One night in which the beauty of art saves a coarsened soul, entrusted to clear moonlight and the notes of a koto.

Summary

Watanabe Kingo is a boy who, ever since his mother left home at the urging of her own parents when he was four years old, grew up learning the world's cruelty in utter solitude, having lost even his father — who drowned himself in drink — to death. Having watched his father's wretched end, Kingo, believing his mother's act as devilish, comes to regard this fleeting world as devoid of sincerity, and as the years pass his heart twists ever harder, like stone. Reviled by all as a pickpocket and a thief, watched with suspicion even by the police, he falls at last into the reputation of "Watanabe's Kingo, a true brigand," and has thought of death more than once. On a rainy autumn night, as Kingo creeps toward the house of a woman named Shizu, who lives alone in Negishi, the sound of a koto she plucks by herself reaches his ears. Under a moon clear in the frosty night, that pure melody, like music of the heavens, calls forth the gentleness long hidden in the depths of Kingo's heart, and tears he cannot hold back come flooding out. Even the tenderness of the mother he once resented as a devil now pierces him to the core, and Kingo comes, at last, too late, to know how hard it is to abandon this world. Taking the sound of the koto's unseen player as a friend for a hundred years, Kingo at last steps forward into a world in bloom.

Characters

  • Watanabe Kingo　A boy abandoned by his mother at four and left destitute by his father's death from drink, driven toward a life of wrongdoing. Saved by the sound of a koto
  • Morie Shizu (Oshizu)　A nineteen-year-old woman living alone in Negishi. Devoted body and soul to the art of the koto, she is the mistress of the house Kingo creeps toward. Kingo never sees her face, only hears her music
  • Kingo's mother　Left her husband and child when Kingo was four, urged on by her own parents
  • Kingo's father　After his wife left him, sank into drink and met a wretched end

The opening (original text)

In the sky, the light of sun and moon knows no change, and the tranquil blossoming of spring should belong alike to every soul in this fleeting world — then why does the storm rage in these treetops alone? Alas, upon one innocent body has fallen the misfortune of scattered branch and leaf; cruelly beaten by rain and blown by wind through fourteen springs and autumns, there drifts a child who finds himself, in the little thread of life yet remaining to him, adrift in a state he can only call his own bitter fortune. It was not that his mother, in this child's fourth year, left the house of her own will only to escape her own suffering alone, and yet—

Koto no ne by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

A body like his, that could not stand even against a loosed arrow, would in the end lie exposed as carrion across field and hill, following his father's pitiable end; or else, his evil name carried abroad along the roadside, he would live out his days in the wretched world of a man chained at the waist. And yet — the gentleness that had lain hidden in the depths of his heart now rose in harmony with the sound of the koto beneath the moon at the third watch of the night, and the tears that began to spill from him — were they beads of dew? If beads they were, he would not have traded them for all the cities of the House of Zhao. Was it love, or was it only tenderness? He did not so much as know the face of the one who played. At the faint sound leaking out beyond the brushwood fence, he did not know if he felt glad; he knew only shame. Even the tenderness of the mother he had once resented as a devil now pierced him through, and Kingo knew, at last and too late, how hard a thing it is to abandon this world. The moon grew ever clearer, and the scent of the chrysanthemums by the hedge filled his sleeves in the night; and when the night wind blew and swept away the clouds within his heart, the koto's sound rose again — would it become, he wondered, a friend for a hundred years? Or would it leave behind an anguish of a hundred years? From this night, Kingo stepped out into a world of flowers in full and glorious bloom.
