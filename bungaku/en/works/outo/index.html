About this work

A short story published in the magazine Sekai in May 1948, shortly before his death, known for its opening line: I want to believe that parents matter more than children. From the evening meal in the household of a writer with three small children, it moves through the frictions between husband and wife and the pressure of daily life to the father escaping to a bar, self-mocking and wry throughout. It gave its name to Ōtōki, the anniversary of Dazai's death, and reads almost as a testament.

Summary

"I want to think that parents matter more than children." The father, a writer, lives overwhelmed by his children in a household of three small ones: a daughter of seven, a son of four, and a second daughter of one. The four-year-old boy is slow in developing and cannot speak yet. Husband and wife avoid talking seriously about him, and cover their grief with exchanges made to sound like jokes. At the supper table an idle joke of the father's brings an awkward silence between them, and the words the mother lets fall — "Between my breasts there is a valley of tears..." — go into him like a blade. To live is to have chains tangled about one from every side, so that the least movement makes the blood spurt. The father leaves the house with the envelope of his manuscript fee and goes straight to a bar. Thinking, of the cherries set before him, how pleased the children would be if he brought them home, he eats them looking thoroughly as if he did not enjoy them, spitting out the stones, and murmurs inwardly, like a man putting on a bold front: parents matter more than children.

Characters

  • The father　A writer with three small children, who runs from the weight of daily life to a bar
  • The mother　The father's wife, who holds back her tears and keeps the household going

The opening (original text)

I want to think that parents matter more than children. However admirably I may try to think, like some old-fashioned moralist, that it is all for the children's sake — the truth is that the parent is the weaker of the two. In my household, at any rate, that is how it is. I certainly have no such shameless, convenient designs as being looked after and helped by my children when I am old; and yet this parent, in his own house, is forever watching to see whether the children are pleased with him.

The ending (original text)

In my house the children are never given anything luxurious to eat. They may never even have seen cherries. If I gave them some, they would be delighted. If their father brought them home, they would be delighted. If the stems were tied together with thread and hung around the neck, cherries would look like a coral necklace. But the father eats the cherries heaped on the big plate, looking thoroughly as though he does not enjoy them, and spits out a stone, and eats, and spits out a stone, and eats, and spits out a stone; and the words he murmurs in his heart, like a man putting on a bold front, are: parents matter more than children.

Famous passages

I want to believe that parents matter more than children.

The opening of a story published in the year of his death. In softening a flat statement into “I want to believe,” rather than asserting it outright, lies a self-concealment typical of Dazai, exposing the weakness of a father overwhelmed by his family.

Living is a terrible business. Chains are wound around you from every side, and if you move even a little, blood spurts out.

A monologue of the narrator, cornered by the weight of family life. Echoing the opening line, “I want to believe that parents matter more than children,” it states the hardship of living nakedly through the images of chains and blood.
