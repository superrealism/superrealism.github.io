About this work

Uchida Roan renders vividly, with the air of the time, the fever for Westernization in the Rokumeikan years, when ministers and generals danced in fancy dress. Telling of the frenzy and the collapse of the radical Westernizing policy pushed by Inoue Kaoru and Mori Arinori, he draws how a new literature sprouted from the nutrients that great flood left behind, until The Character of Modern Students was welcomed as the first voice of a rising literature. A recollection of the culture of early Meiji.

Summary

An essay in cultural history in which Uchida Roan recalls the fever for Europeanization of the Rokumeikan age forty years ago, and draws how the new literature was born out of it. Taking the recent craze for dancing for his opening, he tells of the brilliant frenzy of Europeanization with the Rokumeikan for its stage, and gives a vivid account of the unheard-of fancy-dress ball at which ministers of state and generals put on powder and wigs and danced, and of the strange rumor about a great lady that arose that night. He sets out the extraordinary state of the world in which Mori Arinori, the brain of the Europeanizing policy, tried to remake the education of women from its foundations, in which mixing of the sexes and free love were preached in the girls' schools, and in which women's riding and even theories of improving the race were put forward. In time a conservative reaction against Europeanization set in; the men of spirit were driven out of the capital under the Peace Preservation Ordinance, and Ozaki Yukio left the city in fine style, and the Inoue cabinet fell — but this absurd flood of Europeanization, Roan holds, left behind the nourishment from which a new culture could sprout. Taking the political novels of Yano Ryūkei and Suehiro Tetchō and Tōkai Sanshi for its forerunners, and with the old popular writers left behind, Shosei katagi by the Bachelor of Letters Haru no Ya Oboro was welcomed as the first cry of the new literature; and he places the Europeanizing policy of Count Inoue as "a most precious absurdity."

Characters

  • Inoue Kaoru　The foremost champion of the Europeanizing policy, who led the Rokumeikan age and resigned when it brought on a conservative reaction
  • Mori Arinori　The minister of education, the brain of the cabinet of the day. A radical Europeanizer who tried to remake the education of women from its foundations
  • Ozaki Yukio　Gakudō Koji. Ordered out of the capital under the Peace Preservation Ordinance, he was admired for boasting, "I am already a minister of state"
  • Tsubouchi Shōyō　Haru no Ya Oboro, Bachelor of Letters, who put Shosei katagi before the world as the first cry of the new literature
  • Uchida Roan　The author

The opening (original text)

The passing of the anti-Japanese bill in America turned into the furious agitation of reactionary bodies, and the blow fell sideways upon the dance floor of the Imperial Hotel, which a party of sword-dancers broke into. The good men and women lost in their dancing were threatened with blunted swords, and the hotel's dance floor was shut for a time. Now the heat has cooled and the dance floor has been opened again after several months, but the fever for dancing, great as it was, is no longer what it was. But the dancing fever of the so-called Rokumeikan age forty years ago was nothing like this. It was not so general as now, to be sure; but since the gentlemen of the highest offices led the way, high society was swept along in a moment.

Shijūnen mae by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The young Count Inoue of those days would surely never have thought this dangerous. The Count's Europeanizing policy was an imitation of the husk of civilization; but it made men's minds new and filled them with spirit and made an epoch of a new civilization. Even though it came to a check, there is no denying that it sowed the seed of a new culture. The Count's cultural policy of that time ended at last as an absurdity in the old family style; and yet this comedy was a most precious absurdity.

Shijūnen mae by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
