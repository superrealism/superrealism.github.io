What is Tanka?

Category
Form / Genre

A poem of thirty-one syllables in five lines of 5-7-5-7-7. Also called "misohitomoji." In the age of the "Man'yōshū" it was one verse form alongside the chōka (long poem) and sedōka, but even after the chōka declined it continued to be composed, and from the Heian period on, "waka" came to mean, almost exclusively, this form. Even this form, which had carried Japanese lyricism for more than a thousand years, had its old conventions called into question once the Meiji era began. In 1894 Yosano Tekkan, in "Bōkoku no On" ("Sounds of a Nation's Ruin"), fiercely attacked the old school of poetry centered on the Imperial Poetry Bureau, and in 1898 Masaoka Shiki serialized "A Letter to Practitioners of Tanka" in the newspaper "Nippon," rejecting the "Kokin Wakashū" and calling for a return to the "Man'yōshū." From Tekkan's "Myōjō," Yosano Akiko released "Midaregami" ("Tangled Hair," 1901), boldly singing of sensuality, bringing a fashion for a Romantic poetic style, while Shiki's Negishi Tanka Society crystallized into "Araragi," thoroughgoing in its commitment to sketch-based composition, and produced Saitō Mokichi and others. Ishikawa Takuboku, in "Ichiaku no Suna" ("A Handful of Sand," 1910) and "Kanashiki Gangu" ("Sad Toys," 1912), engraving the joys and sorrows of daily life in plain colloquial language and a new three-line format, opened a new path for modern tanka.
