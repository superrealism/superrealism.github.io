About this work

"Ill on a journey, my dreams run about a withered field" — Akutagawa's short story, drawing without mercy and one by one the inner states of the disciples gathered around the dying Bashō. Vanity lurking under grief, relief, and a sense of release. His cold eye on the complexity of the human heart puts a famous scene under a wholly new light.

Summary

The afternoon of the twelfth day of the tenth month of the seventh year of Genroku, in a back room of Hanaya Nizaemon's house in front of the Midō in Osaka. Matsuo Tōsei of the Bashō hermitage, revered as the great master of haikai, is quietly breathing his last at the age of fifty-one. At his pillow the physician Bokusetsu watches a pulse that comes at longer and longer intervals, and the old servant Jirobei, who has followed him from Iga, keeps up a low invocation of the Buddha's name. Around them sit the disciples — Kikaku, Kyorai, Jōsō, Otokuni, Masahide, Shikō, Izen. Akutagawa looks in turn into the heart of each of them. Kikaku, appointed to give the water of the last moment, feels in the midst of his grief something almost like distinction. The methodical Kyorai, crushed by his master's death, worries whether he is grieving in the correct way. The shrewd Shikō has already begun to think how he will tell of this scene afterward. Izen is flustered at his own inability to weep where weeping is called for. And last of all comes Jōsō, the humblest and least conspicuous. In the midst of his master's death he felt a strangely peaceful feeling flow into his breast — a feeling like grief becoming pure and clear, like the light of dawn spreading through darkness. But its true nature he does not want to admit even to himself: it was the joy of release, the free spirit that had long submitted to the yoke of Bashō's personal pressure at last stretching out its limbs. In that entranced and sorrowful joy Jōsō, with a faint smile on his lips, made a reverent obeisance to the dying Bashō.

Characters

  • Matsuo Bashō　The great master of haikai, at the point of death
  • Jōsō　The disciple who becomes aware of the joy of release
  • Kikaku, Kyorai, Shikō and Izen　The disciples sitting about his pillow
  • Bokusetsu　The physician who watches his pulse

The opening (original text)

It is the afternoon of the twelfth day of the tenth month of the seventh year of Genroku. The sky, which had burned red for a while at dawn, drew the eyes of the merchants of Osaka as they rose from bed toward the distant tiled roofs, wondering whether it would rain in showers as it had yesterday; but happily there was not rain enough even to blur the tops of the willows that had shed their leaves, and it became at length a cloudy but faintly bright and quiet winter noon.

Kareno shō by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This peaceful feeling of Jōsō's was the joy of release, in which his free spirit, having long submitted in vain to the yoke of Bashō's personal pressure, was at last trying to stretch out its limbs with its own natural strength. In that entranced and sorrowful joy, telling the bodhi-seed beads of his rosary, he made a reverent obeisance to the dying Bashō with a faint smile on his lips, as though the disciples sobbing around him had been swept from the bottom of his eyes and gone.
