About this work

A classic of Japanese folklore studies published in 1926, based on a series run the year before in the magazine Asahi Graph. Yanagita pursues the "mountain people" — those said once to have lived in the hills after a manner unlike the people of the plains — as they vanish under modernization, following local traditions and actual incidents: spiriting-away, recluses in the mountains, those who went up and never came back, the legends of mountain hags and tengu. Through them he works his way into the old strata of the Japanese mind. The anecdote placed at the opening, of a charcoal-burner who killed his own children at the end of his poverty, is famous.

Summary

"There is probably no one but myself who remembers it now" — Yanagita begins by setting down the terrible story of a charcoal burner in the mountains of western Mino who, some thirty years before, killed his own two children with an axe out of sheer poverty. The charcoal would not sell, and the starving children are said to have sharpened the axe themselves and held it out to him, telling him to kill them with it: a hidden reality of appalling cruelty. From there Yanagita goes on gathering all sorts of stories about the mountains — the people called Sanka, who moved about the hills and fields without fixed houses; men who went into the mountains out of anger at the world and became like hermits; women who ran mad after childbirth and fled into the hills; the rare ones who came back down to the villages; and the legends of mountain hags, tengu and spiriting away. Using these as his clues, he searches for the reality of "mountain people" who may have lived a life apart from those of the plains, and throws light on the old strata of ordinary life and belief that are being lost. It is a monument standing at the very beginning of Japanese folklore studies.

The opening (original text)

1. That There Are Lives Buried in the Mountains There is probably no one but myself who remembers it now. More than thirty years ago, in a year when times were very bad, a charcoal burner of about fifty in the mountains of western Mino killed two of his children with an axe. His wife had died some time before, and he was left with one boy of thirteen. Then, by what circumstances I do not know, he took in a girl of about the same age and was raising the two of them together at his charcoal hut in the mountains. Their names I have forgotten now. Do what he would, the charcoal would not sell; however often he went down to the village, he could never get so much as a cup of rice. On the last day too he came back empty-handed, and finding it hard to look at the faces of the small starving creatures, he went straight to the back of the hut and slept through the afternoon.

Yama no jinsei by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I earnestly hope that in days to come this question may receive somewhat closer attention from the learned world, and that it may not merely furnish new material for anthropology but gradually make plain, from this direction, many things in the history of Japanese civilization that cannot yet be explained by any means. Above all, the thought that we may have mixed in our blood some portion of the blood of the wild mountain people is, for us, of quite endless interest.

Yama no jinsei by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)
