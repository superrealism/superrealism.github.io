About this work

A short story Yokomitsu Riichi published in the first number of the coterie magazine Bungei Jidai in 1924. Known for its famous opening — that it is high noon, and the special express is running at full speed, packed full — it renders the crowd psychology of the passengers of an express stopped by a landslide, in cinematic figures full of speed. Its bold style, challenging the established realism, was the signal fire for the birth of the New Sensationalist school, and a landmark in modern Japanese literature.

Summary

In a special express train running at full speed and packed full, a brazen little acolyte goes on singing at the top of his voice without a thought for anyone around him. When at last the train is brought to a stand by a landslide, the passengers, indifferent until then, grow excited and press the station men, who can give them nothing to the purpose. An announcement is put out urging them to change to a line that goes round; but no one will move, unable to reckon the profit and the loss. Just then a well-fed man of money steps calmly forward and holds out his ticket, and at that signal the crowd all at once pours after him into the connecting train. Left behind, the acolyte goes on singing calmly, alone in the emptied express; and before long the clearing of the landslide is announced, and the express sets off again at full speed, carrying him and no one else, as though nothing had happened.

Characters

  • The acolyte　A brazen little boy priest who goes on singing without a thought for anyone. A figure set against the confusion of the passengers
  • The passengers　The crowd running this way and that in the stranded express
  • The stout gentleman　A man of money, whose girth suggests an enormous fortune, and who comes to lead the crowd

The opening (original text)

It is high noon. The special express train was running at full speed, packed full. The small stations along the line were ignored like stones. At all events, among the passengers crammed into that train amid such a phenomenon there was one brazen little acolyte. Taking a seat with the air of a full-grown man, he began to tie a hand towel round his head. Then, beating the window frame with both hands, he began to sing at the top of his voice.

Heads and Bellies by Yokomitsu Riichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And the acolyte? In high spirits, beating the window frame. Alone, rolling the whites and blacks of his eyes like a pendulum. "Ah — plum, cherry, peony, peach, one man cannot carry them all, yoi, yoi."

Heads and Bellies by Yokomitsu Riichi (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

It was noon. The special express train ran full speed ahead, packed with passengers. The small stations along the line were ignored, as if they were stones.

The celebrated three lines that announced the arrival of the New Sensationist school. The leap of personification in the small stations being “ignored, as if they were stones” shocked the literary establishment of its day.
