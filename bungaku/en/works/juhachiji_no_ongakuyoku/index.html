About this work

A short science-fiction story Unno Jūza published in the magazine Modern Nippon in 1937. Set in a future dictatorship that has gone underground after repeated wars, it draws the terror of a music bath that governs the brain and remakes the citizens into uniform human beings. One of the central works of a writer called the father of Japanese science fiction, and a dystopian novel that drew the nightmare of a managed society before its time.

Summary

In a future ravaged by repeated wars until the earth's surface lies poisoned with gas and bacteria, the survivors of humanity live burrowed underground. In the nation of Miruki, ruled by the dictator President Miruki, the population is governed by "the music bath" — an invention of the scientist Dr. Kohaku. Every day at eighteen hundred hours, for exactly thirty minutes, vibrational music is fed into the brain through spiral chairs, remolding every resident, without exception, into an obedient, industrious "standard person." But within the populace forced into this uniformity, a poison quietly pools, and some even begin, in secret, to change their sex. Just then the ambitious woman minister Asari, together with Mrs. Miruki — who secretly adores Kohaku — schemes to bring the doctor down, staging a scandal against him on a television broadcast and sending him to execution. But Kohaku has an android built in his own exact likeness blown up in his place, and hides himself deep within his laboratory. Asari, having seized real power, plunges into a tyranny that increases the music bath to twenty-four sessions a day; the people, exhausted, go mad, and fall one after another. Then the alarm sounds: rocket warships from Mars are approaching. But the utterly enfeebled populace no longer has the strength to fight, and they perish trying to force open the doors. At last, when even Lord Miruki and the woman minister have exhausted themselves, Dr. Kohaku — believed dead — appears clad in armor, leading five hundred androids. He silences Miruki Country's controlling music and has a new music bath played instead, one that celebrates humanity, and, as the sole surviving human being, sets out to build an ideal nation populated entirely by androids.

Characters

  • Dr. Kohaku　A brilliant scientist of Miruki Country. Inventor of the music bath and the androids. Coldly rational; in the end builds a nation of androids alone
  • President Miruki　The dictator of Miruki Country. Wears his beard as a symbol of authority. A womanizer, infatuated with the android Annette
  • Asari　An ambitious woman minister. Conspires with Lord Miruki and seizes real power through intrigue, imposing the tyranny of the music bath
  • Mrs. Miruki　The President's wife. Confesses her love to Kohaku, but is caught up in the conspiracy and dies in the explosion
  • Bara and Pen　Kohaku's young disciples (a female student and a male student). Weary of the controlled society, they grow drawn to the android Annette
  • Annette　The beautiful android girl Kohaku has created

The opening (original text)

Beneath the sun, the earth lay in twilight. In the country that lay directly beneath that darkening zone, the eighteen-hundred-hour time signal had just begun, solemnly, to shake the hearts of a million residents. "Ah, eighteen hundred hours." "The eighteen-hundred music bath." "Now then, everyone — quickly, to your seats, so no one is late!" In District Alicia there were only three: Dr. Kohaku, the male student Pen, and the female student Bara. Before they had even caught the time signal in their ears, the three of them had flung open the door and sprung out into the blue corridor. Along that blue corridor, seats made of thick, silver-gleaming metal pipe bent into spirals stretched away in rows, far into the distance.

The ending (original text)

A music of humanity's praise. Was it to be played for the cold corpses of the people of Miruki Country? Or was it to be played to transplant a human soul into the beautiful androids the doctor had created? No — it was an elegy played for Kohaku the autocrat, the one surviving human being alone. For a man possessed of so brilliant a mind, reviving the countless dead of Miruki Country would have been no great difficulty. But the doctor held no such intention at all. A scientist was, in the end, a creature that cold. And so Dr. Kohaku set himself, with firm conviction and great self-assurance, to building here the utopia he had long held as his ideal. The newly risen nation of Kohaku's androids, wrapped now in a new music bath in praise of humanity, had at last set out on the founding of a new world.
