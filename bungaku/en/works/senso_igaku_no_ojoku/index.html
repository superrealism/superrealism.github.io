About this work

The Kyushu University vivisection case, in which American prisoners of war were dissected alive at Kyushu Imperial University late in the Pacific war. A memoir left by the professor of anatomy who was implicated and tried as a war criminal, setting down the facts as he saw them and his doubts about the war trials, written when Endō Shūsaku's The Sea and Poison appeared.

Summary

In 1957, upon reading Endō Shūsaku's novel The Sea and Poison, the author feels as though an old wound has been torn open. For the novel depicted, in vivid detail, the very affair — the so-called Kyūshū University vivisection incident — that had branded him a war criminal and sentenced him to twenty-five years of hard labor. In May 1945, the author, then the professor in charge of anatomy at the Kyūshū University medical school, received a telephone call from Ishiyama Fukujirō, head of the First Surgical Department, asking to borrow the anatomy lecture room for an operation on American prisoners of war. A few days later, returning from a business trip and startled by the sentry posted in the corridor, the author opened the door of the operating room to find an uncanny scene: physicians in white coats, surrounded, as though at some spectacle, by military staff officers. A single piece of advice he gave, pointing out on a specimen the location of the substantia nigra when asked, would later be counted against him as an "advisor" to the crime. Why did the incident happen in Fukuoka, and why under Professor Ishiyama? The author traces the background — the Western Army wielding the National Mobilization Law, a university grown far too cozy with the military, and the notion of putting the execution of prisoners to "meaningful use" — and reveals as well the truth behind a fabricated cannibalism charge, extracted through torture and leading questions. The verdict brought severe sentences, including five hangings. Sentenced at sixty to twenty-five years, the author still does not look away from his own responsibility, even as he quietly raises his doubts about war-crimes trials themselves — the victor sitting in judgment over the vanquished — and closes by speaking of the second match of his life, begun within the walls of Sugamo Prison.

The opening (original text)

There is a phrase: to have an old wound torn open. When I read Endō Shūsaku's novel The Sea and Poison, published in the magazine Bungakukai, I felt exactly as though my own old scar had been torn open. For this novel depicted, in vivid detail, the so-called Kyūshū University vivisection incident (in fact more properly called the Aikawa incident) — the very affair that branded me with the label of war criminal and sentenced me to twenty-five years of hard labor. To dissect a living human being — could such a cruel, horrifying thing, unimaginable in today's peaceful world, truly have happened?

Sensō igaku no ojoku ni furete by Hirakō Goichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In the end, what I wish to say is this: is it truly right that the victor should seize the power to judge, and pass sentence upon the vanquished according to the victor's own arbitrary will? America is, by its own reckoning, a Christian nation. The strength of Christianity lies in the richness of its life of conscience. The apostle Paul in particular emphasized a "good conscience," and it was conscience and faith that gave him courage. And yet, the more he examined himself, the more he trembled to find himself a man riddled with faults and wounds — and it was precisely for that reason that he could say, "I am the chief of sinners." I too wished, in some small way, to follow his example, and so I dared to take up this pen. I can only ask my readers' indulgence.

Sensō igaku no ojoku ni furete by Hirakō Goichi (Aozora Bunko)
