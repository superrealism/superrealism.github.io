About this work

An adaptation drawn from Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, published in Chūō Kōron in November 1905 and collected in the early volume Yōkyoshū. The title refers to an ancient Chinese funeral song that likens human life to the frailty of dew on a leek leaf. The story of Queen Guinevere and the maid Elaine around Lancelot of the Round Table is built into five chapters — "Dream," "Mirror," "Sleeve," "Sin" and "Boat" — and written in a dignified classical style.

Summary

At Camelot, where the knights have set out for the tournament in the north, Queen Guinevere continues her secret trysts with Lancelot, hidden from every eye. She tells him of an ill-omened dream — a golden serpent coiling round her hair — and, to turn aside suspicion, urges him to go to the tournament himself; Lancelot, answering her, sets off belatedly for the north. On the way, lodging at the old castle of Astolat, the maiden Elaine falls in love with him at first sight, and at midnight offers him a sleeve of burning crimson, begging him to bind it about his helm; the knight, in exchange, leaves his shield behind as a token before departing. Just then, the Lady of Shalott, who weaves as she watches only the world reflected in her mirror, shatters that mirror in her wish to look directly upon his figure as he passes along the road. The queen waits, tormented by jealousy of the owner of that red sleeve, for Lancelot, who has won renown at the tournament but vanished after taking a grievous wound; at court, Mordred and the others denounce the queen's guilt. Meanwhile Elaine, wasted thin with love, leaves behind her last testament and breathes her last; her body, a letter still clasped in her hand, is set upon a boat, which drifts at last to the water-gate of Camelot.

Characters

  • Lancelot　The foremost knight of the Round Table, in the service of King Arthur
  • Guinevere　King Arthur's queen. Carries on a secret affair with Lancelot, hidden from every eye
  • Elaine　Daughter of the lord of Astolat. Gives Lancelot a crimson sleeve, and wastes away with love and dies
  • The Lady of Shalott　A woman who lives in a high tower and weaves while watching only the world reflected in her mirror
  • Mordred　Leads twelve knights and denounces the queen's guilt before Arthur

The opening (original text)

A hundred, two hundred, knights gathered in throngs, every last one of them, hastened off to the tournament in the north, and in the stone-worn halls of Camelot there remains only the trailing whisper of Queen Guinevere's long robe. A single pale rose-colored garment is flung carelessly across her shoulders, baring even her white upper arms, while her skirts, lightly swept, wrap about jeweled slippers, their excess trailing behind her over two steps of the stone stair as she climbs. At the very top of the climbed staircase, a curtain woven deep in dun-colored thread with a great flower hangs as though reproaching the absence of any soul, and stirs not so much as a breath.

Kairokō by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Of all the dead, Elaine's corpse is the most beautiful. Her cool, serene face lies buried in golden hair tangled like cloud, and she lies as though smiling. The king asks, in a solemn voice, "Who is this?" The old man, resting his hand upon the oar, opens his mouth not at all, as though mute. Guinevere descends the stone stair at once, and from amid the scattered lilies takes up the letter clasped in Elaine's right hand, breaking the seal to see what it holds. A sorrowful voice crosses the water once more, and, as though a fine thread had been plucked to send a ripple through it, pierces every ear: "...Beautiful... love, its color... fading..." Having finished reading, Guinevere bends low and presses her trembling lips to Elaine's brow — her brow, translucent within the boat — saying, "Beautiful girl!" And at the same moment a single hot tear falls upon Elaine's cold cheek. The thirteen knights looked, each of them, into one another's eyes.
