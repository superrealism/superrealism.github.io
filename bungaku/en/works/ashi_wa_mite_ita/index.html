About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Shūkan Asahi in 1947. In the reed beds of the Kumai river wrapped in thick fog, something happened early one summer morning eighteen years ago that only those reeds saw. A piece quietly folding human guilt and karma into the figure of Tanomo, a man who lives for his own gain, and the rustle of the reeds on the bank.

Summary

Around four in the morning at the start of May, the Kumai River lay covered in thick fog. Reeds grew thick from the riverbank into the water, a fragile embankment of ten-odd shaku, and the traces of someone having climbed up and down it — all by the wide backwater where the river bends sharply around the edge of the Kannon-ji hills. With no wind, amid the faintly stirring mist, a single cormorant flies off without a sound. This quiet riverside reed bed had once witnessed something. Early one summer morning eighteen years before, in thick fog, a certain event took place — and only the reeds on the riverbank had seen it. At the center of the story is a man who lives for his own selfish desires: Tanomo. A letter reaches him, left behind by a woman who died writing only the words "joy, and happiness." But reading it, Tanomo recalls nothing at all, and with a contemptuous frown mutters, "Whoever she was, what a foolish thing to have done," and throws the letter away. Then, cheerfully, he takes up once more his scheming to secure the post of castle deputy. The wind passes, and the reeds rustle and sway — as though, over the event of eighteen years before and Tanomo's having just now cast the letter aside, the reeds were whispering something to one another. A piece by Yamamoto that renders human sin and karma through the rustling of riverside reeds.

The opening (original text)

Around four in the morning at the start of May — The Kumai River lay covered in thick fog. All around was still dim, and there was scarcely any seeing far in any direction. Reeds grew thick along the bank; they grew from the shore out into the river, seven or eight ken into the water, continuing on both upstream and down.

Ashi wa mite ita by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Tanomo threw the letter away, and once more thrust his fishing rod into the ground. The wind passed over, and the reeds, leaning all one way, swayed with their leaves aligned. Rustling with a dry sound — the reeds seemed to be whispering something to one another. About the event that had happened in thick fog one early summer morning eighteen years before, and about Tanomo's having just now thrown the letter away... the wind kept passing steadily, and the reeds went on rustling and swaying.
