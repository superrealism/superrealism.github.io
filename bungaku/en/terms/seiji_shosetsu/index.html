What is Seiji Shōsetsu (Political Fiction)?

Category
Form / Genre

Fiction written from the mid-1880s into the early 1890s, in the course of the Freedom and People's Rights Movement, aimed at propagandizing and enlightening readers in political thought. Most of its writers were themselves participants in the movement, and, framing the story through Western or ancient historical events, they set out the ideals of constitutional government, and were widely read by young people aiming for the opening of a national assembly. Representative works include Yano Ryūkei's "Keikoku Bidan" ("A Noble Tale of Statesmanship," 1883–84), drawing on ancient Greece to depict the ideals of the Constitutional Progressive Party; Tōkai Sanshi's "Kajin no Kigū" ("Chance Meetings with Beautiful Women," from 1885), expressing righteous indignation at independence movements in various countries; and Suehiro Tetchō's "Setchūbai" ("Plum Blossoms in the Snow," 1886). Their style is in a Sino-Japanese idiom, and their characters often remain mere mouthpieces for the author's thought, with political usefulness prioritized over literary polish. The genre's role effectively ended with the opening of the Imperial Diet in 1890 and it declined rapidly, but in departing from the playful spirit of gesaku to bring social themes into fiction, and in introducing the patterns of Western narrative through translation and adaptation, it forms a prehistory to the modern novel that begins with Tsubouchi Shōyō's "Shōsetsu Shinzui" ("The Essence of the Novel").
