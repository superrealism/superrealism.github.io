The Tale of the Heike

The sound of the bell of Gion Shōja echoes the impermanence of all things. The hue of the sāla flowers declares that all who flourish must fall. The proud endure not, but vanish like a dream on a spring night; the mighty fall at last, to be as dust before the wind.

The opening invocation, ringing out the theme of impermanence that runs through the entire work. Passed down through the generations as heikyōku, chanted by blind biwa priests, it is a masterpiece among masterpieces, engraved deep in the Japanese heart together with its aesthetic of glory and ruin.

The fan flew up into the air, spun around once, twice, and then dropped swiftly into the sea.

The scene at the Battle of Yashima in which Nasu no Yoichi shoots through the fan held up as a target on a Taira boat. One of the famous scenes of The Tale of the Heike, in which both the Minamoto and the Taira praised his superb archery.

The saying that whoever was not of the Taira was not even human was received as though it were only natural; and when it came to the “Rokuhara style,” everyone, without exception, fell into line — down to the fold of one's cap and the way one wore one's robes, all imitating the Taira clan.

A scene depicting the Taira clan at the height of their glory. The arrogance behind the original line — that whoever did not belong to this house was less than human — quietly prepares the ruin to come.

“There is a capital beneath the waves as well. Come, let us go.”

The words the Nun of the Second Rank spoke as she plunged into the sea holding the child Emperor Antoku at the Battle of Dan-no-ura, where the Taira perished. Known as one of the most tragic scenes in The Tale of the Heike.
