About this work

An early short story Orikuchi Shinobu published in Mizuho in 1917, an attempt at legend-literature leading on to The Book of the Dead. Taking as material the legend of Shuntokumaru — from the nō play Yoroboshi and the sermon-ballad — it draws the strange upbringing of Shintokumaru, son of a dengaku priest of Sumiyoshi. Because of a disease inherited from his ancestors, his father commits him to a pure life and abandons him young, and he goes about the provinces dancing as a youth of rare beauty. That beauty stirs up the women wherever he goes, and leads his fate toward the dark. A product of Orikuchi's folklorist imagination, trying to tell the original form of a legend as fiction.

Summary

Shintokumaru's father, the monk Shinkichi, was a dengaku (ritual field-dance) performer who had come from Sumiyoshi. Though he had originally taken religious vows and become a monk at a temple, it was said that on account of a certain woman he had fallen from that path and thrown in his lot with the wandering dengaku hijiri, the itinerant holy men. In the blood of Shinkichi and Shintoku lurked an illness inherited from their ancestors. To atone for his sin—or perhaps to put an end, in his own generation, to this disease that clung so stubbornly to his bloodline—the father left the young Shintoku with the words: "Live a pure life." When Shintoku was still nine, his father, having shown him the eerie swelling on his back one night at an inn on the outskirts of Nara, vanished before morning. Abandoned by his father, Shintoku was taken in and raised as a child performer by the troupe of dengaku hōshi attached to Sumiyoshi's Jingūji temple, the Uryūno troupe. Among this company it was customary for boys to have their heads shaved at eleven or twelve, but Shintoku alone was left unshaven until he was seventeen. His slender face, eyebrows soft as a woman's, teeth beautiful as shells spilling from red lips—so rare was his beauty that his master, the monk Gennai, could not bear to shave his head, and put it off, year after year. In the provinces the troupe toured each year, this beautiful young man became famous—people said, "Surely this is what Ushiwaka must have looked like"—and women made much of him. One year, at an inn in Seki in Ise, after Shintoku performed a perilous umbrella dance, the younger sister of the wealthy lord of Seki lost her heart to him, and, taking a servant girl as her companion, slipped out of her parents' house to follow the troupe. At a loss, Shintoku told his master, and Gennai sent the two women away, then that night disciplined Shintoku until dawn. The next morning, his fellow disciples found Shintoku standing dazed beneath the wisteria trellis, his head shaved blue-green, bowed low. Around the sorrow of a boy loved for his beauty and bound by that same beauty, Orikuchi retells, in the form of a novel, the primal shape of the Shuntokumaru legend. At the close is appended the author's own note that he had used the novel as a form for the study of legend.

The opening (original text)

Shintokumaru's father was a dengaku performer who had come out of Sumiyoshi. But he is no longer alive. Shintoku sometimes calls to mind the scene of his parting from that father. Shintoku was nine at the time. The children of dengaku hōshi, who made their living the whole year round by touring, apart from the sacred rice-planting rites at Sumiyoshi, were taken on journeys of two or three leagues from the time they could just barely toddle along on their own, and in his ninth year Shintoku was taken along with his father's troupe, crossing Yamato, on through Iga and Ise, for a performance of the rice-planting Noh.

Shintokumaru by Orikuchi Shinobu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Shuntokumaru" is a later phonetic rendering, and I believe that "Shintokumaru" is, after all, the correct form. As for the character in Shintokumaru's name that is read "doku," I would ask that it be read not as the voiced "doku" but as the unvoiced "toku." That said, the title of the Noh play Yoroboshi hints at the origin of this tale, and I believe it shows, too, the precious twilight glow that the legend of the wealthy lord of Takayasu casts across the history of Japanese song and drama.

Shintokumaru by Orikuchi Shinobu (Aozora Bunko)
