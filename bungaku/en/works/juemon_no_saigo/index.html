About this work

A novella Tayama Katai wrote for publication in 1902. An early work before The Quilt, strongly influenced by Turgenev and a forerunner of naturalism. Set in a small village in the mountains of Shinshū, it draws the successive acts of arson by the dissolute Jūemon, who bares his teeth at the order of the village, and his destruction. It renders in Jūemon, a child of nature, the ungovernable passions of human beings and of nature itself, and closes with the sentence that nature returned in the end to nature: a work marking Katai's starting point.

Summary

At a gathering of five or six men, talk turns to a novel by Turgenev, and one of them begins to speak. He once met, he says, out in the countryside, a man who seemed to have stepped straight out of A Sportsman's Sketches — a creature of pure, untamed natural force — and the encounter had moved him deeply. "I" travel to visit that mountain village once more, after six years, relying on two students from Shinshū (Nagano) whom I had grown close to in Tokyo. It is a peaceful little village of about a hundred households, overlooking the rushing waters of the Otani River and Mount Kōsha — but the village is now gripped by a string of arsons, unheard of in decades, and no one can sleep soundly at night. The culprit is known to all: Jūemon, a dissolute man of forty-two or forty-three, shunned by the villagers for his bad conduct some five or six years before. He keeps, as his common-law wife, a girl raised wild on the Iizuna plain, quick and fierce as an animal, and since he never sets the fires with his own hand but only commands the girl to do it, he cannot be caught even though everyone knows he is guilty. In this beautiful, peaceful village, an ungovernable passion of man and nature alike has come bursting forth — and "I" see in it the same fierce whirlpool of human life that never ceases even deep in the mountains. In time, Jūemon and the girl meet violent and untimely deaths, and their graves are raised at a temple, where the villagers now and then leave flowers and incense. "Gentlemen, nature has returned, in the end, to nature!" — under the clear influence of Turgenev, this early masterwork of Tayama Katai's naturalism depicts the ruin of a "child of nature" who bared his fangs against the order of the village.

Characters

  • Jūemon　A dissolute man of forty-two or forty-three, shunned by the villagers for his bad conduct. Using an animal-like girl as his instrument, he sets a string of fires in the village, baring his fangs, as a "child of nature," against the village's order — and meets a violent and untimely end
  • The girl　A girl raised wild on the Iizuna plain, quick and fierce as an animal. Becomes Jūemon's common-law wife, and sets the fires at his command
  • I　The narrator, who visits the mountain village relying on students from Shinshū, and hears and witnesses the affair of Jūemon

The opening (original text)

At a certain gathering where five or six people had come together, the talk turned, by some chance or other, to the works of the Russian novelist I. S. Turgenev, and a good deal of interesting commentary passed back and forth on the fate of Rudin, on the character of Bazarov, and the like — when a certain man suddenly leaned forward and said, "Speaking of Turgenev puts me in mind of something. I once met, out in the countryside, a man who might have stepped straight out of A Sportsman's Sketches, and it moved me tremendously."

Jūemon no saigo by Tayama Katai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I still keep up with the people of that village even now, and just the other day, as it happened, one of the village's more adventurous sorts ran off and came to call at my house, so I asked him whether the village had been peaceful since. "No, nothing like that happens anymore," he said. "If a thing like that kept on happening, the village couldn't go on. Luckily enough, the harvests have been good ever since, and the village is better off now than it was in those days." And he told me, too, that the graves of Jūemon and the girl now stood at a temple, and that the villagers left flowers and incense there from time to time. Gentlemen, nature has returned, in the end, to nature!
