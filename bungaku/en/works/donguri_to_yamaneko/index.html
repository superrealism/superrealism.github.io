About this work

The opening story of the collection The Restaurant of Many Orders, published at the author's own expense in December 1924. Invited by a strange postcard, the boy Ichirō walks deep into the mountains and attends a trial held by a wildcat over which of the acorns is the greatest. The verdict — that the most stupid, the most muddled, the one that amounts to nothing at all, is the greatest — crystallizes Kenji's values with a good deal of humour, and the story is among the best known of his Ihatov tales.

Summary

One Saturday evening a strange postcard arrives for Ichirō from the wildcat, written in a very poor hand: "There will be a troublesome trial, so please come." Beside himself with delight, Ichirō sets off the next morning up along the valley stream into the deep mountains, asking the chestnut tree, the whistling waterfall, the mushroom band and the squirrel which way the wildcat has gone. On the grass under the alder trees some three hundred golden acorns have gathered, and for three days they have been quarreling over which of them is the greatest — the one with the pointed head, or the round one, or the big one, or the tall one — until the wildcat, presiding as judge, is at his wits' end. Ichirō whispers in his ear: the one who is the most foolish, the most muddled, the one who is no good at all, is the greatest of all. When the wildcat delivers that judgment, the acorns fall dead silent. Impressed, the wildcat asks Ichirō to be an honorary judge, and asks whether he may write "You are hereby summoned" on his postcards from now on; Ichirō laughs and says no. Sent home in the mushroom carriage with a measure of golden acorns for a present, Ichirō never received a postcard from the wildcat again.

Characters

  • Ichirō　The boy invited by the wildcat's postcard
  • The wildcat　The judge, at his wits' end over the acorns' trial
  • The carriage groom　The wildcat's man, one-eyed and a poor hand at writing
  • The acorns　More than three hundred of them, quarreling over which is the greatest

The opening (original text)

A strange postcard came to Ichirō's house one Saturday evening. Mr. Kaneta Ichiro September 19 You are in good helth, wich is fine. Tomorow there will be a trublesom trial, so pleese come. Do not bring no fire arms. Yamaneko, respectfuly That was all. The writing was very poor, and the ink so gritty that it came off on the fingers. But Ichirō was so delighted that he could hardly bear it.

The Acorns and the Wildcat by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

As the carriage went on, the light of the acorns grew fainter and fainter, and by the time it stopped they had turned into ordinary brown acorns. And the wildcat's yellow surcoat, and the groom, and the mushroom carriage all vanished at once, and Ichirō was standing in front of his own house holding a measure full of acorns. After that, no more postcards came signed "Yamaneko, respectfully." Ichirō still thinks now and then that he might as well have told him he could write "You are hereby summoned" after all.

Famous passages

“Very well. Silence, all of you. Here is my verdict. Whichever among you is the least splendid, the most foolish, the most hopeless, the most utterly worthless, the one who seems to have his head squashed flat — he is the most splendid of all.”

The verdict the wildcat, coached by Ichirō, hands down to the acorns squabbling over which of them is grandest. Turning the very yardstick of value upside down, this scene shows Kenji's thought at its most direct.
