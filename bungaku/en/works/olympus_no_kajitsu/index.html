About this work

Tanaka Hidemitsu's representative work, published in "Bungakukai" in 1940. Built on the author's own voyage to the Los Angeles Olympics as a rower, it is a novel of youth in which a faint love for Kumamoto Akiko, a woman track athlete, is set down as a letter written ten years afterward. The deck of the Taiyō Maru, a Japanese team that spends its days between training and practical jokes, and the one thing he could never bring himself to say. A pure and entirely one-sided love in which almost nothing happens, pursued in a clumsy, comic, honest hand. It received the Iketani Shinzaburō Prize and has been read on across the generations.

Summary

"Aki-chan. Even to call you that has come to feel absurd" — Sakamoto Shigemichi, "I," now twenty-eight with a wife and a child, takes from the purse he bought in Los Angeles the dried apricot he was given on board the Taiyō Maru, throws it out into the garden, and begins to write. Nearly ten years ago, at twenty, he was on a ship bound for the Olympics as a rower. He was a timid giant of a man who crawled about looking for the suit he had been given only the night before they sailed and lost, and whom the older men at the training camp called "Big Osaka" and teased. On the ship crossing the Pacific he met Kumamoto Akiko, of the women's track team. Games on deck, practice, horseplay, and a feeling that swelled only inside his chest. Without ever really talking to her, the competition ends and the day of return comes. He sent the photograph taken at Yokohama to her dormitory, and spent a summer doing nothing but waiting for an answer to his letters. Day after day he walked home from the shore of Sagami as if praying, watching the sunset; the answer never came. Afterward, by way of the left-wing movement, arrest, and recantation, "I" became a literary young man, and hears news of his old companions on the wind. There were nights when he tore up the manuscript he had begun.

Characters

  • Sakamoto Shigemichi (I)　A rower who competed at the Los Angeles Olympics. The writer of the memoir, twenty at the time
  • Kumamoto Akiko (Aki-chan)　A woman track athlete who crossed to America on the same ship. The object of "my" feeling
  • Mori　Stroke and captain of the crew. He drops his delegation badge before they sail and causes an uproar
  • Nakamura　An athlete on the same expedition, who later brings news of Akiko

1 Aki-chan. Even to call you that has come to feel absurd. Miss Kumamoto Akiko. You too must be close to thirty by now. I am twenty-eight. I have taken a wife already, and we have a child. I heard on the wind recently that you are teaching gymnastics at a girls' school in Kyushu.

The ending (original text)

I met Tōkai at the front last year. I hear that Satō, our reserve, was killed in action. At the front, on a bright moonlit night when I had made up my mind to it, I suddenly wrote you a letter; and still no answer came. Did you, I wonder, ever care for me at all?
