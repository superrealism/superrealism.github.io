About this work

A short story Dazai published in Bungakukai in 1939. The skin of the narrator, married at twenty-eight to an older and taciturn husband, breaks out one day all over in a red rash. Her fear and shame at the ugly eruption, and the wavering of her feeling for her husband, are set down closely in the first person, in the woman's-monologue manner. A slight change in the skin invades the whole of a self at once — a keenly felt drawing of a woman's self-consciousness and of how she is held up all the same by her husband's unchanging love.

Summary

"I," twenty-eight years old, secretly feel grateful that my older, taciturn, sincere husband took me for his wife, plain and unremarkable as I am. One day I find a rash the size of an azuki bean just below my left breast, and — perhaps because I scrubbed it too hard at the bathhouse — the red eruption spreads, before I know it, from my chest to my stomach, my back, and my neck, over my whole body. Looking at my own ugly, naked body in the mirror, I am overtaken by an anxious feeling, as though I have become someone other than myself, cut off from the world. My husband examines my skin carefully, treats me with tenderness, and takes me to the doctor. As this small change in my skin stirs up something deep within my heart, I come to find myself quietly held up by my taciturn husband's unchanging kindness. Then, on the day the rash begins to heal, as I hold my hands up to the sunlight, my husband asks, "Are you glad?" and I feel ashamed.

Characters

  • I　A twenty-eight-year-old wife. The narrator, suffering from a rash spreading over her whole body
  • That person　My older, taciturn husband. Watches over my body and treats me with tenderness

The opening (original text)

A single eruption, the size of an azuki bean, suddenly appeared below my left breast, and looking closer, I saw small red eruptions scattered all around it too, as though a fine mist had been sprayed over the skin; but at the time it did not itch or bother me at all. Feeling annoyed by it, I scrubbed under my breast hard with a towel in the bath, hard enough to graze the skin. That, it seems, was the wrong thing to do.

Hifu to kokoro by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I held both my hands up to the sunlight again and again, looking at them. "Are you glad?" At those words I felt ashamed.
