About this work

A long detective novel Unno Jūza serialized in Shin Seinen in 1936. The narrator, a writer of detective stories whose hobby is walking late at night, meets the mysterious boss who rules the night city — the midnight mayor — and is drawn into a conspiracy over a golden key that shakes the city government. Turning on the secret of a man who lives a double life with different faces by day and by night, it is the author's central detective romance, richly coloured with urban darkness and fantasy.

Summary

Asama Shinjūrō, "I," a writer of detective fiction newly appointed to a post as a judicial officer, takes supreme pleasure in his late-night walks. On a snowy night, in the back streets of T City, I witness two men carrying a corpse with a dagger driven into its back, and am struck down for my trouble. When I come to, the body has vanished, leaving behind only a broken pocket watch and, oddly, three nickel coins that have become magnetized. Fleeing the pursuing police, I am given shelter in an underground den by a bearded vagrant who nonetheless rules the night city — the "Night Mayor." Skilled in both science and deduction, the Mayor entrusts the solving of the mystery to Hayami Rintarō, a scientist of the city. Behind the case lies a hidden struggle over the governance of T City: the upright Mayor Takaya, and Dōzaka Saburō, a powerful figure in the city council who wields allegations of an improper land sale to try to impeach him. At the heart of the struggle lies the whereabouts of "T City's golden key," which opens the safe in city hall. Dōzaka had had the key stolen, then silenced the man who stole it (the very murder I witnessed that night, the dagger drawn to the magnet), and now keeps the key on his own person, never letting it go. I and Hayami put Dōzaka's mansion to sleep by pumping poison gas in from beneath the floor, and steal the key back from behind his bulletproof vest. But the city council session the next day descends into chaos — Dōzaka had planted a powerful magnet inside the safe and made off with the documents. Events cascade into Mayor Takaya's suicide, and a murder-suicide attempt by Masumi, the woman whose brother Dōzaka had killed. After it is all over, I, feeling responsible, resign my post and visit the cave of the "Night Mayor," only to find it empty — nothing left behind but a note and a false beard. The true identity of the "Night Mayor," the ruler of the night city, was none other than Superintendent-General Kurokawachi himself, wearing his daytime face.

Characters

  • I (Asama Shinjūrō)　A writer of detective fiction, newly appointed as a judicial officer. Takes late-night walks as his pastime, and the narrator drawn into the case
  • The Night Mayor　A bearded vagrant who rules the night city. Skilled in science and deduction; his true identity is Superintendent-General Kurokawachi
  • Hayami Rintarō　A scientist of the city. Aids the "Night Mayor" with grotesque inventions such as a poison-gas syringe
  • Superintendent-General Kurokawachi　A capable superintendent who works in secret on the Mayor's side. Wears two faces, one by day and one by night
  • Dōzaka Saburō　A powerful figure in the city council. The mastermind who tries to impeach the mayor over allegations of an improper land sale
  • Masumi　The woman I love. Her brother is killed by Dōzaka, and she carries out her revenge
  • Oteru　A helpful woman who comes and goes at Hayami's tower

The opening (original text)

It was on the night of March twenty-ninth — a night bitterly, wretchedly cold, spring in name alone — that I first met the "Night Mayor." The radio weather bulletin reported that a high-pressure system that had lain over the Chinese mainland was moving east and closing in on the Japan Sea coast, that the northwest wind was strengthening, and, adding a most unwelcome forecast, that T City's region would likely see snow again within the next two or three days. That broadcast, too, before long, cut off abruptly, and one by one the domestic stations bid their goodnights and fell silent, their signals fading from the pitch-black, ether-laden night sky — until at last there remained only the voice of a woman announcer at the Nanking broadcasting station, speaking on, slowly, sorrowfully, in some foreign tongue.

Shin'ya no shichō by Unno Jūza (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

For a while I stood there, dazed, pressing the "Night Mayor's" false beard and letter to my chest. I had had some inkling of it before now, but so this was it after all — the "Night Mayor" had been none other than Superintendent-General Kurokawachi's own figure on night duty; or rather, I suppose it would be more accurate to say that Superintendent-General Kurokawachi had been the daytime face of the "Night Mayor." But to have been cast off so abruptly by the "Night Mayor," now that I too had become a vagrant, left me terribly lonely. And it was not only that I had been cast off by the "Night Mayor" — I came to understand clearly, afterward, that I had been cast off by his whole band as well. After that, late at night, I visited Building Thirteen in Marunouchi more times than I can count, but never again did I find that towering spire I had once seen, nor could I even locate the back passage; and, needless to say, I never once met Hayami Rintarō, nor Oteru, again. Even now, I sometimes set out on a whim, walking the late-night streets with no particular destination. At such times I always feel that tonight, surely, I will run into the "Night Mayor" by chance — but, unhappily, I have never once, since that time, crossed paths again with either him or his band. And so the many mysteries they left behind remain, even now, exactly as they were.

Shin'ya no shichō by Unno Jūza (Aozora Bunko)
