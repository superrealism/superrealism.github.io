About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in King in 1940. The young Okada Toranosuke comes to seek out Bessho Kuranosuke, an unknown swordsman, meaning to master the way of the sword. But the old man has put down the sword and become a farmer. Sword and hoe — what one takes up may differ, but the way is one and the same. A piece drawing the essence of a discipline through the meeting with a true teacher.

Summary

On a scorching afternoon at the end of August, Okada Toranosuke stops, at a loss, at a fork in the road. A wasteland overgrown with weeds as tall as a man, the Meguro river swollen and overflowing — he has come here having heard that the swordsman Bessho Kuranosuke lives somewhere in this area. Asking the way of a girl passing by, Toranosuke at last reaches the place, and finds a single old man silently working the soil, spear and sword set aside, hoe in hand. Toranosuke, meaning to master the deepest secrets of the sword, asks to become Kuranosuke's disciple. But the old man will not teach him swordsmanship. "The way is one," he says. "A sword and a hoe — what one takes up may differ, but the way is one and the same, only the one. I'll teach you nothing at all from here on. Farming is hard work, you know." That one remark pierces straight through Toranosuke's chest. Toranosuke, who until this day had been trapped inside the shell of "martial technique," now felt, in a rush, as though he had broken free of it entirely. The way is one, reaching out in every direction, without end — and he felt that he himself had, without question, come to stand at one edge of that great way. A piece of Yamamoto's, drawing, through the meeting with a true teacher, the essence of a "way" that runs through both sword and hoe alike.

Characters

  • Okada Toranosuke　A young man seeking the way of the sword
  • Bessho Kuranosuke　A swordsman who has given up the sword to become a farmer
  • Nami　Kuranosuke's granddaughter

The opening (original text)

Okada Toranosuke came to a fork in the road and stopped, wiping his pale forehead, damp and beaded with sweat, with the back of his hand, and, tilting up his hat, looked left and right in bewilderment. "I have come here having heard that Master Bessho Kuranosuke's residence is somewhere around this area. If you happen to know it, would you tell me the way?"

Kuranosuke rusu by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"The way is one," he said. "...A sword and a hoe — what one takes up may differ, but the way is one and the same, only the one. I'll teach you nothing at all from here on. Farming is hard work, you know." "Master..." Toranosuke, his eyes brimming with tears, looked up at the old man's face without so much as a blink. He had found a teacher, a true teacher worthy of respect; the way ahead of him was set. From the shell of "martial technique," which had bound him tightly, day and night, until this very day, he now felt, in a rush, that he had broken entirely free.

Kuranosuke rusu by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
