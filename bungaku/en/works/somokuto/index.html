About this work

"Push on and push on, and still the green mountains" — the collection the wandering haiku poet Taneda Santōka compiled himself from a lifetime's work. From taking Buddhist orders through his years of begging on the road, wandering San'yō, San'in, Shikoku and Kyūshū, it can be followed with short prose passages for each section. Free-form verses with neither season word nor five-seven-five mark out a life that was walking itself. Published in the year he died, and in effect his last collection.

Summary

"I offer this book before the spirit of my mother, who hastened to her death while she was still young" — with that dedication to the mother who drowned herself when he was ten, the collection begins. The first chapter, "The Begging Bowl," opens where in the fourteenth year of Taishō he took orders and became keeper of the Mitori Kannon hall in Higo. "The pines all droop their branches — namu Kanzeon." The next year Santōka, carrying a bewilderment he had no way of untying, set out on a wandering life of begging, and there he found the poems of his life: "Pushing in, and pushing in, and still green mountains," "Under the blazing sky, going about begging." On the news of the death of his friend Ozaki Hōsai he answered with "A crow caws — I too am alone," and went on, "In the midst of life and death the snow keeps falling." After that, wandering without aim along the Sanyō and Sanin roads, through Shikoku and Kyushu, he made such poems as "A straight road, and lonely," "Drizzling rain — and I am not dying," "Softly drunk, the leaves come down." Travel and drink and solitude, with a little human kindness slanting in between — and as the chapters go on, the walk moves to the Shikoku pilgrimage, to a journey in Shinano, and to a quiet life at the Issō hermitage in Matsuyama. "After all it comes to knowing oneself. I shall keep to my own foolishness." The manuscript closes with the note "February, the fifteenth year of Shōwa, at the Issō hermitage at the foot of Mount Miyuki"; and in October of that year Santōka ended a wandering life of fifty-eight years.

I offer this book before the spirit of my mother, who hastened to her death while she was still young The Begging Bowl In February of the fourteenth year of Taishō I at last took orders and became keeper of the Mitori Kannon hall in a remote village of Higo; and it was truly a life of dwelling alone in the hills and woods — quiet if one called it quiet, lonely if one thought it lonely. The ending (original text)

A crow flies off — let me cross the water For the first time in three years I have put the manuscript in order (July of the thirteenth year of Shōwa to September of the fourteenth) and picked out some seventy-two poems. Famous passages

Pushing on and on, still the green mountains

The representative haiku of Santōka, who set out on a wandering, mendicant journey. Discarding both seasonal word and fixed form, only the endless green mountains stand in his way, however far he walks.

My own retreating figure — does the winter rain fall on it now?

A verse composed as though watching his own retreating back, on his begging journey, vanish into a passing winter shower. It is a representative shape of solitude in free-verse haiku.

A straight road — how lonely.

A haiku from his mendicant wanderings. The clear view down a single straight road only sharpens the loneliness of the way ahead. The colloquial ring of free-verse haiku stays in the heart.

A crow caws — I, too, am alone.

A haiku headed “In response to a verse by the late Hōsai.” Answering Ozaki Hōsai's “Even when I cough, alone,” it sets his own solitude beside the cry of a crow — a haiku linking the two masters of free verse.

When I walk, a cuckoo; when I hurry, a cuckoo.

A haiku from the roads of Shinano. Whether he walks or hurries, a cuckoo calls: the repetition of the same words layers the rhythm of his steps over the bird's song and conveys a lighthearted side of his travels.

There is no road but this one — spring snow falling.

A haiku of resolving that this road is the only one for him. In the figure walking on through falling spring snow lies the resolve of a man who has accepted a wandering life.
