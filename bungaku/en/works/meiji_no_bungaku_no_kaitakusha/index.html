About this work

Tsubouchi Shōyō, who raised the novel — despised as light entertainment — into a work of the spirit. Uchida Roan praises frankly the immeasurable force The Character of Modern Students and The Essence of the Novel had for the young of that day, and his services in theatre reform and at Waseda and the Literary Society. An essay honouring not so much the works themselves as his services as the epoch-maker who opened a new road.

Summary

An essay in which Uchida Roan praises the services of Tsubouchi Shōyō as the greatest opener of new ground in the literature of Meiji. Judging frankly at the outset that Shosei katagi is no masterpiece but a poor thing, he explains that even so, for a Bachelor of Letters to come forward and sign his own name boldly to a novel — a form until then treated as mere popular writing — astonished the world like a thunderclap out of a clear sky, and that the authority of a Bachelor of Letters in those days was beyond reckoning. Shōsetsu shinzui made the world bow to him; and both Futabatei and Ozaki Kōyō, Roan holds, were moved by Shōyō's fame to throw themselves into the literary world. That society came to recognize the novel and the drama, despised as popular writing, as a work of the spirit contributing to civilization, was entirely Shōyō's doing. He cites further the challenge to the theatrical world with Kiri hitoha, the flourishing of Waseda Bungaku, and the success of the Literary Society, and argues that Shōyō's greatness lies less in the value of the works themselves than in his having opened one barred gate after another into closed territory. That the iconoclasts of recent years could arise at all is thanks to Shōyō's having opened new roads — and so he concludes that Tsubouchi is a great epoch-maker of the literature of Meiji.

Characters

  • Tsubouchi Shōyō　Haru no Ya Oboro, Bachelor of Letters. The author of Shosei katagi and Shōsetsu shinzui, who raised the novel from popular writing to a work of the spirit. The opener of the literature of Meiji
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who had been helped by Shōyō since about the twenty-second year of Meiji
  • Futabatei Shimei　Said to have written fiction moved by Shōyō's name. He is also called the true forerunner of the novel
  • Ozaki Kōyō　The great talent said to have been fired with literary ambition by Shōyō's fame

The opening (original text)

Everyone knows what Tsubouchi has done. There is no need to say it particularly; it is enough to say that he was the greatest opener of new ground in the literature of Meiji. Such men as Fukuchi Ōchi and Suematsu Kenchō were also pioneers of the founding period; but they only put in the hoe and turned the soil over, and the man who truly labored and made a virgin land where no foot had trodden into fine rich fields was Tsubouchi. To speak plainly, Tsubouchi's first work, Shosei katagi, is no masterpiece. Even if I declare it publicly a poor thing, Tsubouchi will certainly not be angry.

Meiji no bungaku no kaitakusha by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Since iconoclasts swagger everywhere nowadays, no one will say it openly to Tsubouchi, who is their senior; but privately there may be some who say that Tsubouchi's literature has nothing to do with them. Yet had Tsubouchi not opened the road of the new literature, the literature of today might not have advanced so far; and that so many iconoclasts like these gentlemen have arisen is, quite simply, Tsubouchi's doing. Tsubouchi is a great epoch-maker of the literature of Meiji.

Meiji no bungaku no kaitakusha by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
