About this work

A book Inoue Enryō built on his Hundred Talks of Monsters of 1898, adding the new investigations he collected in twenty years of travelling the country. He introduces a hundred and thirty cases — fox possession, ghosts, balls of fire, houses that creak, strange sounds and lights — in plain colloquial prose that children could read at home or at school, and gives each a rational explanation. As he says himself, the book comes from a wish to settle the anxious minds of ordinary people, and Enryō the enlightener is visible throughout.

Summary

Enryō, who wrote A Hundred Talks of Monsters and its sequel in the thirty-first year of Meiji, met with a reception beyond his hopes; but dissatisfied that they leaned upon the books of the ancients and upon tales of antiquity, he left them out of print. In the twenty years that followed, traveling about the whole country, he gathered several hundred items of material — accounts of monsters at first hand from those who had experienced them, reports from members of his society, newspaper cuttings sent by well-wishers in the provinces, and cases he investigated on the spot himself. From these he chose a hundred and thirty and put them before the world afresh as The True Nature of Ghosts. Stories of being bewitched by a fox, of seeing a ghost, of a ball of fire that flew, of a house where a noise comes from the ceiling, of a Buddhist image that moves, of a prophecy that came true — to these vivid reports from every province Enryō gives his answers one after another, from the points of view of illusion, of suggestion, of preconception, of natural phenomena and of human contrivance. Monsters and superstition are closely bound up, and eight or nine parts in ten of all monsters arise from superstition: such is Enryō's judgment. In closing he records that this work is no pastime of an idle man but comes from a genuine wish to quiet the deluded minds of the ignorant, and notes the honor of its having been shown to the Emperor through the Imperial Household Minister; and he vows to search out the depths of the study of monsters still further.

The opening (original text)

Introductory remarks Long ago, in the thirty-first year of Meiji, I wrote A Hundred Talks of Monsters, and then made a sequel to it; and beyond my hopes they were welcomed by the world and went through edition after edition. But some years ago the remaining copies were entirely exhausted, and for a long while I have had to decline orders for them. Afterward I thought of reprinting them again; but since the contents of the book cite the writings of the ancients and hand down tales of antiquity in many places, I considered that to reprint it just as it was would not be interesting, and so it has remained out of print until today.

Obake no shōtai by Inoue Enryō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The above may look like too much praise of the uses of the study of monsters, and some may think it like a quack's list of virtues; but my purpose was not from the first a pastime undertaken idly by a man with time on his hands. It came entirely from a genuine wish to settle the deluded minds of the ignorant; and it is from a single-minded desire that people should not mistake that purpose that I have set out its uses in this way.

Obake no shōtai by Inoue Enryō (Aozora Bunko)
