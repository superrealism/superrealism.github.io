About this work

Oda Sakunosuke's central postwar work, published in the magazine Ningen in 1946. In the burnt ruins of Osaka, the narrator, a novelist, watches the people swarming the black market and the confusion of the defeated country, and turns over his own method of writing. It begins with a visit on a December night from a pitiful old schoolmaster who has tried his hand at black marketeering, and goes on to memories of the bewitching proprietress of the stand-up bar Dice, which he frequented before the war, and to the plan for a new novel called "The Ten-Sen Geisha." Fusing autobiography, criticism and fiction, and rejecting the established I-novel in pursuit of what the novel might be: a characteristic problem work by Oda of the Buraiha.

Summary

On a frozen night in December, in burnt-out Osaka, an old schoolteacher wearing an old-fashioned air-raid hood comes calling on "I," a novelist. Saying he has come to "tell of the present state of the world a little," the old teacher, unable to support a family of seven on his meager salary, had tried his hand at black-market dealing, and now, sniffling through a runny nose, recounts the pitiful, hopeless way it all came to nothing for him. In postwar Japan, he says, only black marketeers and women seem to prosper — the illicit slaughter of cattle, a family's failed trip all the way to Kyoto to sell off the cotton cloth won as a lottery prize, a woman who sells herself for a single rice ball, a Gion geisha who earns a hundred thousand yen a month. Watching this world of confusion and decadence, "I," stuck for what to write, find my thoughts drifting back to memories of before the war. There was once a stand-up bar called "Dice" in Shimizu-chō. Its madam, a former geisha of Sōemon-chō, was a stylish, modern, and sensuous woman, and I recall, with both fondness and bitterness, the awkward day she invited me to the planetarium, and the night I visited her bar after one of my books had been banned from sale. Through an argument with a young man, a onetime leftist turned man of letters, "I" speak of the decadence of our generation, which can no longer believe in any ideology or system, and toy with a theory of the novel that trusts nothing but sensation. Then, walking the back streets of Osaka at night, I let a new novel take shape in my mind, one idea building on another — a tale, reminiscent of Saikaku's "The Life of an Amorous Woman," to be called "The Ten-Sen Geisha": the story of a woman who falls to become a ten-sen geisha, and the man who pursues her to the very end, sinking down together with her. A piece that boldly weaves together autobiography, criticism, and fiction to depict both the shifting face of Osaka across defeat in war and the secret workings of a novelist's own creation.

The opening (original text)

At the bottom of the frozen night, a white wind ran white, and the sound knocking at the rain shutters was the sound of cold itself. Rising to use the privy, I saw the shadow of a garden branch shaking violently against the windowpane — it was the wind of the last month of the year. The visitor who came through that wind wearing an old-fashioned air-raid hood, once he took the hood off, wore the face of that same bitter month. His eyes, pale and swollen, ringed with dark, conspicuous circles, blinked anxiously and bloodshot, and for a man who had come to "tell of the present state of the world a little," he seemed strangely restless, unable to settle.

Sesō by Oda Sakunosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

As her retreating figure, wrapped shivering in a purple meisen kimono, vanished beyond the sliding door, I found myself thinking — if I were to write it, it would be that younger sister... — and I sat listening to the sound of the white wind, blowing in across the burnt-out ruins, striking against the glass of the window.

Sesō by Oda Sakunosuke (Aozora Bunko)
