About this work

A short story Okamoto Kanoko published in Shin Nippon in 1938, the year she died: a work of her last period. The husband, who studies the history of manners and goes out along the Tōkaidō in search of the ways of travel in earlier times, and the narrator, who grew up learning painting from a father versed in court usages. Travelling the Tōkaidō as a couple, they turn over quietly the business of human life, handed down through generations and passing away. A richly lyrical piece looking at what beats under an ordinary life.

Summary

"My" husband specialized in the history of customs, and, with a particular interest in the manners and habits of travel in ages past, had already set out on his surveys of the Tōkaidō in his student days at the First Higher School, back at the very start of the Taishō era. "I" am a woman raised learning painting under my father, an amateur scholar of court and military etiquette who had lived through the turmoil of the Restoration — able to copy fragments of picture scrolls, or sketch the neck-guard of a helmet, but never able to draw a single picture of my own invention. My husband, an eccentric young man given to rummaging through old, moth-eaten books and family relics, was in time praised by my father as "a rare and admirable young man these days," and the two of us were joined together. The story turns on the days this couple spent traveling the Tōkaidō, and, weaving in the old customs of travel handed down at the post towns and the lingering images of the people who once lived there, quietly widens its thoughts toward the workings of human life, handed down across generation after generation, and passing away in turn. Through the train window the mountains of the Ise road come into view, and in the fields, with winter drawing near, daikon radishes lie spread out to dry, covering the ground. "I" feel that even within a half-life as ordinary as my own, once twenty years have passed, something like a broad pulse can be felt beating within it — a pulse that somehow touches, here and there, the pulse of other people to whom I bear no particular connection. Watching the white hair of my husband, dozing off contentedly, "I" head toward Kuwana with no heart to hurry. A lyrical piece from Kanoko's final years, gazing at the pulse of time and generation flowing beneath the surface of an ordinary half-life.

The opening (original text)

My husband, who specialized in the history of customs, had turned his particular interest toward the manners and habits of travel in ages past, and had first set out on his surveys of the Tōkaidō, it seems, back in his days as a student at the First Higher School, in the very early years of Taishō. I know nothing of that time myself, but I certainly saw, during his university days, how often he went there, and once he even took me along with him. Let me explain, for the sake of clarity, the relationship between my husband and myself: my father, as a boy, had lived through the turmoil of the Restoration, and had become an amateur scholar of court and military etiquette; devoted to this pursuit, he had me, his only daughter, learn painting, so that I might help with his research.

Tōkaidō gojūsan tsugi by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Through the train window the mountains of the Ise road began to come into view. In the fields, with winter drawing near, daikon radishes lay spread out to dry, all around the eaves of farmhouses and along the ridges between the paddies. The sky was clear as glass, and the sun shone down. Swaying with the motion of the train car, I felt that even within a half-life passed as ordinarily as my own, once twenty years had gone by, I could sense something like a broad pulse beating within it — a pulse that somehow touched, here and there, the pulse of other people to whom I bore no particular connection. I headed toward Kuwana with no heart to hurry, thinking of the age of Sakurai and his son, and of the age of my father, and of us, and of our own son in turn. My husband dozed on contentedly. The few white hairs beginning to show at his crown caught the light and gleamed.
