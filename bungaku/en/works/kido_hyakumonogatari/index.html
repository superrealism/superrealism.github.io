About this work

A piece from Okamoto Kidō's collections of ghost stories. On a rainy autumn night in the Kōka era, young samurai on night duty inside the castle of a certain daimyo in Kōzuke hold a hundred-tales gathering. A hundred wicks are set in a lamp covered with blue paper, and after each tale one man goes to put out a single wick — and on that errand the eighty-third of them, Nakahara Budayū, sees a woman in white hanging by the neck against the wall of a dark room. Human or monster? What happened before the men who kept watch until dawn to learn the truth is told in a dry, unhurried hand. A small work in which Kidō's great virtue as a ghost-story writer — never hurrying to explain, leaving only the aftertaste — stands out.

Summary

It happened in the ninth month of the first or second year of Kōka, inside the castle of a certain daimyo in Kōzuke. On an eerie night when the rain would not stop, at the gathering of the young samurai on night duty, Nakahara Budayū, looked up to as the senior man of the company, proposed it: whether or not there are monsters, let us hold a hundred-tales gathering tonight and find out. A hundred wicks are set in a lamp with its mouth covered in blue paper, and the lamp is placed in an inner study five ken away. After each tale one man is to grope his way through the dark rooms, put out a single wick, look into the mirror beside the lamp, and come back. Near the hour of the ox, Nakahara rose as the eighty-third, and on his way back from the study saw something white hanging against the wall of a room he had just passed through. He went back and looked closely: a woman in white, hanged by the neck. Being a stout-hearted man, he said nothing and returned to his place, but when all hundred tales were done and he asked, it turned out that everyone from the seventy-fifth on had seen it, and had kept silent for fear of being laughed at as cowards. When they lit a lamp and looked again, a beautiful woman of eighteen or nineteen hung there in white silk with a sash of white crepe, her hair in disorder. Day broke and the figure did not disappear. Officials came hurrying at last, and it was established that this was Shimakawa, a lady-in-waiting of the inner apartments who was said to be called even to the lord's bedchamber. But when the inner apartments were checked, Shimakawa was there, ill in bed and alive. And when they went back out to the front —

Characters

  • Nakahara Budayū　A samurai looked up to as the senior of the company. He proposes the hundred tales, and as the eighty-third sees the woman in white
  • Kakei Jingoemon　The samurai who rose as the eighty-fourth. Like Nakahara he saw the apparition and said nothing
  • Shimakawa　A lady-in-waiting of the inner apartments, rumored to be called even to the lord's bedchamber
  • Shimoda Jihei　The chief steward of the inner apartments, who goes in to learn whether Shimakawa is safe

The opening (original text)

About eighty years ago — O began, and then laughed at himself. No, it may be further back than that. At any rate it was something that happened in the ninth month of the first or second year of Kōka, inside the castle of a certain daimyo in Kōzuke. On an autumn night the young samurai were keeping night duty. The rain that had begun the day before would not let up, and it was an eerie night. It is the same in every age: on such a night it is the custom for ghost stories to begin.

The ending (original text)

Shimakawa herself recovered for a time and went on serving in the inner apartments as before; but some two months later she again gave out that she was unwell, and while she was shut up in her room she hanged herself one night and died. It was said that the illness she had complained of all along had come from some grudge she bore against another. If that is so, was the white woman of that night no more than a specter of some kind? Or had Shimakawa even then resolved to hang herself, so that her living spirit appeared as a kind of phantom? It is a riddle that will never be solved — so Nakahara Budayū told people in his old age.
