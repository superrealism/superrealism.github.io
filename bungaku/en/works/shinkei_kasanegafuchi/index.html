About this work

It begins when the bannerman Fukami Shinzaemon cuts down the moneylending acupuncturist Minagawa Sōetsu, and the descendants of the two houses are swallowed by karma across the generations. In ninety-seven chapters it tells the doomed love of Sōetsu's daughter Toshiga and the young Shinkichi, her vengeful ghost, and the killings and vendetta at the Kasane pool in Hanyū village in Shimōsa. As the title's pun suggests — on a word meaning "nerves" — it preaches the terror of a karma that still clings in an enlightened age that no longer believes in ghosts. A masterpiece of the ghost stories of San'yūtei Enchō.

Summary

Fukami Shinzaemon, a bannerman, kills Minagawa Sōetsu, an acupuncturist and moneylender, in a scuffle when the man presses him to repay a debt. That killing is the beginning of a chain of cause and effect, and misfortune after misfortune swallows the Fukami and the Minagawa families down through the generations. Toyoshiga, Sōetsu's orphaned daughter, becomes a woman teacher of the Tomimoto ballad, and falls into a forbidden love with her young and handsome pupil Shinkichi; but tormented by jealousy over the difference in their ages and by illness, she dies hating him. Toyoshiga's vengeful spirit clings to Shinkichi, and one after another the women who have to do with him meet violent ends. The story then moves to the Kasanegafuchi at the village of Hanyū in Shimōsa, where people who do not know their own connections kill one another in a chain of karma with no way out; and that chain is settled at last when Sōkichi, whose brother and sister were killed, takes his revenge on the villain Yasuda Ikkaku. In an age of enlightenment that dismisses ghosts as a disorder of the nerves, Enchō tells to the end the terror of a karma from which there is no escape.

Characters

  • Shinkichi　A young man connected with the Fukami house. He becomes Toyoshiga's lover, is haunted by her vengeful spirit, and drives many women to their deaths
  • Toyoshiga　Sōetsu's orphaned daughter, a woman teacher of the Tomimoto ballad, who loves the young Shinkichi and dies of jealousy and illness to become a vengeful spirit
  • Minagawa Sōetsu　An acupuncturist and moneylender, cut down by Fukami Shinzaemon, which begins the chain of karma
  • Fukami Shinzaemon　A bannerman, who kills Sōetsu when pressed to repay his debt
  • O-Rui (Kasane)　A woman caught in the karma of the village of Hanyū in Shimōsa, from whom the Kasanegafuchi takes its name
  • Sōkichi　Who avenges his brother and sister and later succeeds to the headman's name of Sōemon
  • Yasuda Ikkaku　A rōnin who piles crime on crime, and is killed by Sōkichi

The opening (original text)

From today I shall tell you a ghost story; but ghost stories have gone very much out of fashion of late, and there are not many who tell them in the halls any more. That is because it has come to be held that there are no such things as ghosts, and that it is all a disease of the nerves; so that gentlemen of the enlightenment dislike ghost stories. For that reason they were long out of use — and now that we have come to this day, the old-fashioned things seem, on the contrary, to strike the ear as new. Since this is not, of course, a thing you listen to and believe, I have been urged that a ghost story of a somewhat different school might do; and so, under the title Shinkei Kasanegafuchi, here is the sequel to the story of Kasane at the village of Hanyū in the province of Shimōsa — a gruesome tale in which ghosts appear one after another.

Shinkei kasanegafuchi by San'yūtei Enchō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

SŌKICHI: Thank you indeed, wrestler. Yasuda Ikkaku, do you know now what it is to have killed my brother and my sister? TASUKE: Take that, you dog — I am his second! And the two of them simply stabbed at him wildly; and when Ikkaku's breath stopped they both went slack and sat down flat and could not speak for a while. Kasha took Yasuda Ikkaku by the topknot, made a fist and struck him again and again. KASHA: So it was you who cut down my benefactor, my master — you who killed Lady O-Sumi in revenge, you dog! And saying it, he pulled the hair out at the temples. The rest of the gang had all scattered and run, so they reported the matter to the village headman, and the headman reported it further, and when the investigation was made it was found beyond doubt to be a lawful revenge for a brother and a sister. Kasha went back to Edo; Sōkichi, at sixteen, became headman and succeeded to the name of Sōemon, with Tasuke as his guardian. The stone that Kasha used to juggle with was engraved with his name, and it is called the Kasha stone, and remains to this day within the Hōonji in Shimōsa. And with that the happy tale of the Kasanegafuchi is at an end.

Shinkei kasanegafuchi by San'yūtei Enchō (Aozora Bunko)
