About this work

A late short story written at the end of 1937. With the proletarian literature movement crushed by suppression and the colours of war deepening, the narrator, whose income has dried up, takes his two children fishing in an upland village. It is the afternoon of the day his daughter told him the rice bin was empty. On the way home, soaked by cold rain, a father thinks about the state of mind of people who kill themselves with their children, and the children, perhaps sensing it, hurry on ahead in silence. A quiet, bitter piece written by a former standard-bearer of labour literature from the point where daily life itself had cornered him.

Summary

On an autumn evening on the highland, on a levee overlooking one of Japan's three great rapid rivers, 'I' sit with my fishing line cast into the water. About ten ken downstream is my son, a fourth-grader; up on the levee is my daughter, a first-grader. When my daughter comes to tell me the rain has started, I answer, 'Go find somewhere to get out of the rain' — but the levee holds nothing but a single small walnut tree, already stripped of its leaves. My son lays dried grass across the branches for a roof, builds a shelter barely two feet square, and huddles inside it with his sister. — That same day, at noon, as we left the house, my daughter had peered into the rice bin and said, 'Mother — there's no more rice, is there.' I have spent more than three years now, unable to find a way through. By the time it grows too dark to see the fishing hook, 'I' realize that I have been turning over in my mind the state of a parent's heart in the moment just before a family suicide. The rapid current beneath my feet is not the kind of river to be troubled by swallowing ten or a hundred people. On the way home, the children walk with startling speed along the muddy prefectural road, and vanish into the darkness ahead. Not noticing for a full thirty minutes, 'I' conclude that the children must be afraid of the graveyard air that clings to me. Six months now since, from looking at the newspaper accounts of the dead and wounded on the battlefield, I found myself drawn, without quite realizing it, toward the side of death. My daughter, waiting partway up the slope, grabs my hand, saying, 'Shall I pull you up?' and tugs me forward, leading the way. At the strength of that small grip, my heart flashes, just for an instant, a pale and ghostly light. Back home, once the children have shed their wet clothes, eaten supper, and gone to bed, 'I' go out to borrow rice.

Characters

  • I　A father whose income has dried up. Thinking of nothing but life and death, he tries to give his children some amusement that costs no money
  • The boy　A fourth-grade son. Roofs a rain shelter with dried grass, and walks at the head of the dark night road
  • The girl　A first-grade daughter. Tells him the rice bin is empty, and pulls her father's hand up the slope

The opening (original text)

One It was growing dark. The figure of my boy, fishing some ten ken downstream, had begun to blur at the edges in the evening dusk. My girl had been playing up on the levee, but a moment ago she had come to tell me, 'Papa, it's started raining.' 'Go find somewhere to get out of the rain,' I answered, and went on fishing all the same — though the levee held nothing growing on it but a single small walnut tree.

Hisame by Hayama Yoshiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Once the children had finished eating and gone to bed, I went out to borrow rice. The village turns dead as a corpse once nine o'clock strikes at night; even the inn that takes in the odd traveler who happens by falls asleep by ten. The banner celebrating some soldier's departure for the front has already been taken in, and only the bare pole, a few leaves still clinging to its tip, stands there lonely under the eaves. What will tomorrow bring?
