About this work

Koganemaru, a dog whose parents were killed by the fierce tiger Kinbō, king of the deep mountains, is raised by an ox and, grown, takes his revenge with sworn brothers among the hunting dogs. Iwaya Sazanami's landmark original tale, generally taken as the starting point of modern Japanese children's literature.

Summary

King Kinbō, the fierce tiger who rules the deep mountains, is egged on by Chōsui, an old fox, to attack and devour Tsukimaru, the dog kept by the steward of the manor below. Hanase, the bitch who has seen her mate killed before her eyes, is heavy with young and cannot take revenge; she gives birth to one pup, Koganemaru, and dies. Raised by a cow, Botan, and a bull, Bunkaku, Koganemaru grows up, learns who he is and who killed his parents, and sets out on a warrior's pilgrimage to sharpen his fangs. On the way he meets Jūrō, a hunting dog, at a ruined temple, and the two swear brotherhood and join their strength against the enemy. Having beaten off Kinbō's followers, the cunning Chōsui among them, they storm the tiger's cave while he is off his guard with drink, and after a fierce fight they avenge Koganemaru's father and mother at last. Presenting the head to the house of their master, Koganemaru and Jūrō are rewarded, and served faithfully thereafter as watchdogs.

Characters

  • Koganemaru　The hero of the tale. A brown male dog with gold hairs mixed along his back, the orphan of Tsukimaru and Hanase, who sets out to avenge his parents
  • King Kinbō　An aged and fierce tiger who rules the deep mountains, oppressing the beasts and threatening the villages. The killer of Koganemaru's parents
  • Chōsui　A cunning old fox in Kinbō's service, who eggs him on out of a private grudge against Tsukimaru
  • Tsukimaru and Hanase　Koganemaru's father and mother. Tsukimaru is killed by Kinbō, and Hanase dies giving birth to Koganemaru
  • Bunkaku and Botan　The cattle, husband and wife, who raise Koganemaru
  • Jūrō　A hunting dog whom Koganemaru meets at a ruined temple. They swear brotherhood, and he joins the revenge

The opening (original text)

Long ago, deep in a certain mountain, there lived a tiger. How many years and seasons he had passed no one knew; his body was larger than an ordinary calf, his eyes put a hundredfold-polished mirror to shame, his whiskers were like a bundle of needles, and when once he roared his voice shook mountain and valley until the birds seemed ready to drop from the treetops. Since there was not a wolf or a deer in the whole mountain that did not fear and obey him, the tiger grew fiercer still, styled himself King Kinbō, looked down on the multitude of beasts, and became the lord of all the beasts of the mountain. It was the beginning of the first month; spring only in name, for after the heavy snow that had fallen since yesterday, fields and mountains, rocks and trees alike were wrapped in cold cotton, and the wind was hardly to be borne. Kinbō had kept to his cave since morning and was crouching there alone, when Chōsui, the old fox who had long stood in his favor, came along the cliff path, pushing his way through the snow, and reached the mouth of the cave at last. Brushing off the snow, he edged in, laid his forepaws down politely, and said, "With this great snow since yesterday you cannot get outside and must keep to your cave; you must be sadly bored." And Kinbō raised himself and said, "Is it Chōsui? You do well to come."

The ending (original text)

Bunkaku, who until now had stayed at the mouth of the cave watching the work of the two dogs without taking his eyes from them, came slowly in, and licked and tended the two where they lay senseless in every way he could. When at last he saw them come to themselves he praised the night's work in the highest terms he had. Presently he bit off Kinbō's head, fastened it to his own horn, and ran down the mountain just as he was, hurrying to the steward's house. And so Koganemaru returned to his master's house and offered up the head of Kinbō; and his master, who had guessed much of it already, rejoiced beyond measure. "Well done indeed, Koganemaru; and Jūrō, you too are splendid. For years this Kinbō oppressed a multitude of beasts, and worse, injured human beings, and grew fiercer by the day; and to have killed him, and so removed a harm from the beasts and lifted a fear from men, is a service beyond all reckoning" — so he praised them with every word he had. Then he gave Koganemaru a gold collar and Jūrō a silver one, and made them both the guardians of his house; and the two, moved by his kindness, are said never to have failed in their loyal service. And so all was well.
