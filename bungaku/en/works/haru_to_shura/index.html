About this work

The only collection Miyazawa Kenji published in his lifetime, brought out at his own expense in 1924. He called the pieces not poems but "mental sketches," records of what had appeared in his mind. From the preface — "the phenomenon called myself is one blue illumination of a hypothesized organic alternating-current lamp" — it opens a world of its own in which the nature of Iwate and the cosmos, the vocabulary of science and a rooted folk imagination all run together. Besides the title poem it holds the great laments for his beloved sister Toshi, "The Morning of Last Farewell" and "Voiceless Grief." Almost unnoticed when it appeared, it is now called a monument of modern Japanese poetry.

Summary

This collection, titled "Mental Sketches: Spring and Asura," opens with a single "Proem." "The phenomenon called I / is one blue illumination / of a hypothesized organic alternating-current lamp" — Kenji sings that his own existence, and the landscapes set down here, are no more than phenomena blinking on and off within the vast time of the universe, and declares that these are not poems but "mental sketches," copied down just as they appeared in the mind. In the pieces that follow, with the hills and fields of Iwate for a stage, snow and cloud, woods and birds and the life of the farmers rise up in vivid images, mingled with the vocabulary of geology and chemistry and with religious vision. In the title poem, "Spring and Asura," Kenji walks a muddy spring road and looks at himself shaking with anger: "I am one asura." And in the three poems that are the height of the book — "The Morning of Last Farewell," "Pine Needles" and "Voiceless Lamentation" — a brother stands at the pillow of his beloved sister Toshi as she is drawing her last breath, and runs out to scoop up snow in answer to her last request, spoken in dialect, ame-yuju tote chite kenja, fetch me some rain-snow; and the love and grief of it are almost past bearing. Life and death, nature and self, science and faith sound together in what is a monument of modern Japanese poetry.

Proem The phenomenon called I is one blue illumination of a hypothesized organic alternating-current lamp (a compound of all transparent ghosts) that, together with the landscape and with everyone, blinking busily, busily on and off, goes on burning with such certainty — one blue illumination of the alternating-current lamp of cause and effect (the light is kept; the lamp is lost)

Famous passages

The phenomenon called I is a single blue illumination of a hypothesized, organic alternating-current lamp

The opening of the preface to the poetry collection “Spring and Asura.” A cosmology mingling science and faith, in which the self is grasped as a single flickering light, is placed at the very threshold of Kenji's poetry.

My sister, who before this day is out will go far away — sleet is falling, and outside it is strangely bright

The opening of “The Morning of Last Farewell,” sung on the day his sister Toshi died. The figure of a brother running out to fetch sleet engraves the deepest sorrow found anywhere in Kenji's poetry.

The bitterness of anger, and its blueness— at the bottom of the light of April's strata of air, spitting, gnashing my teeth, pacing back and forth, I am a single Asura.

The central lines of the title poem, “Spring and Asura.” Amid the light of spring, the poet depicts himself, walking with his anger and anguish, as an Asura—a dweller in the Buddhist realm of strife.

What we raised the alarm about as a fire just now was a rainbow. It has held, taut and clear, for a full hour already.

The short two-line poem “Report.” Conveying a rainbow so vivid it was mistaken for a fire in the polite tone of an official report, it captures surprise and humor in a single moment of landscape.

Thank you, my brave little sister. I too will go straight on.

A passage from “The Morning of Last Farewell.” Realizing that his dying sister asked him to fetch sleet in order to comfort him, Kenji tells her, with gratitude, of his resolve to live on.

The sword is lightning, the rustle of pampas plumes; the rain of fire scattering in the constellation of the Lion vanishes without a trace across the river of heaven; striking and perishing, all is one life.

The close of “Harakitai Sword Dance Troupe.” It depicts the sword dance, a folk performing art of Iwate, in images of lightning and a rain of fire scattering among the constellations. The line “striking and perishing, all is one life” gives the dance a cosmic sense of life.
