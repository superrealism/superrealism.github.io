About this work

Do you know the uzu no shuge? — a small piece by Miyazawa Kenji, told from the dialect name of the pasque flower. Two pasque flowers with petals like black satin talk with a skylark, and are blown by the wind and scattered as silver down. The ending, in which the narrator likens where their souls have gone to a variable star, is beautiful.

Summary

"Do you know the uzu no shuge?" — botany calls it the okinagusa, the pasque flower, but the narrator feels that name does not quite capture that gentle, young blossom. When he speaks of the uzu no shuge, he says, its petals of black satin, its pale silvery-velvet leaves, and the glossy, shining down of its June seed-heads all rise clearly before his eyes. Asked by an ant scurrying beneath the flower, "Do you like the uzu no shuge?", the ant answers briskly, "I love them dearly. There isn't anyone who dislikes that lady." Sometimes the flower looks nearly black, he says, but when the sunlight falls on it, it seems almost to blaze up bright red. On a hilltop stand two uzu no shuge. A skylark comes flying down and speaks with them. Asked whether they are not afraid of soon turning to seed-down and flying away, the two flowers answer that they are not afraid at all — that they are glad. They look up at a star shining like an arc lamp in the northern sky, let the wind blow over them, and quietly wait for that day. Before long, a beautiful, clear wind comes sweeping in, turning the poplar leaves, raising waves across the oat fields, and climbing the hill. The uzu no shuge shine and sway as though dancing, and cry out: "Farewell, skylark. Farewell, everyone. Thank you, dear sun." And then, as when a star shatters and scatters, their bodies come apart, and each single strand of silver down shines pure white as it flies away toward the north. The skylark soars straight up into the sky and sings a brief, sharp song — and the narrator believes that the two small souls that went up to heaven became two small variable stars.

Characters

  • Uzu no shuge (the pasque flower)　Two flowers blooming on the hill
  • The skylark　A bird that talks with the pasque flowers
  • The ant　Answers the narrator's question about the flower

The opening (original text)

Do you know the uzu no shuge? In botany it is called the okinagusa, the pasque flower, but somehow that name never seems to capture that gentle, youthful blossom. And if you ask me just what uzu no shuge means, I find I both know and do not quite know the answer.

Okinagusa by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I have wondered why the skylark did not fly north, after the silver down of the pasque flowers, but flew straight up toward the sky instead. It must surely be because the souls of the two pasque flowers had gone up to heaven. ...And then, I wonder, what became of those two small souls once they reached heaven? I believe that they became two small variable stars.

Okinagusa by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)
