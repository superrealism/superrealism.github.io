About this work

Hagiwara Sakutarō's first collection, published in 1917 by Kanjō Shisha and Hakujitsusha. Leaving behind classical metre, it gave raw shape to inner unease, fear and physical dread through an extraordinarily acute sensibility and the rhythms of free colloquial verse — a landmark of modern Japanese poetry. It holds poems such as "The Face at the Bottom of the Ground," "Bamboo" and "The Swimmer," which crystallize the hallucinations of a sick nervous system in transparent language, with a preface by Kitahara Hakushū. Modern Japanese poetry is generally said to have entered a new age with this book, and all the free verse that followed to have come out of it.

Summary

Through all its sections — "Bamboo and Its Grief," "Skylark Dishes," "Howling at the Moon" — Hagiwara Sakutarō draws the world as a sick soul's eyes take it in, in the transparent rhythms of the spoken language. In the opening poem, "The Sick Face at the Bottom of the Ground," the roots of green bamboo sprouting from under the earth and the face of an invalid overlap uncannily, and the very budding of life rises up as sorrow. In the famous "Bamboo," the clean vitality of green bamboo standing straight up out of the shining ground is sung with startling force on a rhythm of repetitions. Elsewhere — rotten clams, lonely lust, a dog howling on a moonlit night — physical disgust, fear and the sense of solitude are crystallized in language full of metaphor and music. The poet called his poems "a lonely consolation for those who own a sick soul and for the solitary," and tried to convey, by rhythm and from mind to mind, the very substance of feeling, which no explanation or argument can reach. No collection before it had fixed the unease and the shudder hidden in the modern mind so finely or with so fresh a sense of language, and it changed the history of Japanese poetry.

The opening (original text)

The Sick Face at the Bottom of the Ground At the bottom of the ground a face appears, the face of a lonely invalid appears. In the darkness at the bottom of the ground, softly, softly the stems of grass begin to sprout, a nest of rats begins to sprout, and tangled in the nest countless hairs begin to tremble, and about the time of the winter solstice, out of the lonely, sickly ground, the slender roots of green bamboo begin to grow, begin to grow, and they look truly pitiful, look as though they were smoking, look truly, truly pitiful.

Howling at the Moon by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Bamboo On the shining ground bamboo grows, green bamboo grows, under the ground the roots of bamboo grow, the roots grow thinner and thinner, from the tips of the roots fine hairs grow, faintly smoking fine hairs grow, faintly trembling. On the hard ground bamboo grows, above the ground bamboo grows sharp, headlong bamboo grows, its frozen joints ringing clear, under the blue sky bamboo grows, bamboo, bamboo, bamboo grows.

Famous passages

From the shining ground, bamboo grows, green bamboo grows, underground the bamboo roots grow, the roots gradually taper thin, from the root-tips fine hairs grow, faintly hazy fine hairs grow, faintly trembling.

The first stanza of “Bamboo,” the collection's representative poem. Through nothing but the repetition of the same words, it hones to a fine point the vital force of bamboo thrusting up from its roots underground into the light.

Two pitch-black cats on the roof in the languid night; from the tips of their tails, standing straight up, a thread-like crescent moon hangs blurred. “Owaa, good evening.” “Owaa, good evening.” “Ogyaa, ogyaa, ogyaa.” “Owaaa, the master of this house is sick.”

The whole of the poem “Cat.” The onomatopoeia, catching the cats' cries on the rooftop as if they were a conversation, is eerie and yet endearing. A representative poem of Howling at the Moon, which showed the newness of free verse in the colloquial language.

A frog was killed; the children made a circle and raised their hands, all together, their sweet, bloodied hands they raised; the moon came out; a man stands on the hill. Under his hat is a face.

The whole of the poem “The Death of a Frog.” The innocent children's “bloodied hands” are set beside a figure standing on the hill, and an ordinary scene turns into an eerie fantasy.

Poetry is neither mystery nor symbol nor demon. Poetry is simply the lonely consolation of those who possess a sick soul, and of those who are solitary.

A passage from the author's own preface to the collection. Freeing poetry from the devices of mystery and symbol and calling it the consolation of sick souls and solitary people, these words are quoted as marking the starting point of Sakutarō's poetry.
