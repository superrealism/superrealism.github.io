About this work

The autobiographical novel that made Hayashi Fumiko's name. The narrator, raised moving from place to place as a pedlar's daughter, works as a maid, a factory hand and a café waitress, and survives at the bottom of Tokyo — all set down without concealment in the form of a diary. A tough, humorous vitality runs through it, of a woman who does not stop writing poems even at the lowest point, and on publication in 1930 it became an unprecedented bestseller. This is the first version, as serialized in Nyonin Geijutsu.

Summary

"October ×" — the days of the narrator's wandering are told as a string of diary entries headed only by dates. She looks up at the autumn sky through the skylight of the maids' room at a café, sleeps the dreamless sleep of a live-in worker, and still wishes that she might "truly write something." With no home town of her own, thinking of her mother out peddling, made to cry by men she cannot count on, driven by trips to the pawnshop and by hunger, she moves from job to job — café waitress, office girl, street vendor. A bowl of hot rice, a book of poems, the kindness of the neighbors: the small joys inside a life at the bottom are drawn, in a spoken rhythm that seems flung down on the page and in poems that break in without warning, with something wilder and brighter than self-pity. A heroine who goes hungry without cringing and who, right beside despair, can still say airily that to live is fifty years, caught the hearts of readers under the Shōwa depression and made Hayashi Fumiko's name at a stroke. It is a monument in the line of Japanese autobiographical writing and of writing by women.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, raised moving from place to place as a peddler's daughter, living at the bottom of Tokyo and changing jobs, kept going by the writing of poems alone
  • Mother　The narrator's mother, still peddling in Kyūshū, who exchanges postcards and money orders with her daughter
  • The man from the island　The lover who abandoned her. He came from Innoshima, and the memory of him unsettles her every time

The opening (original text)

Autumn has come October × Looking up at the square skylight, a foot across, I saw for the first time a sky clear and purple. Autumn has come. Eating my rice in the cook's room, I thought how I longed for the autumn of the far-off countryside. Autumn is good.... Another woman came today. Whitish like a marshmallow, and rather amusing. It disgusts me — why is it that I want people so? And yet every customer's face looks like a piece of merchandise, and every customer's face is tired. It hardly matters. I pretended to be reading a magazine and sat there thinking of all sorts of things. Unbearable. I must do something; it is as though I were letting myself rot away.

The ending (original text)

When I open the window, the art students are in the March sun, wrestling with each other, leaning against the wall. How pleasant it must be to pass one's days as easily as that. I like pictures too, you know. See — I like Gauguin, and Dufy. "Any rooms free in the apartment house?" When the fresh, cheerful laughter breaks out, all their eyes look up at me at once. And in those eyes there are skies and mountains and seas. I held up two fingers, imitating Beni. Impatience — to live is fifty years
