About this work

A diary-form essay serialized in the newspaper Nippon in 1901. Nailed to his bed by tuberculosis of the spine, Shiki sets down the feeling of each day, some entries no bigger than a drop of ink. Criticism of haiku and tanka, the company of fellow poets, an inexhaustible affection for plants and food, complaints of a pain that leaves him unable to move, and reflection with death in view — all in an unadorned hand. The work that leads on to "A Sixfoot Sickbed," his last, and a fine example of the literature Shiki made from his sickbed.

Summary

Nailed to his sickbed by tuberculosis of the spine, Masaoka Shiki sets down day after day the thoughts of each day, the shortest of them like a single drop of ink. It begins with the scene at his pillow, where a small New Year's decoration has been tied to the thermometer to greet the year; and there follow, day by day and in an unforced hand, sharp criticism of haiku and tanka, the company of the poets who gather at Negishi, an unfailing affection for the flowers in the garden and for food, and the complaints of a man who cannot even move. Now expounding his theories of verse with heat, now laying bare the living man who cries out in pain inside his mosquito net, Shiki never loses to the last his clear observing eye or his vigorous appetite for living. These fragments, in which an intellectual curiosity undimmed by illness and a tenacity for life show through, lead on to "Sickbed Six Feet," the last work of the following year, and are a fine example of the sickbed writing of his final years.

The opening (original text)

By my sick pillow there is a box holding rolled paper and letter envelopes and the like, and on it I have set a thermometer. To that thermometer a small New Year's decoration has been tied, by way of celebrating the new year a little from my sickbed; and the way the fern branches spread to left and right is very fine indeed. Below it I have set a bitter orange, and beside the orange a globe of about the same size. This globe was given me by Sokotsu as a New Year's gift for the twentieth century.

The ending (original text)

Among those who write haiku about sushi there are many who use the words "sushi tub" and "pressing sushi" without knowing what they mean. The sushi of old must have been ayu sushi and the like. For that, ayu are put in among rice and vinegar is poured over them, and the whole is put in a tub with a weight on it. So it stands one day, or two, or as long as seven days or more, and then at last becomes fit to eat; this is called "ripening." In some places the practice survives even now. Funazushi must be the same thing. In my own province they use sea fish — small sea bream, horse mackerel, mullet and the like — because it is a country by the sea. The ordinary hand-pressed sushi and scattered sushi of today are really a coarse business. (July 2)

Famous passages

The sprays of wisteria set in the vase are short, and so they do not reach down to the tatami.

The first of a sequence of poems on the wisteria that the bedridden Shiki had arranged on his desk. Recording only the plain fact, just as seen, that the short sprays do not reach the tatami, it conveys the gaze of a sick man unable to rise. A representative poem of the “sketch from life” he championed.

In my view, the tanka of the two of us rest on entirely different standards: if Tekkan is right, then Shiki is wrong; if Shiki is right, then Tekkan is wrong. Tekkan and Shiki are not to be spoken of in the same breath.

His rebuttal to being discussed in the same breath as Yosano Tekkan. Declaring that their tanka rest on wholly different standards, so that if one is right the other is wrong, it makes plain Shiki's position as the champion of sketching from life.

The iris has come into bloom — and before my eyes, this spring, which for me will be the last, is about to pass.

A poem from a sequence written in the spring of the year before his death, under the note “forcing myself to take up the brush.” Before the newly blooming iris, he quietly watches this spring go by as the last he will have.
