About this work

A children's story by Miyazawa Kenji, published after his death. It begins when Kenjū, a young man everyone laughs at as a little wanting, plants seven hundred cedar seedlings on a piece of waste ground over everyone's objections. Kenjū dies young, but the grove he planted becomes a beautiful park grove where children play for years afterwards, and goes on asking people what real happiness is. Kenji's quiet song about the unmeasurable worth of a goodness that asks nothing in return.

Summary

Kenjū was a young man who always wore a rope for a sash and laughed for no reason at all — a young man the villagers thought a little bit slow. He rejoiced at the sight of green thickets in the rain, or a hawk soaring across the blue sky, and went on smiling even when the children made fun of him. One day, for the first time in his life, Kenjū begged his family: "Buy me seven hundred cedar saplings." Everyone objected, but his elder brother interceded for him, and Kenjū set about planting the cedar saplings, one by one, with great care, on a stretch of wasteland like an open field. Though people laughed that the soil was too poor for cedars to grow, Kenjū kept cutting back the undergrowth and tending them faithfully, and in time the cedars grew into a straight, upright grove. But then, struck down by a disease then going around (typhoid fever), Kenjū died young, all too suddenly. Time passed, and the village changed entirely into a town, but the cedar grove Kenjū had planted alone remained just as it had been, and became the children's favorite place to play. Eventually a certain scholar, a native of the village, returned home, and learning that this very grove was what Kenjū alone had raised, urged the villagers to preserve it under the name "Kenjū Kōenrin" — Kenjū Park Grove. Through that grove, which goes on teaching thousands upon thousands of children what true happiness is, Kenji quietly sings out the immeasurable worth of a selfless good that asks for nothing in return.

Characters

  • Kenjū　A young man who wears a rope sash and laughs, thought by everyone to be a little slow. Plants and lovingly raises seven hundred cedars on a stretch of wasteland, but dies young of illness
  • The scholar　A learned man native to the village. Returns home, learns that the cedar grove is what Kenjū left behind, and urges that it be preserved as "Kenjū Kōenrin"

The opening (original text)

Kenjū always wore a rope for a sash, and walked slowly, laughing, through the woods and among the fields. At the sight of a green thicket in the rain he would rejoice and blink his eyes rapidly, and whenever he spotted a hawk soaring endlessly across the blue sky he would leap up, clap his hands, and let everyone know. But because the children made such fun of him and laughed at him so much, Kenjū gradually came to pretend that he was not laughing at all.

Kenjū kōenrin by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Kenjū's family wept for true joy. And indeed, there was no telling how many thousands of people this grove's fine, dark-green cedars, their fresh fragrance, their cool summer shade, and its moonlight-colored lawn would go on teaching what true happiness really was. And the grove, just as it had been while Kenjū still lived, let the clear, cold drops of rain fall drip by drip onto the short grass whenever it rained, and breathed out fresh, clean air whenever the sun shone.

Famous passages

Ah, truly, who is wise and who is not, no one can tell. Only, the working of the Ten Powers is wondrous beyond all measure.

The words of the scholar who learns that the cedar grove Kenjū planted has survived as a playground for children. Recognizing the worth of what Kenjū, laughed at as a fool, had done, it calls into question the very yardstick by which we measure people's wisdom.
