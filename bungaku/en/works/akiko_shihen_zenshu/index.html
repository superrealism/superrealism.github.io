About this work

Yosano Akiko, the poet of Tangled Hair, herself chose four hundred and twenty-one pieces from the drafts of poems she had accumulated between 1900 and the early Shōwa years. Akiko as mother, as working woman and as observer of society sings here in a voice quite different from that of her tanka. As her own preface says, this is the handiwork of an amateur: poems that stand as an unadorned record of a life.

Summary

The author's preface is written as a dedication to Mrs. Minobe Tamiko, who had gone on sending her roses. At the beginning of this autumn, having a little leisure, she put in order the drafts of her poems from the thirty-third year of Meiji down to the present, and chose four hundred and twenty-one of them for this one volume — with no more than the thought, she writes, that some day her children might find it at the bottom of something and read it as a fragment of the record of their mother's life. Since with many of the poems she can no longer tell herself when they were made, they are gathered by kinship of feeling. Love and weariness as a mother, the felt substance of a life bringing up many children, her heart as she thinks of her husband setting out for Paris, her wish for the independence of women, and a sharp eye upon society and the age — what could not be said in tanka is sung here in the free form of colloquial verse. In "The Bell at Three," which begins "The bell of Ueno rings," she writes that when the Japanese hear the bell their hearts are all stilled, their own strength is paralyzed and they turn into believers in the strength of another; and she looks at herself sinking under that weakness in the blood. In "The Loneliness of a Certain Day" she lines up false working students, false gentlemen, false trials, false education, and laments that "I have never once met the true." It closes with a note saying that she finished the proofs on the first of November, in the year of the Grand Enthronement.

The opening (original text)

Dedicated to Mrs. Minobe Tamiko Author's preface Dear Mrs. Minobe Tamiko, At the beginning of this autumn, having a little leisure, I put in order the drafts of my own poems made from the thirty-third year of Meiji down to the present, and chose four hundred and twenty-one of them for this one volume. I had thought no more than that, if I gathered them up in this way, some day my children might find it at the bottom of something and read it as a fragment of the record of their mother's life —

Akiko shihen zenshū by Yosano Akiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The Loneliness of a Certain Day Standing at the gate are false working students, false disabled veterans, false men of principle, false patriots; riding in carriages and motorcars are false gentlemen, false ministers, false ladies of rank; and then, if I look at the newspaper, false trials, false marriages, and false education. The alleys of this floating world are thick with them, and I have never once met the true.

Famous passages

Ah, my brother, I weep for you, you must not go and die. Born the last of us, you were loved best by our parents — but did they raise you, put a blade into your hand, and teach you to kill? Did they raise you for twenty-four years telling you to kill and then to die?

The first stanza of a poem addressed to her younger brother, sent to fight at Port Arthur in the Russo-Japanese War. Posing the question, “Did they teach you to kill?”, it is a celebrated poem that defied the mood of its time.

The day comes when the mountains move. Though I say so, no one believes it. The mountains have only been sleeping a while; long ago, they all burned with fire and moved. But you need not believe that. O people, ah, believe only this: all the women who were sleeping are now awakening and beginning to move.

The opening part of the poem sequence “Idle Words,” contributed to the first issue of Seitō, the magazine founded by Hiratsuka Raichō and others in 1911. Likening to a mountain the day when women who had been sleeping awaken and begin to move, it is still quoted as a declaration of women's liberation.

Would that I might write only in the first person — I, a lonely woman in a corner. Would that I might write only in the first person — I, I.

A poem from the sequence “Idle Words,” following “The Day the Mountains Move.” The wish to write only in the first person expresses a woman's will to independence — to speak of herself in her own words.
