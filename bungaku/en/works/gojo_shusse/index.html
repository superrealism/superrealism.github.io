About this work

A short story published in the collection Nantōtan in 1942, the companion to "Wujing's Lament" in Nakajima's two-part "My Journey to the West." Before he becomes Xuanzang's disciple, the monk Wujing is seized at the bottom of the Flowing Sands river by the question of why one lives at all, and — troubled by it as by a disease — goes from one to another of the demon sages who dwell in the riverbed. Wujing's wandering soul, in search of an answer, drawn by Nakajima over his own doubts. A meditative piece.

Summary

Before he joined Tripitaka's party, the monk Sha Wujing was a monster living at the bottom of the River of Flowing Sands. At some point he became gripped by the questions of what he was and why he lived, and brooded over them as though it were an illness. Losing his appetite, muttering to himself, he goes from one to another of the many monster sages and adepts who live at the bottom of the river, looking for an answer. One preaches pleasure, another nothingness; one gives himself wholly to the enjoyments of this world, another spins difficult arguments — and none of them slakes his thirst. Wandering into the blind alleys of thought and worn out in mind and body, he is guided at last, in a dream, by a bodhisattva. And in the autumn of that year Wujing meets Xuanzang, the master of the great Tang, becomes his disciple, comes out of the water and is given human form. Setting out with the guileless and high-spirited Sun Wukong and the lazy, easygoing Zhu Wuneng on a new journey, he still carries with him his habit of talking to himself and a doubt he cannot shake off. It is a piece of "My Journey to the West" in which Nakajima drew, almost as a second self, the wandering of a soul that cannot help asking why.

Characters

  • Sha Wujing　A monster living at the bottom of the River of Flowing Sands, gripped by the question of why he lives, who goes from sage to sage in search of an answer
  • The monsters and adepts of the riverbed　The various sages Wujing calls on for instruction, each preaching his own view of life
  • Tripitaka (Xuanzang)　Who later takes Wujing as his disciple, gives him human form and leads him on the journey

The opening (original text)

It was the beginning of autumn, when the cold cicadas cry in the withered willows and the great fire star runs westward, and Tripitaka, forlorn, was pressing on over hard country with his two disciples, when suddenly there lay before them a great river. The waves boiled up and rolled back, and no one could say how wide the river might be. Climbing the bank to look out, they saw a stone monument at one side, on which the three characters "River of Flowing Sands" were carved in seal script, with four lines of small regular characters on the face of it.

Gojō shusse by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In the autumn of that year Wujing did indeed meet the master Xuanzang of the great Tang, and by his power was able to come out of the water and become a human being. And so, together with the brave and guileless Great Sage Equal to Heaven, Sun Wukong, and the lazy optimist Marshal Tianpeng, Zhu Wuneng, he set out upon a new journey. But even on that road Wujing, not yet quite free of his old illness, did not give up his habit of talking to himself. — From "My Journey to the West" —

Famous passages

We live standing only upon the moment called the present. And that present beneath our feet at once vanishes and becomes the past.

A passage from a story whose hero, Sha Wujing of Journey to the West, keeps asking what the self is. Like a traveler standing on crumbling sand, it speaks of a present that vanishes moment by moment.
