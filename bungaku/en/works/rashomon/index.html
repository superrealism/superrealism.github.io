About this work

One of Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's best-known stories, published in the magazine Teikoku Bungaku in November 1915. Based on a tale from the Konjaku Monogatarishū and set in the ruined Kyoto of the late Heian period, it follows a man who chooses evil in order to survive. Written at the very start of Akutagawa's career, its spare, taut prose and its interest in how morality bends under extremity mark the beginning of everything that followed.

Summary

A servant who has lost his place stands at a loss beneath the Rashōmon gate in the failing light of a rainy evening. He knows there is no way left but to become a thief, yet he cannot bring himself to decide; to get out of the rain he climbs to the upper storey of the gate. There he sees an old woman working by torchlight, pulling out the long hairs of a corpse one by one. Seized by hatred and by a sense of right, he draws his sword and demands to know who she is. She explains that the hair is for a wig, and that the dead woman had sold snake flesh as dried fish while she lived: one must do wrong in order to live, and so what she is doing must be forgiven too. Hearing this, the servant loses every hesitation about becoming a thief. "Then you will not begrudge me if I strip you," he says, tears the robe from her, and runs down the ladder into the darkness of the night. Where he went after that, no one knows.

Characters

  • The servant　A young man dismissed by his master, who shelters from the rain beneath the Rashōmon and wrestles with whether to become a thief in order to live. The protagonist
  • The old woman　An old woman found pulling hair from the corpses in the upper storey of the gate. She defends what she is doing, and the servant strips her of her robe

The opening (original text)

It was toward evening one day. A servant was waiting under the Rashōmon for the rain to stop. Under the wide gate there was no one but this man. Only, on a great pillar with its red lacquer peeling here and there, a single cricket had settled. Since the Rashōmon stands on Suzaku Avenue, there ought to have been two or three others waiting out the rain — a woman in a market hat, a man in a soft courtier's cap. But there was no one but this man.

The ending (original text)

It was not long before the old woman, who had lain as if dead, raised her naked body from among the corpses. Muttering, groaning, she crawled by the light of the still-burning torch to the mouth of the ladder. Then, her short white hair hanging down, she peered out below the gate. Outside there was only the night, black and unfathomable. Where the servant went, no one knows.

Famous passages

It was toward evening on a certain day. A servant was waiting under the Rashōmon gate for the rain to stop.

A spare introduction that fixes time and character in a single breath, in one corner of a ruined Heian capital. From here the story of a servant's quiet step into evil, taken in order to survive, begins to move.

No one knows where the servant went.

The final line that closes the whole story. It lets the servant, having stepped into evil, vanish into the darkness, refusing to tell what became of him and casting the reader off, unanswered.

“Then you won't hold it against me if I strip you. I'm another who'd starve to death if I didn't.”

Words the servant flings out after hearing the old woman's excuse, as he resolves to become a thief. Justifying evil for the sake of survival by the old woman's own logic, the story heads into darkness.
