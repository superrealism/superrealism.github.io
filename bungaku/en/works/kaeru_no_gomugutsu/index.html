About this work

A children's story by Miyazawa Kenji, in which three frogs living beside a weir — Kan, Bun and Ben — give themselves up to "cloud-watching" at the summer thunderheads, and then let vanity and jealousy grow between them over a single pair of rubber boots. It was not published in the author's lifetime, and Kenji's humor comes out well in it: the comedy of the chain by which the boots were come by, back through field mouse, mouse, cat, dog and horse; the frogs' greeting of "goodbye now" repeated over and over; and the breath of a narrator who breaks off partway to tell the reader, "Only a little more. Please be patient."

Summary

A deep weir ran under a wood of pine and oak, and on its bank, in the shade of the dayflowers, stood Kan the frog's house; under the oak tree stood Bun the frog's, and in the shade of the pampas grass Ben the frog's. The three were of an age and much of a size, and each as cheeky and mischievous as the others. On a summer evening the three sit in the clover meadow and do what they call cloud-watching; for a frog there is nothing he can look at so long as the summer thunderheads. One day Kan, following a troublesome chain of connections back through a field mouse, a mouse, a cat, a dog and a horse, at last comes by a pair of rubber boots. As Kan goes walking briskly about in them, Bun and Ben cannot hide their envy. Just then Rura, a young frog who has come looking for a husband, appears, and points at Kan without a moment's hesitation. Out of pure mortification the other two plan their revenge. First they lure him out for a walk over the stubble of cut reeds, and the rubber boots are cut to ribbons. Then they agree that after the wedding they will drop the pair into a post hole covered over with leaves. But the plan rolls off in an unexpected direction, and before they know it all three are at the bottom of the hole. Half a day later Rura and her father find them, hang down a vine of ground pine, and haul them up one at a time. All three lay half dead with their white bellies turned up, and they are brought round at last by being rubbed with the down of a gomazai plant. After that every one of them mended his ways and became a good worker.

Characters

  • Bun the frog　The frog who lives under the oak tree
  • Ben the frog　The frog who lives in the shade of the pampas grass
  • Rura the frog　The young frog the three of them contend for

The opening (original text)

A deep weir flowed below a wood of pine trees and oak trees. The bank was thick with brambles and dayflowers and knotweed, and under a clump of some ten of those dayflowers stood the house of Kan the frog. Then, under an oak tree in the wood, stood the house of Bun the frog. And in the shade of the pampas grass on the far side of the wood stood the house of Ben the frog. The three of them were of an age and much of a size, and every one of them was as cheeky and as mischievous as the others. One summer evening the three of them, Kan and Bun and Ben, sat in the clover meadow in front of Kan's house doing what they call cloud-watching. For all frogs, every one of them, dearly love to look at the summer thunderheads.

The ending (original text)

"They may be dead by now." The father frog, taking care not to fall in, put his ear to the mouth of the hole and listened, and there was a faint sound of a splash. "We have them!" he cried, and hurried home and brought back all his fellow frogs. And they fetched a vine of ground pine from the wood and let it down into the hole, and hauled the three of them out at last, one at a time. All three lay with their white bellies turned up, their eyes shut and their mouths held tight, half dead. Everyone fetched the down of a gomazai plant and rubbed them with it and did what they could, and at last they were saved. So Kan the frog was married to Rura the frog at last; and the other frogs too mended their ways from that time, and all of them became good workers.
