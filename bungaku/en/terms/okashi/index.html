What is Okashi?

Category
Other

One of the aesthetic principles running through Heian-period literature: a sense of viewing something from a slight distance and finding it brightly, intellectually amusing. It carries a range of meanings — "charming," "delightful," "clever" — far broader than the modern word "okashii" ("funny, strange"). Sei Shōnagon's "The Pillow Book" uses this word again and again, cutting out, with a sharp eye and ready wit, the passing of the four seasons, events at court, and the smallest matters of daily life. Its opening line — "In spring, the dawn. As the sky at the mountains' rim gradually brightens..." — plainly conveys this sensibility. In literary history, it is often explained as a pair with "mono no aware," the sentiment that seeps deeply into the heart, and "The Tale of Genji" is customarily called the literature of "mono no aware," "The Pillow Book" the literature of "okashi." Where "aware" stays close to sorrow, "okashi" is an attitude that keeps a certain distance from its object and affirms the world with a bright eye. This critical, light-footed gaze also flows into the essays of the medieval period and the laughter of early-modern haikai, forming another undercurrent of Japanese literature.
