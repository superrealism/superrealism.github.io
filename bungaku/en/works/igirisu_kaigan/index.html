About this work

A piece in which Kenji, then a teacher at the Hanamaki agricultural school, set down the bank of the Kitakami River where he used to go with his pupils. He called that place, where a pale tuffaceous mudstone lies widely exposed, the "English Coast." Talk of the geology, fossil walnuts and the footprints of cloven-hoofed animals left in the mudstone, and what happened on a day when he went into the water with the boys, are told in the tone of a daybook of farm practice. The English Coast at Hanamaki is a real place that still exists on the Kitakami, and its name became widely known through this piece.

Summary

During the fifteen days of farm practice in the summer holidays, whenever a piece of work was finished, we used to go for our amusement to the west bank of the Kitakami River. A little downstream from where the Sarugaishi River joins it, a pale tuffaceous mudstone lies exposed in a wide band along the water, and because it is washed every time the river rises it is always clean and shining white. Someone or other began to call it the English Coast, and the name stuck. When the sun beats down the rock dries dead white and cracks run over it, and if one walks there in a broad hat one's shadow falls black on the stone. The boys swim and run on the rock, and I go about examining the beds of mudstone. Walnuts lie buried in them, turned to fossils, and there are footprints that look like those of some cloven-hoofed animal. One day the boys gather to dig one of them out. Some of them cross by jumping in at the rough shallows, but there is no need to worry, since the headmaster has gone in ahead of them and is swimming slowly. It was yesterday that we carried the specimens back to the school; today is the ninth day of the practice and it has been raining since morning. Ahead of us is still the threshing of the barley, a hard business with the awns working into one's body — but we mean to make it as interesting as we can. The piece is set down to that closing line with all the feel of a teacher's daily life in it.

The opening (original text)

During the fifteen days of farm practice in the summer holidays, there was a place we nicknamed the English Coast, where we often went for our amusement every two or three days, whenever a piece of work was finished. It was not really a coast at all, but the bank of a river that had very much the air of a coast. It was the west bank of the Kitakami River — a little downstream, on the west side, from where the cold Sarugaishi River, which comes from Sennin Pass in the east through Tōno and past Tsuchizawa, cutting across the Kitakami mountains, falls into the Kitakami. At the English Coast a pale tuffaceous mudstone lies exposed along the river over a very wide stretch, and if one stood at its southern end, a man at the northern end looked smaller than the tip of one's little finger. And because those beds of mudstone are washed clean every time the river rises, they were pale and fresh beyond describing.

The ending (original text)

After a while we all carried it back to the school together. And, as I said before, that was yesterday. Today is the ninth day of the practice. It has been raining since morning, so we can do no work out of doors. We mean to amuse ourselves indoors, drawing plans and the like. Ahead of us there is still the threshing of the barley to be done. The weather is bad and it will not dry properly, which is a nuisance. Threshing barley is very hard work, with the awns working into one's body every which way. Everyone says it is the worst of all the farmer's jobs. Around here they even call it the summer sickness. But it will not do to think of it in that way at all. We mean somehow to make it as interesting as we can. (9 August 1923)
