About this work

Andersen's best-known tale, in which a duckling who alone is large and gray, shunned by his brothers and even by his mother, lives through a winter and at last sees himself reflected in the water of spring. Kikuchi Kan's version carries it over into a long-breathed prose that reads less as plain words for children than as a piece for grown readers. It has long been read as a story into which the author folded his own upbringing, and in Japanese "the ugly duckling" has settled into the language as a phrase for one who blooms late.

Summary

In the summer sunshine, beside an old house where burdock leaves grew thick, a duck sat on her eggs. One shell broke after another, but the largest egg alone would not hatch. The bird that came out of it at last was gray, unlike his brothers, big and ill-shaped. Even in the duck yard he is pecked and laughed at by the ducks and the hens, and when his own mother says that he had better go away somewhere, the duckling runs off. On the marsh of the wild ducks he is terrified by the sound of guns and passed over by a great dog; in an old woman's cottage the hen and the cat reproach him for being able neither to lay an egg nor to purr. In autumn, seeing a flock of great white birds crossing the evening sky, his chest aches, though he does not know what they are called. Then the hard winter comes; he is nearly frozen into the ice, is saved by a farmer, runs away again, and survives half-frozen in the reeds. In spring, when he goes up to three swans that have come down on the water, bowing his head to be killed if they will, what he sees reflected in the water is not a gray ugly bird but a beautiful swan.

Characters

  • The ugly duckling　Born alone large and gray, he is shunned wherever he goes
  • The mother duck　She defends her young one at first, and then casts him off
  • The old woman　She takes him into her cottage, but presses him to lay eggs
  • The hen and the cat　The older residents of the cottage, who look down on the duckling
  • The swans　They welcome the new one on the water in spring

The opening (original text)

It was so lovely out in the country. It was summer; the wheat stood yellow and the oats green, the hay was raised in stacks down in the green meadows, and there went the stork on his long red legs, chattering Egyptian, for that was the language he had learned from his mother. Round about the fields and the meadows lay great woods, and in the middle of the woods were deep lakes. Yes, it was truly lovely out in the country. In the midst of the sunshine there stood an old manor house with deep moats around it, and from the walls down to the water's edge grew great burdock leaves. They were so tall that a little child could stand upright under the largest of them.

The ending (original text)

The young swan was quite abashed, and hid his head under his wing; he did not know what to do with himself. He was only too happy, and yet not in the least proud, for a good heart is never proud. He who had been despised for being ill-favored — was he not now being called the most beautiful of all the birds? Even the elder bowed its branches down to him over the water, and the sun shone warm and bright overhead. And the new swan ruffled his feathers, curved his slender neck, and cried out from the bottom of his heart: "Ah, when I was the ugly duckling I never dreamed of such happiness as this!"
