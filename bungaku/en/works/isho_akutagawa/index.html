About this work

The will Akutagawa left for his family when he killed himself. He writes of his wife and children and of the settling of his affairs, and speaks of his own death as the final reckoning of his past life. The private and urgent last words of a man who died in July 1927 at thirty-five.

Summary

"We human beings do not easily kill ourselves for the sake of a single event. I am killing myself for the sake of a general settlement of my past life" — so Akutagawa begins. Looking back over his life, he sets down frankly his regret over a relation with a married woman, his calculations in love, his remorse over what at his adoptive family's house was "something resembling filial duty," and his disgust with everything. "I feel disgust now not only with myself, of course, but with everything." He then leaves words for his wife and his children, and writes out in fine detail the settlement of his affairs — the collection of his haiku, the characters for the gravestone, the memorial services. And he closes: "Do not forget my mind, which begs the forgiveness of all people and would forgive all people." The private and urgent last words of a great writer. A piercing posthumous manuscript, conveying one ending of the history of modern literature.

The ending (original text)

Third. Have Okimoto make an album of my seals. It would be well also to add a collection of my haiku and give them out at my memorial service or the like. Fourth. For the characters on the gravestone you must be sure to trouble Oana. Fifth. Do not forget my mind, which begs the forgiveness of all people and would forgive all people.
