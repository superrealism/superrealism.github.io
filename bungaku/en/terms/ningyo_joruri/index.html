What is Ningyō Jōruri (Puppet Theater)?

Category
Other

A distinctively Japanese puppet theater formed by the union of jōruri, a narrative chanted art, the shamisen, and puppetry. In 1684, Takemoto Gidayū opened the Takemoto-za in Dōtonbori, Osaka, and established the powerful Gidayū-bushi style of chanting, and thereby it became independent as an art form in its own right. As the theater's house playwright, Chikamatsu Monzaemon based "Sonezaki Shinjū" ("The Love Suicides at Sonezaki," 1703) on an actual love-suicide incident, depicting ordinary men and women driven to the brink by the conflict of duty and human feeling, and opening the genre of sewamono, while also succeeding with grand jidaimono such as "Kokusen'ya Kassen" ("The Battles of Coxinga"). The stage is characterized by the way the three arts — the chanter narrating the story, the shamisen player, and the puppeteers — bring their breath into unison to build a single performance, and expression grew still more refined once the technique developed of having three puppeteers together operate a single puppet. Celebrated works still performed today, such as the collaborative work "Kanadehon Chūshingura" by Takeda Izumo and others, were born at the Takemoto-za. Many of these plays were also adapted for kabuki, and the two forms influenced each other. From the Meiji era on it came to be called "Bunraku," after the name of the troupe run by Uemura Bunrakuken, which had kept the theater going. The conflict of duty and human feeling that Chikamatsu depicted has been read as an archetype of Japanese tragedy, carried on by modern writers as well.
