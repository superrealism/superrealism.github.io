About this work

One of the most famous experimental poems in modern Japanese verse: by simply repeating one line — a field of rape blossoms all over — it makes a whole field of them appear. It is subtitled "Mosaic in Pure Silver." Collected in the volume Sei Sanryō Hari, it was dismissed at the time as obscure, and is now Yamamura Bochō's best-known poem, carried in school readers. Between the repetitions, a wheat-stalk pipe, a skylark and a daytime moon are slipped briefly in.

Summary

One line — "nothing but rape blossoms" — is repeated and repeated. After seven lines, "a faint whistle of wheat straw" slips in. "Nothing but rape blossoms" runs on for eight lines more, and this time "the chattering of a skylark" is mixed in. After eight more lines comes "and ailing, the moon at noon" — a sick noon moon floating faintly above the sea of rape flowers. And then one line more: "nothing but rape blossoms." Before the reader knows it, the repetition of the letters themselves begins to look like a stretch of yellow flowers. Telling no meaning, and making a landscape appear simply by laying words down over the page, the poem is, as its subtitle "a mosaic in pure silver" says, a poem of construction in which each word is set like a small piece of a mosaic. Included in the collection Seisanryō hari (1915), it was not understood at the time, but later came to be widely read as one of the representative pieces of modern Japanese poetry.
