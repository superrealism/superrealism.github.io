About this work

Burnett's story (1905), written as a companion piece to "Little Lord Fauntleroy," translated by Kikuchi Kan for the "Complete Library for Primary School Children." It follows Sara, a girl brought by a father who has made a fortune in India and placed in a boarding school in London, who becomes overnight a penniless orphan through his death and ruin, and who does not lose her pride even when she is treated as a drudge in an attic room. Kikuchi wrote in his preface that it shows, from both sides of rising and falling fortune, a girl who will not bend what is right in any circumstances.

Summary

A London winter, deep in fog. Captain Crewe, who has made his fortune in mines in India, places his seven-year-old daughter Sara at Miss Minchin's boarding school. Through her father's open-handedness Sara is given special treatment, a sitting room of her own and a maid, and is deferred to for the cleverness with which she speaks French. Yet she herself does not know how to give herself airs, and takes the part of the servant Becky, and of Ermengarde, who is laughed at as a dunce, and of little Lottie. Then, at her eleventh birthday party, comes the news of her father's death and the loss of his whole fortune in a diamond mine. Miss Minchin turns on the instant, moves Sara into the attic, and drives her to errands and drudgery. Through days of cold and hunger, Sara tells herself that a real princess would behave like a princess whatever happened. One snowy day she buys buns with a coin she has found and gives most of them to a girl hungrier than herself. Before long an Indian gentleman who has moved in next door, and his servant Ram Dass, begin leaving wonderful gifts in the attic, and it comes out that this gentleman was her father's dearest friend and his partner in the mine.

Characters

  • Sara Crewe　A girl born in India, who becomes a penniless orphan at her father's death
  • Captain Crewe　Sara's father, who dies after his investment in the mine fails
  • Miss Minchin　The headmistress of the boarding school, whose manner changes with Sara's fortunes
  • Becky　The scullery maid at the school, who becomes Sara's friend
  • Carrisford　The Indian gentleman next door, Captain Crewe's dearest friend

The opening (original text)

1. From India to London Once on a dark winter's day, when the yellow fog hung so thick and heavy in the streets of London that the lamps were lighted and the shop windows blazed with gas as they do at night, an odd-looking little girl sat in a cab with her father and was driven rather slowly through the big thoroughfares. She sat with her feet tucked under her, and leaned against her father, who held her in his arm, as she stared out of the window at the passing people with a queer old-fashioned thoughtfulness in her big eyes. She was such a little girl that one did not expect to see such a look on her small face. It would have been an old look for a child of twelve, and Sara Crewe was only seven. The fact was, however, that she was always dreaming and thinking odd things, and could not herself remember any time when she had not been thinking things about grown-up people and the world they belonged to.

The ending (original text)

Anne took her hand. The two of them looked into each other's eyes again. "I am so glad," said Sara. "I have just thought of something good. I am sure the mistress will let you give the bread away, and I think you will be glad to do it. You know very well yourself what it is to be hungry." "Yes, miss," the girl answered. Anne said nothing more after that, but only stood there; and Sara felt that she understood what Anne was feeling. Anne stood where she was for a long time, watching as Sara left the shop with the Indian gentleman, got into the carriage, and drove away.
