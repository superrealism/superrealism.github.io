About this work

The piece for the year of the rat — the folklore and beliefs of the rat — and the last of the series. It begins with sound documentary work, proving from the positions of the lunar mansions when the twelve branches and the twelve animals were matched in China, and moves on to the customs in various parts of China of celebrating a mouse's wedding at the New Year, and to beliefs the world over that avoid saying the creature's name. It closes the great work by showing that even the detested rat has somewhere been revered as a divine thing.

Summary

When the new year comes in as the year of the rat, everyone at once thinks of rats — so Kumagusu begins, and first traces the origin of the practice of assigning twelve beasts to the twelve branches. Zhao Yi argued that it began in the Later Han; but reasoning from the Huainanzi, from the commentaries on the Zhuangzi, and from the shifting of the positions of the constellations, Kumagusu holds that it must already have been fixed in the time of the Zhou. That the Chinese left the concrete names of the twelve beasts behind and built up the abstract symbols of zi, chou, yin, mao was their great achievement, and lent enormous strength to the advance of calendar-making, divination and the compiling of histories. He then introduces the customs of various parts of China which celebrate the wedding of the rats at the New Year. In some places nothing at all is said about rats that night, and the lamps are put out so as not to disturb them. In Japan too, for the first three days of the year, the rat was called by another name, "the bride." From here the talk widens to the belief that forbids calling animals and gods by their real names. That in Sweden the wolf is called "the silent one" and "gray-foot" and the bear "the old man" and "the strength of twelve men"; that the ancient Egyptians hid the names of gods and demons alike; that in medieval Japan the real names of women are not handed down. In closing Kumagusu says that few things are so hated and disliked as the rat, and yet there are many people who live by eating it, and peoples who, superstitiously enough, have made it a divine thing and produced legends about it — so that the rat too, one sees, is not wholly useless.

The opening (original text)

When the new year comes in as the year of the rat, all of you at once think of rats. The assigning of the twelve beasts to the twelve branches — the year of zi the rat, the year of chou the ox — arose long ago in China and spread to the neighboring countries, Japan, Korea, Annam and the rest; and in India and Mexico too something like twelve creatures were assigned to the days of the calendar; but I have not heard of their being assigned to the points of the compass as in China.

Jūnishikō (nezumi) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Few things are so hated and disliked as the rat; but putting together what has been set out little by little, one sees that in the width of the world there are many people who live by eating rats, and peoples who, superstitious as it is, have made it a divine thing and produced all sorts of legends and tales about it. One sees that the rat too is not wholly useless.

Jūnishikō (nezumi) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
