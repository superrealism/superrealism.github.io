About this work

One of the four pieces of Kotan, with "The Moon over the Mountain" and "The Curse of Letters." In a settlement on a lake in Scythia, a man called Shaku begins to utter strange words, as though the spirits of beasts and men had entered him, ever since his brother was killed by barbarians. The people listen entranced to his stories, but in time the possession leaves him, and, no longer working, he is disposed of by the decision of the settlement. A fable placing the birth and destruction of the poet in a world before writing. The offhandedness of the last line is startling.

Summary

It is said that Shaku of the village of Neuri is possessed. The spirits of hawks and wolves and otters take him and make him utter strange words. Among these lake people, who drive logs into the shallows and build their houses upon them, Shaku was the most ordinary of men. He began to grow strange only after his younger brother Dekku was killed in the spring of last year in a raid by the Ugri, the nomads of the north. Standing blankly beside his brother's body, from which the head and the right hand had been cut away, Shaku presently began to utter deliriums as though his brother's right hand, and then his brother himself, had begun to speak. ... But afterward the things that take him spread even to beings that have nothing to do with his brother. A carp swimming in the lake speaks of the sorrow of the scaled tribe; a falcon of Mount Torus speaks of the grandeur of the view; a she-wolf of the plain speaks of the hardness of hunger. People gather in curiosity, and Shaku too comes to expect an audience. Before long some begin to say that this is no possession, but that Shaku is thinking it out and speaking it himself. Shaku himself does not know the meaning of what he does. Only, the pleasure of getting into things other than himself by imagination has become too strong to hold back. His stories grow more skillful day by day, and his materials move gradually toward the human society about him, until at last a story comes out that suggests the elder of the village. The elder is angry, and lays a plan, and draws the people's attention to the fact that Shaku does none of the village's work. Winter passes and Shaku's possession leaves him. A man who tells no stories and does no work may, by the village's agreement, be disposed of.

Characters

  • Shaku　One of the lake people, who begins to tell stories as though possessed
  • Dekku　Shaku's younger brother, killed by the barbarians
  • The elder　A man of power whose house mark is a leopard's claw, who plots Shaku's expulsion

The opening (original text)

It is said that Shaku of the village of Neuri is possessed. All sorts of things, they say, take hold of this man. The spirits of hawks and wolves and otters take possession of the poor Shaku and make him utter strange words.

Kitsunetsuki by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

On the evening of the next day a great feast was held round a fire on the lake shore. In the great pot, along with mutton and horseflesh, the flesh of the poor Shaku was simmering. For the people of that region, where food is not very plentiful, every fresh body except that of one who has died of disease is naturally used for food. The curly-headed young man who had been Shaku's most eager listener, his face flushed by the fire, was chewing the flesh of Shaku's shoulder. That a poet was eaten in this way long before the blind Maeonides who was called Homer began to sing those beautiful songs, nobody knows.

Kitsunetsuki by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)
