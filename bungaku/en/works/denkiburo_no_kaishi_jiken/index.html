About this work

A detective story Unno Jūza published in the magazine Shin Seinen in 1928, in effect his debut. Set at an electric bath, then greatly in fashion, it draws a run of strange deaths and the unexpected mechanism behind them. The manner, weaving a taste for science together with the grotesque, was the starting point of a writer later called the father of Japanese science fiction and a pioneer of the scientific detective story.

Summary

Igami Yōkichi, a man who loves the tranquility of a midday bath, is suddenly electrocuted and convulses in the tub of Mukaiyu, the "electric bath" he frequents. As the men's side falls into uproar, in the women's bath next door, a young woman is shot dead by a blowdart fired from the ceiling, and her clothing and even her wooden clogs vanish without a trace. In the crawlspace above the ceiling, the body of Yozō, a bathhouse hand, is found, along with a telescope, a motion-picture camera, and a blowgun. Prosecutor Akabane Naozō is thrown into confusion by one mystery after another: blood from the murdered woman's body dripping through a knot-hole in the ceiling; the victim's clothing vanished without a trace. But from the notebook of a policeman investigating the identity of the electrocution victim, he finds the name of Kashida Buhei, a man in the motion-picture business, and sees through the case. The culprit, Kashida, driven by the perverse ambition to film a young woman's death agony, had sent high voltage through the electric bath to cause the commotion of electrocution in the men's bath, and in that opening, shot the woman in the women's bath dead with a blowdart, filming it all; he then stabbed Yozō, who had noticed him, and tried to slip through the police cordon disguised as a woman, wearing the victim's own clothes. Once captured, the film, screened for the investigators, showed not the death agony they had expected, but only, because of the high-speed camera, the entrancing, mermaid-like figure of a woman swimming.

Characters

  • Igami Yōkichi　An idle man who loves a midday bath. Is electrocuted in the electric bath, setting off the case
  • Akabane Naozō　The prosecutor. Thrown into confusion by one mystery after another, he sees through the culprit from a policeman's notebook
  • Kashida Buhei　A young man in the motion-picture business. Commits his crime out of the perverse ambition to film a woman's death agony
  • Yozō　A hand at Mukaiyu. A voyeur with a secret peephole in the ceiling crawlspace, silenced to keep him quiet
  • Oteru　A woman working at a restaurant. Stops by Mukaiyu on her way home from visiting her aunt, and is chosen as the sacrifice

The opening (original text)

Igami Yōkichi loved a bath. Above all, that unhurried, tranquil atmosphere of a midday bath, when the customers were thin on the ground, was, for an idle man like himself, the very place best suited to carry along, unbroken, the sluggishness his body still bore from waking out of some extravagant sleep. And besides, on days like this, when the last traces of winter's cold still lingered faintly against his bare skin even in the mild weather of these past two days, to lie sprawled, half shamefully bathed in the dazzling sunlight pouring down from the high ceiling, his whole body soaking in the clear water of the tub — the fatigue, or rather that peculiar, indulgent intoxication of it, was a pleasure that no amount of money could buy.

Denkiburo no kaishi jiken by Unno Jūza (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And so, with the capture of the culprit, the dreadful mystery of the "electric bath" met its full and terrible explanation. In time that film was sent on to the Metropolitan Police Department, and screened, just once, before the assembled officials who had taken part in the investigation and the department's own senior officers. But every one of those present at that screening, rather than the death agony of a young woman they had expected to see, found themselves shown, upon the pure white tiles' waves, the bewitchingly beautiful, swimming figure of Oteru's body — a figure that might well have been mistaken for the dance of a southern-sea mermaid — and every eye there, though it spoke no word, could not help but drift with a look of rapt fascination. For that film, whether by design or by pure chance, had been shot with a high-speed camera.

Denkiburo no kaishi jiken by Unno Jūza (Aozora Bunko)
