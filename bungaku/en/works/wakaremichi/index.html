About this work

A short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the magazine Kokumin no Tomo in January 1896, from the creative period called her fourteen miraculous months. In a poor back tenement, a woman who takes in sewing and a shop-boy live pressed together like brother and sister; it draws their faint affection and the parting that comes on New Year's Eve. Against the fate of a woman who cannot live without selling herself, it sets the purity of a boy who cannot accept that fact, and shows the sorrow of those at the bottom of Meiji society.

Summary

O-Kyō, a young woman who lives by taking in sewing in a poor back tenement, and Kichizō, a short little apprentice at an umbrella shop, are on terms as close as sister and brother, and talk over the miseries of their days together. On the last night of the year, when Kichizō calls at O-Kyō's house as usual, she suddenly tells him that from tomorrow she is going into a mansion "to be a kept woman." Weary and sick of her poor life, O-Kyō has made up her mind to accept the offer. Kichizō, who in a world where he has no one to lean on has been comforted only by the feeling of a brother for a sister, is thrown into a violent confusion between the shock of losing the one support of his heart and his pity for a woman who must sell herself for money; and he clings to O-Kyō's sleeve as she goes.

Characters

  • O-Kyō　A young woman who lives by taking in sewing in a poor back tenement, and is as close as a sister to Kichizō. One of the two central figures
  • Kichizō　A short little apprentice at an umbrella shop, nicknamed "Issunbōshi," who has only O-Kyō to lean on

The opening (original text)

"Is O-Kyō in?" — and there came from outside the window the sound of someone knocking at the boards. "Who is it? I have gone to bed already; come tomorrow," she lied. "It doesn't matter if you're in bed; get up and open it. It's Kichi from the umbrella shop. It's me," said the voice, a little louder. "You tiresome boy, what have you come to say at this hour? Begging for rice cakes again?" she laughed. "I'll open it now; wait a moment" — and saying so, sticking her needle into the sewing she had half done, up stood a woman of twenty or so, of a stylish sort.

Wakaremichi by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Then will you give up going as a kept woman?" he said, turning her round. "It is not a place anyone goes to because she wishes it; but I have made up my mind that it must be so, and I am sorry, but I cannot listen to you." And Kichi, staring at her with tears in his eyes, said, "O-Kyō, for pity's sake, take your hand off my shoulder."

Wakaremichi by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)
