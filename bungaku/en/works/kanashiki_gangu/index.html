About this work

Ishikawa Takuboku's second collection of tanka, published by Shinonome Shoten in June 1912, two months after his death. It holds 194 poems written between A Handful of Sand and the end, and takes its title from a line among his papers: that poems are his sad playthings. Days in a sickbed, poverty, thoughts of his family, an interest in the coming age — all sung in the plain measure of three-line tanka, in the words of ordinary life. The furthest point of the poetry of daily living in modern tanka.

Summary

"When I breathe, there is a sound in my chest — a sound lonelier than the winter wind!" The collection holds a hundred and ninety-four poems from the last part of Takuboku's life, from the end of 1910 until just before his death, when his lungs were failing. Days away from work, wandering along the riverbank; the sky seen from a hospital window; the emptiness of closing one's eyes and finding nothing rise in the mind. Tenderness toward his small child, guilt toward his wife and mother, and, for all that, an expectation of "a new tomorrow" he cannot put away. He watches the movement of his own heart even in forgetting to take his medicine, and poem by poem the daily life at the pillow of a dying man is set down. Near the end of the book comes a small scene of ordinary living: turning to look at a white dog going by outside the garden, he talks over with his wife whether they should keep a dog. Published after his death under the editorship of his friend Toki Aika, the book sings the sorrow of daily living in the language of daily living, and with A Handful of Sand it has made Takuboku's name imperishable.

The opening (original text)

When I breathe, there is a sound in my chest. A sound lonelier than the winter wind! I close my eyes, and nothing rises in my mind. Lonely — and so I open my eyes again.

Sad Toys by Ishikawa Takuboku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Having my pillow brought out to the edge of the veranda, for the first time in a long while I make friends with the evening sky. A white dog goes by outside the garden. Turning to look after it, I put it to my wife that we might keep a dog.

Famous passages

When I draw breath, there is a sound that rings within my chest. A sound lonelier than the winter wind!

The opening poem of his second tanka collection, kept written up to the year before his death. A poignant self-observation, listening closely to the sound within lungs ravaged by disease.

Somehow, this year seems likely to bring good things. New Year's morning, clear and windless.

A poem on the bright mood of New Year's morning. Takuboku, suffering from illness and poverty in his last years, records a fleeting hope in plain words.
