About this work

The sentence that conveys Miyazawa Kenji's thought most directly is here: that until the whole world becomes happy, there can be no happiness for the individual. It is the outline of lectures written to be given to farmers at the Rasu Chijin Association, setting out in short, aphorism-like lines Kenji's ideal of binding labour, art and faith into one. The closing words — that eternal incompletion is itself completion — are equally well known.

Summary

"We are all of us farmers; we are very busy and our work is hard. / I want to find a way to live more brightly and with more life in it" — so the introduction addresses the reader. He wishes to argue where the proofs of modern science, the experiments of seekers after the Way, and our own intuition agree. And placed at the center is the line: "So long as the whole world does not become happy, there can be no happiness for the individual." Our fathers and masters of old lived joyfully though they had little, and there was art there, and religion. But now we have only labor and survival; religion is worn out and has been replaced by cold science, and art has fallen away into desolation. Just for that reason, Kenji preaches, "With art, set that gray labor burning." From there the argument goes on to the nature of a farmers' art, its fields, its making, its producers and its criticism, and sets out a conception of daily life itself as one enormous work of art. "This is the sun Japan in the space of the Milky Way, the fields of the province of Rikuchū." And in the conclusion he says that what we need is a transparent will that wraps the galaxy, and a huge strength and heat, and goes on: "Our road ahead is steep, and it shines," "The poet enjoys even pain" — closing with the line, "Eternal incompletion, this is completion." When you have understood, throw away arguments like this too; here there is nothing but the thought of Miyazawa Kenji in 1926.

Famous passages

Until the whole world is happy, there can be no happiness for the individual

A passage from the introduction. It directly expresses Kenji's refusal to separate personal happiness from the world as a whole, and his commitment to thinking about people's lives and art together.
