About this work

A children's story Akutagawa wrote about cowardice, self-loathing and atonement. Shiro, a white dog who ran away and left a black dog to the dog-catchers, turns as black as pitch all over, as if in punishment. Given up even by his owner and in despair, he piles up good deeds at the risk of his life, trying to win back the self he lost. Animals as protagonists, and a serious treatment of conscience and recovery: one of his central stories for children, read by adults as well.

Summary

One day on his walk, the dog Shiro sees a black dog he knows being stalked by a dog-catcher. He starts to call for help, but fearing that the noose will fall on him too, he abandons the black dog and bolts. And when he gets home, the body that had been pure white has somehow turned as black as ink. His mistress and the young master do not recognize him as their own dog, and drive him away; and Shiro is crushed by loathing for his own cowardice. Understanding that a heart blackened with dirt has shown itself on his body, he decides that his life is worth nothing, and goes about the country performing one act of self-sacrifice after another — saving people from a burning house, shielding a child from a runaway horse. Even when he has become famous enough to appear in the newspapers he still cannot die; and worn out, he returns to his old house, meaning to sleep beside the kennel and to die after one last look at his master's face. When he wakes the next morning, his body has turned pure white again. Held in the arms of the delighted children, Shiro sheds tears.

Characters

  • Shiro　A dog whose body turns black as punishment for deserting a friend, and who tries to win himself back by risking his life for others
  • Kuro　The black dog, Shiro's companion, killed by the dog-catcher
  • The young lady and the young master　The brother and sister who own Shiro

The opening (original text)

It was one afternoon in spring. A dog called Shiro was walking along a quiet street, sniffing at the ground. On both sides of the narrow street ran hedges putting out their buds, and here and there among them cherry trees were in bloom. Keeping close to a hedge, Shiro happened to turn down a side street. But no sooner had he turned than he stopped short, as though he had had a great fright.

Shiro by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Oh, look, Shiro is crying!" said the young lady, holding Shiro tight and looking up at her brother's face. And the young master — see how grandly he speaks! "Huh. You're crying yourself!"

Shiro by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)
