About this work

An essay by Izumi Kyōka, beginning by counting the names of the dolls — cherry dolls, willow dolls, rape-blossom dolls, bean dolls. Memories of his mother's doll stand, the late festival of the north country, and dolls glimpsed on his travels are strung together in his own splendid style. A piece in which a doting love of small, dear things and a longing for his dead mother show through everywhere.

Summary

'Dolls — of course there are the paired dolls, man and wife. Cherry-blossom dolls, willow dolls, rape-flower dolls, peach-blossom dolls, and violet dolls in white, scarlet, and purple' — so the essay begins, counting off the names of dolls one after another, running on to the humble horsetail dolls of the countryside and the shared-umbrella dolls of the spring rains. Black display shelves, miniature shrine-cabinets, three-tiered stands, chests of drawers, long chests, all in gold maki-e lacquer with silver fittings. Opening a drawer no bigger than a little finger to find it lined in red inside is a beauty all its own. Glass vessels holding white sake, small flounder and shrimp arranged on scarlet felt, Narihira clams — the smaller each thing is, the more endearing, and set out together they look, Kyōka writes, like a glimpse of the Dragon Palace itself. If someone should ask, 'Now, where did you ever see such dolls?' the answer is: they are the dolls of his mother, remembered from when he was six or seven. His old home lies far off, beyond the mountains. They could hardly have been costly things, but thinking of how even a little sake cup for play, its base tied with red thread, or a jar of konpeitō candy, had been carried all that long road from Edo by horse and palanquin, even the Nanking sand inside a box of chiyogami paper becomes, in memory, a ruby, an emerald. In his home province in the north, the snow had not yet melted by the third month, so the Doll's Festival, it seems, was kept in the fourth month instead — just the time when the windows, shut up all winter, were thrown open, the roar of the wild sea turned to a spring breeze, and the plum, the cherry, and the peach all came into bloom together. The second half of the essay turns to a scene glimpsed along the Tōkaidō during a journey — the reflection of a doll-display shelf hanging upside down among willow branches, a white rainbow arching over a bridge, heat-shimmer playing over the Abe River. Before a pretty girl standing outside a rice-cake shop, Kyōka silently wishes, 'may good fortune come to this young girl,' and, playfully wishing for a doll of Genji in imitation-Murasaki style, laughs at himself, saying, 'I'm afraid I'm getting a little drunk around here,' and lays down his brush.

The opening (original text)

Dolls — of course there are the paired dolls, man and wife. Cherry-blossom dolls, willow dolls, rape-flower dolls, peach-blossom dolls, and violet dolls in white, scarlet, and purple. In the countryside, horsetail dolls, dandelion dolls. Shared-umbrella dolls for the spring rains. Dolls in the mood of the Asazuma boat, its sleeve rowing lightly through small waves. The five court musicians, the ladies-in-waiting. Only that one doll leading a Pekingese dog, I could wish away, for it has neither form nor grace. Even to count off paper dolls, island dolls, bean-sized dolls, one-mon dolls, is enough to stir a modest, nostalgic fondness.

Hinagatari by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

What I would want — if it could somehow be had — is a Genji doll in the style of the imitation-Murasaki tales, as fine in form as one of Kunisada's colored prints: Hanagiri first of all, then Lady Fuji, Murasaki, Tasogare, and Katsuragi — Katsuragi being, as everyone knows, none other than the Lady of the Misty Moon. Neither shining clear, nor clouded through and through, this night in spring... I'm afraid I'm getting a little drunk around here.

Hinagatari by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)
