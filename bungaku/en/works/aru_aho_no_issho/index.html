About this work

A posthumous autobiographical work, finished just before Akutagawa's suicide and entrusted to his friend Kume Masao. In fifty-one short fragments, from "1: The Age" to "51: Defeat," it sets down the life of an artist — the author himself — as sharp poetic pieces. Sentences that read like aphorisms on life, art, madness and death follow one another, marking the course of a mind driven into what he called a "vague unease." Read, with "Cogwheels," as the furthest point Akutagawa reached.

Summary

"Whether this manuscript should be published at all, and if so when and where, I leave entirely to you" — the work opens with Akutagawa's preface addressed to his friend Kume Masao. The text itself consists of fifty-one short numbered fragments, from "1: The Age" to "51: Defeat." The elation of youth, looking down on life as if from the top of a ladder before the shelves at Maruzen; teachers and friends; love and marriage; the joy and the torment of writing; the fear of madness; the friction with his family and with society — the events that trace Akutagawa's own life are set out one after another as sharp fragments stripped of explanation. As the sequence advances, shadow gains on light and the shadow of death on life, and the narrator wears away nerve and body alike. In the last fragment, "Defeat," the hand that holds the pen has begun to shake, his mind is never clear except in the hour after a dose of Veronal, and he supports his hand-to-mouth existence only as a man leans on a thin sword whose edge has long since gone — so he quietly sets down his own defeat, and the work closes.

Characters

  • I (he)　The narrator, an artist looking back over his life. The figure carries a strong impression of Akutagawa Ryūnosuke himself
  • Kume Masao　The writer and friend to whom this posthumous manuscript was entrusted. The preface is addressed to him

The opening (original text)

Whether this manuscript should be published at all, and if so at what time and in what magazine, I should like to leave entirely to you.

A Fool's Life by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The hand that held his pen had begun to shake. Saliva ran from his mouth as well. His head was never once clear except after waking from a dose of 0.8 of Veronal — and even then it stayed clear for barely half an hour or an hour. He lived from day to day in that dim half-light. Leaning, as it were, on a thin sword whose edge had all been chipped away.

Famous passages

“Life is not worth a single line of Baudelaire.”

A line from a scene atop a bookstore ladder, looking down on the people below. Placed at the head of these autobiographical fragments, left unpublished at his death, it reflects both Akutagawa's art-above-all stance and a bottomless despair.

He merely lived from day to day in the half-dark—leaning, as it were, on a slender sword whose blade was chipped away.

The close of “Defeat,” the final section of this autobiographical sequence of fragments. A piercing self-portrait of his own last years as a man leaning on a slender, chipped sword.
