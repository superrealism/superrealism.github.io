About this work

A very short piece Dazai published in the magazine Wakakusa in 1939. The narrator, stuck for songs and moved down from the Misaka pass to Kōfu to write fiction, is privately saved day by day by the clear singing of the mill girls that reaches him from the silk factory across from his lodging — and then, on a winter night, he hears a drunken young man shout proudly to his sister, "I can speak English." That innocent voice, saying that he attends night school and may be called up before long, brings back to the narrator's chest the spirit of song he had nearly forgotten. A quietly beautiful small piece from the same Kōfu stay as "One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji."

Summary

Having given up composing tanka and drifted idly about Tokyo, 'I' gradually grasp, however dimly, the direction my writing should take, and set to work on a full-length novel. While working on it upstairs at a teahouse on the Misaka Pass in Kōshū, I find the winter cold unbearable and move to a lodging house on the outskirts of Kōfu. Sitting at my desk in a sunlit room, I hear, from a silk-reeling mill across the lane, the massed voices of the factory girls singing. Among them is one especially clear, lovely voice, and I find myself laying down my pen to listen, even wishing I could thank whoever it belongs to. On a cold night in February, a drunken young man's rough voice rises in the lane by the mill. Over and over he shouts 'I can speak English' at his elder sister, one of the factory girls. Haltingly but earnestly he pleads his case — that he has been secretly attending night school without even telling their mother, that he may soon be called up to the front, that surely he is a good boy. Sliding my paper screen open a crack and looking down, I see the young man, in a white raincoat out of season, standing with his back against the wall, while above him his sister leans out of the window, gazing down at her brother. That drunken English, 'I can speak,' strikes me almost painfully to the heart. In the beginning was the Word — for a moment 'I' feel as though I have remembered a forgotten song. A slight and trivial scene, raised here by Dazai into an unforgettable lyricism — this is one of his finer small works.

The opening (original text)

Suffering is a night of endurance. Resignation, its morning. Is this world itself but a labor of resignation? An enduring of loneliness? So it was that my youth, eaten away day by day like something gnawed by insects, at last found what happiness it could within a mean and narrow lane — or so I am told it goes. Having lost the voice of my own poetry, I drifted for a time in Tokyo, idle and unproductive, until at last I began, in a low mutter, to set down something that was not quite poetry — something more like a 'murmuring of daily life.' Little by little, through my own work, I came to sense the direction my writing ought to take — well, something along these lines, perhaps — and with something resembling confidence, I set to work on the long novel I had been quietly planning for some time.

The ending (original text)

That drunken man's English, "I can speak," struck me almost painfully. In the beginning was the Word; all things were made through it. For a moment I felt as though I had remembered a forgotten song. It was a trivial enough scene, and yet, to me, unforgettable. Whether that factory girl that night was the one with the lovely voice, I do not know. Probably not, I imagine.
