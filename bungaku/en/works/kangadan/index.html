About this work

A short story Kōda Rohan published in 1925, known as a fine fantastical work of his last years. Professor Taiki Bansei, come to a temple in the mountains to rest a case of nervous exhaustion, is led on a night of downpour and flood to a hermitage in the dark hills, and, gazing at an old painting, is drawn into the world inside it. The space between art and reality, life and enlightenment, drawn in Rohan's own weighty and humorous style.

Summary

Born into a poor family and, after years of hardship, finally making his way into university as a late-blooming student, "Master Great-Vessel-Late-to-Complete" (Taiki Bansei Sensei) is struck down by the nervous exhaustion that had just then begun to be recognized as an ailment, brought on by his excessive studies, and sets out to convalesce in a place of quiet mountains and clear waters. One rainy day he arrives at a ruined temple — said to have once been a fine one — in a small village before a mountain gate, and is given lodging by a curt old priest and a young monk named Zōkai. As the sound of the endless rain — a steady roar — fills the world entire, the master is seized by the strange sensation that he has become someone other than himself, some other self in some other world. At midnight, floodwaters from the mountain stream surge up into the temple grounds, and Zōkai leads the master, clinging to a single umbrella, along a pitch-black mountain path through the driving rain, to take refuge in a safe hermitage beside a waterfall. In the hermitage sits an old monk, deaf, motionless as a living clay figure. In an inner room, the master turns over in his mind the smoke-blackened tablet reading "The bridge flows, the water does not flow," and before long finds himself gazing, transfixed, at an old genre painting of mountains and water hanging on the wall. As he brings the lamp closer and traces its details, the sailing ships, the wine-shops, and the people within the painting begin to move, vividly alive, and a boatman poling a small boat among the reeds calls out to him, "Won't you come aboard? Won't you come aboard?" Just as the master is about to blurt out, without thinking, "I'm coming now!" — a cold wind gusts through some gap, the lamp flickers, and boat and boatman recede into the distance... Afterward his illness was entirely cured, but the master never returned to his studies; he is said to have buried his very name somewhere deep in the mountains and become nothing more than a simple farmer. A late masterwork in which Rohan, in a style at once weighty and touched with wit, depicts the borderland between art and reality, between life and enlightenment.

Characters

  • Master Great-Vessel-Late-to-Complete (Taiki Bansei Sensei)　A man born into a poor family who, after years of hardship, made his way to university as a late-blooming student. Afflicted with nervous exhaustion, he convalesces at a mountain temple, and on a night of torrential rain is drawn into the world of an old painting. His illness is later cured, but he never returns to his studies and becomes a farmer
  • Zōkai　A young monk at the ruined temple. On the night of the flood, he leads the master to the hermitage
  • The old monk　A deaf old monk who sits motionless in the hermitage

The opening (original text)

This happened a very long while ago now, but I once heard a curious tale from a certain man, and I have not forgotten it even now — though the names of the people and places in it have long since scattered off to who knows where, like smoke from a bonfire deep in the woods. The man who told me the tale had a friend, a certain So-and-so. This friend was, in every ordinary respect, a thoroughly capable man; though a late bloomer in his studies, he had managed to make his way as far as his second year at university.

Kangadan by Kōda Rohan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Oi!" the boatman called out, opening his mouth wide. Master Bansei smiled. "I'm coming now!" — he very nearly answered, without thinking. In that instant, a cold wind, slipping in through some crack, came blowing through, and the lamp flame wavered. Boat and boatman, having drifted in from far off to near, now drifted away again from near to far. It was all over in a single instant, with nothing before or after. Outside, the sound of the rain: a steady roar. Master Great-Vessel-Late-to-Complete told this much of the tale to a close friend of his. His illness was entirely cured. But he was never again seen at his old university. He seems to have resolved to bury his name somewhere among the mountains and waters, and become an ordinary man once and for all. Someone once said they had seen him, in a certain place, tanned dark by the sun, become nothing more than a plain farmer. Whether he was a great vessel that failed to be completed, or a great vessel already complete — that, one supposes, was no longer any concern of the master's.

Kangadan by Kōda Rohan (Aozora Bunko)
