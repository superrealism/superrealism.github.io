About this work

A novella Yokomitsu Riichi published in the magazine Kaizō in 1930. Set in a nameplate factory, its peculiar style — full stops without paragraph breaks — renders directly the stream of consciousness of workmen taken over by mutual suspicion. Who is betraying whom, and what is truth and what delusion, are never made clear, and their psychology accelerates fused with the bodily sensation of being eaten away by chemicals. The high point of the work called new psychologism, and one of the outstanding experimental works in modern Japanese literature.

Summary

"I" have drifted into the employ of an odd master who runs a nameplate works, and taken on the work of handling dangerous chemicals. My fellow workman Karube suspects me of being a spy sent to steal the master's secret, keeps a constant watch on me and sometimes even lays hands on me; but little by little I come to overwhelm him in knowledge. Then a new craftsman, Yashiki, is taken on, and both Karube and I begin to look at Yashiki with suspicion as the agent after the secret of the formula. Uneasy rumors about the master's wife are tangled up in it as well, and it becomes less and less clear who is a friend and who an enemy, what is real and what is my own delusion. One day, when the mutual suspicion of the three has come to its height, Yashiki drinks the chemicals and ends his life; and "I" can no longer escape the doubt that it was I who did it. "Let someone else judge me in my place" — the story closes with the monologue of an "I" who has lost all means of making out the truth.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, who has drifted into a nameplate works and is eaten away little by little by suspicion of those around him
  • The master　The proprietor of the works. Innocent and fond of his children, and yet somehow out of his senses
  • Karube　A fellow craftsman who goes on suspecting me of being a spy sent against the house
  • Yashiki　A newly hired craftsman, suspected by both Karube and me, who at last drinks the chemicals and dies

The opening (original text)

At first I sometimes thought that the master of the house I was in might be mad. Watching him, I saw him get angry because his child, not yet three, disliked him, and say that there was no law by which a child might dislike his own father.

Machine by Yokomitsu Riichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I no longer understand myself. I only feel the sharp point of the machine coming closer, inch by inch, aimed at me. Let someone else judge me in my place. For if you ask me what it is that I have done, there is no way that I should know.

Machine by Yokomitsu Riichi (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

In the beginning I sometimes wondered whether my employer might not be mad.

An opening written in a distinctive style, stripped down in its punctuation. The psychology of four men at a nameplate workshop meshes together like clockwork.
