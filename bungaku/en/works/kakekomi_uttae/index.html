About this work

A short story published in the magazine Chūō Kōron in February 1940. Judas Iscariot rushes to the authorities to denounce his teacher, and the whole of it is given as one unbroken monologue. The swing between love and hatred, devotion and betrayal, is carried in a driving colloquial rhythm from beginning to end. He is said to have dictated it to his wife at a single sitting; it is famous as the point at which Dazai's art of the speaking voice reached its height.

Summary

"Let me tell you. Let me tell you, sir. That man is cruel." A man rushes in to an official and pours out, without pausing for breath, his charges against his master, "that man." He is the same age as his master, and how much he has done for him behind the scenes all this time; and for all that, the man despises him and makes much only of the other disciples. The burning jealousy he felt when Mary poured costly ointment on his master's feet, his irritation at a flight that may not last the day — and even as he speaks his hatred, a rapture at his master's beauty comes flooding out, so that the confession swings violently: kill him, and then again, I only wanted to be loved myself. At last the man tells them where his master is and takes the thirty pieces of silver. "Everything I have told you has been a lie." Whether he loved him, or only wanted the money — not knowing himself, the man gives his name at the end: "My name is Judas the merchant. Heh, heh. Judas Iscariot."

Characters

  • I (Judas)　Judas Iscariot, torn between love and hatred for his master, who rushes in to lay his charge
  • That man (Jesus)　Judas's master. Beautiful, and never letting go of Judas's heart
  • Sir　The official who hears Judas's charge

The opening (original text)

Let me tell you. Let me tell you, sir. That man is cruel. Cruel. Yes. He is a hateful creature. A wicked man. Ah. I cannot bear it. He must not be allowed to live. Yes, yes. I will tell it calmly. That man must not be allowed to live. He is the enemy of the world. Yes, I will tell you everything, all of it, the whole of it. I know where he is. I will take you there at once. Cut him to pieces and kill him.

The ending (original text)

Yes, sir. Everything I have told you has been a lie. I followed that man about because I wanted money. Oh, that must be it. Tonight I saw plainly that he was never going to let me make anything by it, and so, being a merchant, I turned my coat quickly. Money. There is nothing in the world but money. Thirty pieces of silver — how splendid. I will take it. I am a stingy merchant. I want it more than I can say. Yes, thank you kindly. Yes, yes. I forgot to say: my name is Judas the merchant. Heh, heh. Judas Iscariot.

Famous passages

I have something to tell you. I have something to tell you, my lord. That man is cruel. Cruel. Yes. A hateful man. A wicked man. Ah. I cannot bear it. He must not be let to live.

An opening that pushes forward entirely through Judas's own breathless, first-person accusation. The rapid-fire short sentences give the very sound of love flipped inside out into hatred.

My name is Judas, the merchant. Heh heh. Judas Iscariot.

The closing, in which the narrator reveals his own name at the end of his monologue. Only now is the reader told whose confession this has been all along.
