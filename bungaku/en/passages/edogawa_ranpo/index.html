Famous passages by Edogawa Ranpo

Well-known lines from the works of Edogawa Ranpo.

8 passages

The Two-Sen Copper Coin

“I envy that thief.” Things had grown so desperate between the two of them that words like these were exchanged.

The opening of the work regarded as the starting point of orthodox Japanese detective fiction. A conversation that begins as a lament over poverty rolls on into a battle of wits over a cipher.

The Case of the Murder on D. Hill

It was a sultry evening in early September. I was sipping iced coffee at my usual café, the Hakubaiken, halfway along the main street of D. Hill.

The opening of the work in which the great detective Akechi Kogorō first appears. From a sultry-night café begins the story of the detective and narrator who would come to represent Japanese detective fiction.

The Psychological Test

Why Fukiya Seiichirō came to conceive the dreadful crime I am about to relate, I do not know in detail. And even if I did, it would have little to do with this story.

The opening of the story. Brushing aside the criminal's motive as unknown, the narration narrows its focus to the steps of the crime and the battle over the psychological test, announcing an inverted detective story told from the criminal's side.

The Red Chamber

Seven solemn men who had gathered in search of abnormal excitement (I was one of them) sat sunk in deep armchairs upholstered in scarlet velvet in the “Red Room,” fitted out expressly for the purpose, waiting impatiently for tonight's speaker to begin some strange tale.

The opening of the story. Seven men seeking a thrill to relieve their boredom listen to an uncanny tale in a room swathed in scarlet. The eerie stage setting rises up in a single sentence.

The Stalker in the Attic

Perhaps it was a kind of mental illness. Whatever game, whatever occupation, whatever he tried, Gōda Saburō found nothing in this world the least bit interesting.

The opening of the story. Gōda Saburō's boredom, finding nothing interesting in the world whatever he does, leads him at last to walking the attic and to a perfect crime. A classic instance of the abnormal psychology born of ennui that Ranpo loved to portray.

The Human Chair

Who could possibly imagine anything so absurd as a human being hiding inside a chair?

A line from a letter confessing to having taken up residence inside a chair. Depicting the abnormal lurking within an everyday piece of furniture, this story became synonymous with Ranpo's name.

The Traveler with the Pasted Rag Picture

If this tale was not a dream of mine, nor a vision born of some passing madness, then surely that man who traveled with his pasted rag picture was the madman.

From its very first line, this opening suspends the boundary between dream and madness. It begins the story of a man met on a train home from viewing a mirage, and the lover who lives on within his pasted rag picture.

The Fiend with Twenty Faces

In those days, in every town and every house across Tokyo, whenever two or more people met face to face, they would talk of the mysterious “Fiend with Twenty Faces,” as casually as remarking on the weather.

The opening of the Boy Detectives' Club series. The sheer spread of rumor alone conveys the enormity of the mysterious thief, making readers wait before Akechi Kogorō and young Kobayashi appear.
