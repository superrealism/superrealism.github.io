About this work

A fine short work from Nagai Kafū's middle period, published in Shinshōsetsu in March 1921. Neither quite fiction nor quite essay, it sets down against the steady falling of autumn rain the loneliness of a man living alone and his grief for a vanishing Edo. The narrator, long alone since parting from his wife and breaking with his mistress, is drawn by the sound of rain at midnight into his dead father's study and remembers him among the books of poetry and the utensils of an old man of letters. A lyrical piece deeply coloured by Kafū's turning his back on the changes of the age and mourning an elegance that is being lost.

Summary

"I," long since divorced from my wife and having broken with my mistress as well, live alone, and in the chill of early autumn feel that the rest of my life will most likely draw quietly to its close, like an overcast autumn day that never quite comes to rain before dark. In this new age, when abstinence from drink and tobacco is loudly urged and even the elegant taste for sencha tea and smoking is falling out of fashion, all I can do is watch, with a kind of loneliness, the culture and feeling that have come down from Edo slip away. One midnight, woken by the sound of heavy rain beating on the roof like a waterfall, I go around the houses worrying over leaks, and end up in the detached study my late father was fond of in his lifetime. A rosewood desk, a crystal paperweight, a scroll of poetry on the wall — I let my mind wander for a while amid my father's presence still lingering there and the world of the old man of letters he was. In the sound of the autumn rain that will not stop, Kafū laments his solitude and mourns a changing world, while yet drawing an inexhaustible poetic feeling out of that very loneliness.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, long parted from both wife and mistress and living alone. On a night of autumn rain, remembers his dead father and a vanishing Edo in his solitude.

The opening (original text)

The two hundred and tenth day of that year was, I believe, a cool, moonlit night. The two hundred and twentieth day that followed, another day of ill omen for storms, passed almost without my noticing it either, with only the hurried cries of the evening cicadas in the same lingering summer heat and westering sun as always, before night came on. Once night had fallen I did hear, as if to make some show for the day's ill name, the sound of rain beginning to fall, but the wind neither tore the banana leaves nor laid low the asters or cockscombs.

Ame shōshō by Nagai Kafū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

One of these days I shall write of it again, under some title like "A Record of the House for Sale."
