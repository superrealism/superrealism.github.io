About this work

A linked sequence of very short fantastical pieces, serialized in the Asahi Shimbun in July and August 1908. Each of the ten nights opens with the line "I dreamed this dream." Love and death, faith, art and the unease of modern people are set down in the grammar of dreams rather than of logic, which gives the work an unusual place among Sōseki's writings. Beautiful and unsettling by turns, like prose poems, it continues to draw new readings.

Summary

Ten linked dreams, each beginning "I had a dream like this." In the first night a woman, dying, leaves him with the words: "Wait a hundred years for me. I will surely come to meet you." As the man passes days beyond counting beside her grave, a single white lily blooms, and he realizes that the hundred years have already come. In the third night a man walking a night road with his blind child on his back is led by the child's voice until the memory of a murder he committed a hundred years ago is forced upon him. In the sixth night Unkei, the Kamakura sculptor, is carving guardian kings at the gate of the Gokokuji in the Meiji age. Told that the brows and the nose are buried in the wood and one has only to dig them out with the chisel, the narrator goes home and tries it on his firewood — but in the wood of Meiji there proved to be no guardian kings at all. Elsewhere — a barber's shop seen in a mirror, a man who throws himself from a ship, the story of Shōtarō in his panama hat carried off by a woman — a promise made in love, the memory of a crime, doubts about art and the age, and a nameless unease are drawn with the sudden leaps and the vivid images that belong to dreams.

Characters

  • I　The narrator who dreams each of the nights
  • The woman　In the first night. She dies leaving the words "Wait a hundred years for me"
  • Unkei　In the sixth night. The Kamakura-period sculptor, appearing in the Meiji age to carve the guardian kings
  • Shōtarō　In the tenth night. A handsome man in a panama hat, carried off into the mountains by a woman

The opening (original text)

I had a dream like this. I was sitting at the bedside with my arms folded when the woman, lying on her back, said in a quiet voice that she was going to die now. Her long hair was spread over the pillow, and her soft oval face lay in the midst of it. Under the pure white of her cheeks a warm color of blood showed just as it should, and the color of her lips was red, of course. She did not look in the least like dying. But in that quiet voice she said distinctly that she was going to die. And I too felt certain that she was.

The ending (original text)

Ken had told the story of Shōtarō as far as this, and then he said that it was no good looking at women too much. I thought he was right. But Ken said he would like to have Shōtarō's panama hat. Shōtarō will not pull through. The panama will be Ken's.

Famous passages

I had a dream like this.

The short phrase that opens each of the ten nights. Offering no reason or explanation, it draws the reader straight into the logic of dream — the emblematic opening of Sōseki's experimental sequence of stories.

“Wait for me — a hundred years.”

A promise left behind by a dying woman on the First Night. Because of this single line, the ending — a white lily blooming a hundred years later — quietly holds together within the logic of the dream.

“So the hundred years had already come,” I realized then for the first time.

The close of the First Night. The moment the dreamer, who has waited beside the woman's grave, kisses a white lily that has bloomed there, he realizes that the promised hundred years had already passed.

In the end I realized that no guardian kings were buried in the wood of Meiji. And with that, I also more or less understood why Unkei was still alive today.

The close of the Sixth Night. Having watched Unkei carve guardian kings in the Meiji era, the dreamer tries carving wood himself and comes to this realization, tinged with bitter criticism of modern Japan.
