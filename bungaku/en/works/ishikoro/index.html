About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Fuji in 1944. Tada Shinzō, an odd samurai who brings back stones from the roadside instead of enemy heads from the battlefield. The world laughs, and his wife Matsuo too has thought little of him — until she comes to see the real worth of a husband who seeks neither name nor advancement and lives simply as a fighting man. A fine short story quietly drawing how hard it is to see a person's true value.

Summary

At Kofu Castle in Kai, it was the custom for daughters of respectable families to take turns going up to the castle to make what were called "Lord Shingen's hidden straw sandals." The girls, watching the samurai come and go from the castle, would amuse themselves gossiping about who had won honor in battle. Among them, Matsuo, daughter of the samurai commander Akiyama Hōkinokami, was whispered about behind her back as arrogant — precisely because of her outstanding beauty, and because of her father's authority — and was always left out of the group. One day, hearing the other girls laugh in amusement that "Lord Tada picks up stones off the road on the battlefield instead of enemy heads," Matsuo felt her heart give an inexplicable start, and found herself sharply rebuking them: "How dare you speak ill of a man you don't even know." Before long Matsuo herself is married to that very Tada Shinzō, but she is disappointed to find her husband exactly the eccentric of common report, carefully keeping his stones in a lacquered box. It is her father Hōkinokami's words that at last open her eyes: "Seeking neither fame nor advancement, living out, in silence, the path he believes in as a single warrior — that is the kind of man Tada Shinzō is." Matsuo, who had laughed at the world's gossip while failing, herself, to see her husband's true worth, is overcome with heartbreaking regret. Returning to her rooms and lifting the lid of the box, she finds within it nothing but a few ordinary stones from the roadside. She seems to hear her husband's voice: "I can't help loving things like this." "My husband... you are truly a fine man." A masterful short story by Yamamoto depicting how hard it is to see another's true worth.

Characters

The opening (original text)

Out on the veranda of the rope-making hall in the Retirement Bailey, bright with the late-autumn afternoon sun, more than ten young girls had been whispering and chattering noisily among themselves for some time now. Through the cherry trees, already stripped bare of leaves, one could see, across the moat, samurai coming down from the castle in twos and threes along the far road. The girls, pointing at the men now, were amusing themselves as young girls will, sizing each one up. Matsuo sat apart from the rest of the group, making straw sandals busily by herself.

Ishikoro by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"My husband... you are truly a fine man," she murmured, and, unable to bear it any longer, Matsuo broke into muffled sobs... Before long, when her husband returned in triumph, there would surely be one more stone added there — an ordinary stone, invisible to others, yet a symbol of countless deeds of honor and valor...
