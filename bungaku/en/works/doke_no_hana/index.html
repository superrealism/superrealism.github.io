About this work

An important early work published in the magazine Nihon Rōmanha in 1935. It follows the convalescence of Ōba Yōzō, a young man who survived a double suicide off Kamakura in which only the woman died, while the author repeatedly appears in the text to discuss how a novel should be written — an experimental I-novel. Giving his protagonist the same name he would later use in No Longer Human, and laying his own attempted suicide over it, Dazai renders the self-consciousness of a young man playing the clown, and a doubt about writing itself, by bold means.

Summary

Ōba Yōzō, who threw himself into the sea at Kamakura with a woman meaning to die together, has let her die alone and lies in a hospital by the shore, saved himself. In talk half banter and half emptiness with Hida, a would-be painter who comes to see him, with young Kosuge and with the women nursing him, Yōzō swings between his feeling for the dead woman and the clownish part he plays as a survivor. The story follows the scenes of his convalescence quietly; and into it the author, "I," breaks in again and again, telling openly how this novel is being written and where his own pen is going wrong. With the story of the characters and the monologue of the man writing it crossing each other, Yōzō looks down at the sea from the cliff. Offering neither salvation nor solution, the book closes: "And — no; that is all there is to it."

Characters

The opening (original text)

"Through me is the way into the city of woe." My friends have all left me, and look at me with sorrowful eyes. Friends, speak to me; laugh at me. Ah, my friends turn their faces away in vain. Ōba Yōzō sat up on the bed and looked at the offing. The offing was smoky with rain.

Dōke no hana by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Yōzō looked far down at the sea. From just beneath his feet the cliff dropped some three hundred feet, and Enoshima looked small directly below. In the depths of a heavy morning mist the seawater moved and swayed. And — no; that is all there is to it.

Dōke no hana by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

“Through here, the city of sorrow.”

The opening. A phrase based on the inscription on the gate of Hell in Dante's Divine Comedy, it leads the reader through the gate of the city of sorrow into the story of a young man who survived a failed double suicide.

Ah, why am I in such a hurry to pass judgment on everything? Who on earth taught me this mean spirit that cannot go on living without tying every thought up into something neat?

A passage in which the author himself steps into the middle of the story. The voice interrogating his own haste to reach conclusions breaks through the frame of the novel to reach the reader — a sign of the experimental methods of the early Dazai.
