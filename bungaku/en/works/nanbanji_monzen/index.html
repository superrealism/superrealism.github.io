About this work

A one-act play set at the gate of the southern-barbarian church in the fourth ward at Kyoto at the end of the Eiroku era. A central play by Kinoshita Mokutarō, an aesthete piece woven from the foreign colour of things brought by the southern barbarians and a taste for Japan's Christians. The light through glass, the padre's bell, and the acolyte Chōjun, who has entered the Buddhist orders and goes mad in his longing for a new mystery — it survives in literary history as a work symbolizing the vogue for the southern barbarians at the end of Meiji.

Summary

It is the close of an autumn day in the last years of the Eiroku era. Below the towering stone wall of the Nanban-ji (Southern Barbarian Temple) in Shijōbō, Kyoto, children have gathered on the road before the gate, singing: "Sunset glow, twilight glow. In the pond of Magadha the peppery fish glitter and shine. Glass flasks, tinta wine glitter and shine." A blind pilgrim, an elder sister and her younger sister, come passing by, startled by the unfamiliar sounds and the gleaming temple roof. Merrymakers and old men pass to and fro, and it becomes clear that this temple is the Nanban-ji, where the teachings of the padres are preached. Before long the dancing girl Shirahagi appears. Chōjun, a young monk once in love with her — when she was known as O-Tsuru — now finds his heart in turmoil even as he lives the religious life. He is possessed, as though bewitched, by the "new mystery" embodied in the wares and music brought from over the southern seas, and by the teaching of an unseen god. As the head monk and his novices pass by chanting sutras, Chōjun blurts out, "It is always for this mystery that I weep with longing," and at last collapses. The head monk declares it "the temptation of the heavenly demon Hajun" and departs, the sound of chanted nembutsu fading into the distance. Shirahagi runs to him and calls his name, and Chōjun, opening his eyes a slit, says: "Ah, Tsuru. How dear to me is the heart I bore for you back then, when I loved and longed for you — had that love of ours been granted then, I would have wanted no mystery at all." The curtain falls to the sound of the temple bell within the gate.

Characters

  • Shirahagi (O-Tsuru)　A dancing girl once in love with Chōjun
  • The head monk　The priest who leads the novices
  • Children and pilgrims　People passing before the temple gate

The opening (original text)

CHILDREN. (Singing.) Sunset glow, twilight glow. In the pond of Magadha the peppery fish glitter and shine. Glass flasks, tinta wine glitter and shine. The bell is ringing, the bell is ringing. In the temple hall the golden cross glitters and shines.

Nanbanji monzen by Kinoshita Mokutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

CHŌJUN. (His tone changing.) Ah, Tsuru. How dear to me is the heart I bore for you back then, when I loved and longed for you — had that love of ours been granted then, I would have wanted no mystery at all. (Chōjun closes his eyes.) The bell within the gate tolls, ending with a small chime. Curtain.
