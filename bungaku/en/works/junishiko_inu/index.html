About this work

The piece for the year of the dog — legends of the dog — serialized in four parts in Taiyō in 1922. Beginning from an origin tale of the dog told by the islanders of the south seas, he introduces a Romanian legend in which the Lord fixed the lifespans of the animals, and an Estonian tale explaining why cat and dog fell out. At the end he notes that a tale resembling Momotarō taking a dog to attack Demon Island is found in Tahiti in the south seas too.

Summary

By the account of the natives of New Britain in the South Seas, the dog once walked upright and ran very fast and killed many people. So the surviving human beings took counsel, heated breadfruit and scattered its seeds on the path where the dog went. The dog trod on them and burned its feet, and falling, burned its hands as well, and from that time could not walk upright. Those seeds, they say, remain to this day as the pads on the soles of a dog's feet — with such a tale Kumagusu begins. Next he recalls an eighteen-year-old dog he saw on his way up Mount Kōya in the fifteenth year of Meiji, and sets out the Western records of the lifespan of dogs. In a Romanian legend, when the Most High summoned the living creatures and fixed their lifespans and their ways of living, the dog disliked the hardship of eating nothing but bones for forty years and begged to have it cut to twenty; and man received the remainder. Since he received years in the same way from the ass and from the monkey, a man past thirty labors like an ass, past fifty barks like a dog, and past seventy is laughed at like a monkey. An Estonian folktale explains that the dog and the cat fell out because the cat lost the certificate the dog had left in the sheepdog's keeping. In closing he cites the Tahitian legend of Hiro, who took a dog with him to destroy a monster, and points out its likeness to the tale of Momotarō.

The opening (original text)

By the account of the natives of New Britain in the South Seas, the dog once walked upright and ran very fast and killed many people. So the surviving human beings took counsel and heated breadfruit thoroughly and scattered its seeds on the path where the dog went. The dog trod on them and burned its feet, and falling, burned its hands as well, and from that time could not walk upright. Those seeds, they say, remain to this day as the pads on the soles of a dog's feet.

Jūnishikō (inu) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Here let me only say that a parallel to Momotarō's taking a dog with him when he attacked Ogre Island is found in the South Seas as well. Hiro of Tahiti is the god of salt, and is fond of boring holes in hard stone.... Once, while Hiro slept in a cave, the god of darkness came to destroy him. A dog suddenly barked and woke its master, and Hiro rose and put down the host of his enemies. Hiro's boat and rudder, and that dog, turned into a mountain and a stone, and are said to be on that island to this day.

Jūnishikō (inu) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
