About this work

A short story published in the magazine Chūō Kōron in 1919. Taking up the story of the monk Zenkai, who really did cut the Ao-no-Dōmon tunnel through a dangerous stretch of the Yamakuni River in Buzen, it sets a man's lifelong labour of atonement for a crime against the vendetta that comes to find him. Known for an ending in which gratitude and enmity melt into one, and widely read as Kikuchi Kan's central work on the salvation of a human soul beyond simple reward and punishment.

Summary

Suspected of an affair with a woman of his master's house, Ichikurō kills his master, runs away with the woman and sinks into life as a bandit. One day, seeing the grief of the small child left by a man he had cut down for his money, Ichikurō understands the depth of his sin; he leaves the woman, takes vows, and after a pilgrimage through the provinces takes the name Ryōkai. On his travels he sees how many travellers lose their lives on the chain crossing along the cliffs of the Yamakuni River, and he vows to spend everything he has on cutting a tunnel through that rock wall for people to pass, and sets about that enormous work alone. Meanwhile Jitsunosuke, the son of the man Ryōkai had killed, grows up to avenge his father, travels the provinces, and at last learns where Ryōkai is. Coming face to face with his enemy, Jitsunosuke is asked to wait until the tunnel is finished; struck by Ryōkai's tenacity, he decides to help with the work and bide his time. Year upon year goes by, and on the night when, after one and twenty years, the tunnel breaks through to the far side of the Yamakuni River, Ryōkai asks to be killed by Jitsunosuke's own hand; but Jitsunosuke, overcome with wonder and emotion at what has been achieved, has lost the will to take his revenge, and the two take each other's hands and weep for joy.

Characters

  • Ichikurō (Ryōkai)　He kills his master and sinks into banditry, then repents, takes vows, and goes on digging a tunnel through a cliff in expiation

The opening (original text)

Ichikurō failed to parry the sword his master swung at him, and took a cut, slight though it was, from his left cheek down to his jaw. Conscious of his own crime — the fatal crime of a monstrous love with his master's favourite concubine, provoked though it had been from her side — Ichikurō took the sword his master raised as the punishment that must come, and, even while he tried to keep clear of its point, had not the least thought of resisting it.

The ending (original text)

"Jitsunosuke. Look here. The great vow of one and twenty years is fulfilled tonight, and unlooked for." So saying, Ryōkai took Jitsunosuke's hand and showed him the flow of the Yamakuni River through the small opening. The blackish earth visible directly below the opening was unmistakably the highway that ran along the bank. There, enemy and enemy took each other's hands and choked with tears of great joy. But after a while Ryōkai drew back and said, "Now, Jitsunosuke, this is the day of our promise. Cut me down. If I die in the midst of such bliss as this, my birth in the Pure Land is certain and beyond doubt. Come, cut me down" — and his hoarse voice rang in the night air of the cave. But Jitsunosuke only sat before Ryōkai with his hands folded, choking with tears.

Famous passages

Now, Jitsunosuke, this is the promised day. Strike me down. If I die in the very midst of such rapture, I shall surely be reborn in the Pure Land—of that there is no doubt.

The words of Ryōkai, having dug through the cliff over more than twenty years, asking to be struck down by Jitsunosuke, who came to avenge his father. The pure resolve of one who has finished atoning for his sins moves the heart.

There, forgetting everything, the two men wept together, choked with emotion.

The ending. Before the completed tunnel, the avenger and the man he came to kill go beyond gratitude and vengeance, and weep together, hand in hand. The scene in which the “beyond” of the title comes true.
