About this work

Kitamura Tōkoku's review, published in Jogaku Zasshi, of Uchida Fuchian's translation of Crime and Punishment. He credits Fuchian with introducing Dostoevsky to Japan, begins from the character of Russia as a dark society, and reads hypochondria as a great element of the regret in human life. Closing with the sentence that he rejoices beyond measure at Fuchian's courage in bringing this book into our literary world, it stands at the starting point of the reception of Dostoevsky in Japan.

Summary

Crime and Punishment, that grave, wretched and gloomy psychological novel, lays bare without reserve one side of the dark society of that strange giant, Russia — so Tōkoku begins. We have long heard the names of Tolstoy and Turgenev, but it must be said that the credit for introducing the name and the works of Dostoevsky to our literary world belongs chiefly to Fuchian. Tōkoku then draws the character of the country called Russia. Though her territory is vast and her armies full, the happiness of her people is not abundant; an iron fence is set between the nobles and the small folk, and the voices of commoners crying for equality arise. They delight in strong tea without milk and drink pure spirits without water; they are gloomy and resolute. It is the sinister character of the Russian not to move lightly as the Frenchman does, but to brood in silence and to keep a poisoned blade in his breast. ... Crime and Punishment is surely a direct copy of just this sinister character and this condition of bitter misery, set upon a single hypochondriac. What sort of disease is hypochondria? The unlearned do not suffer from it; on the contrary, learning brings it on, and it is those with knowledge who suffer it most. Is not the grudge of human life a great element of this disease? Tōkoku follows the work. The opening, in which a student in rags hides from his acquaintances; the old woman moneylender; the confession, half penitence and half boast, of the dismissed official in the tavern; his sick wife and his daughter. In the sixth installment the question of the great crime of murder is raised out of the talk in an eating house, and the moneylender is at last killed. From the eighth installment onward comes the spiritual "punishment" — the digging out of the demon within the heart, growing finer and more marvelous as it goes.

The opening (original text)

Crime and Punishment, that grave, wretched and gloomy psychological novel, may be said to lay bare without reserve one side of the dark society of that strange giant, Russia. We have long heard the names of Tolstoy and Turgenev and the rest; but as for the name and the works of Dostoevsky, it must be said that the credit for introducing them to our literary world belongs chiefly to Fuchian.

Crime and Punishment (Uchida Fuchian translation) by Kitamura Tōkoku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I do not care to say much. Once, when I read the passage in Hugo's Les Misérables in which the silver is stolen, I was astonished at its fineness; but what this book contains is perhaps not of that order. I rejoice above all in the courage with which Fuchian has introduced this book to our literary world. I wait for the second volume to come out quickly.

Crime and Punishment (Uchida Fuchian translation) by Kitamura Tōkoku (Aozora Bunko)
