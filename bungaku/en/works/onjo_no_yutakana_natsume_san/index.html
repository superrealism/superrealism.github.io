About this work

Natsume Sōseki was rumoured to be difficult and to turn callers away at the door. But for Uchida Roan he was a man of generous warmth, at ease from their first meeting as though they had known each other a hundred years, who never made a cheap pun and carried a well-bred humour. From that first meeting, at which Sōseki was papering the sliding screens, to his tastes in fountain pens and ink and his interest in things Chinese, these are recollections of the Sōseki he saw over ten years and more of friendship.

Summary

A recollection in which Uchida Roan, on the strength of an acquaintance of more than ten years, draws the warm-hearted face of Natsume Sōseki. The world rumors him a difficult man who turns callers away at the gate; but to Roan he was not that. Soon after I Am a Cat had come out, passing before the house at Sendagi Roan looked in, and Sōseki, who was papering the sliding doors, welcomed him with "Talk to me while I finish papering," and though they had never met, they opened to each other like friends of a hundred years and sat long together. He never once went home having merely stated his business, but always stayed talking two or three hours; in the course of talk Sōseki never made a single pun, but mixed in two or three pieces of refined humor in an hour; and he agreed readily even to troublesome requests — so that Roan writes he was never once made to feel uncomfortable. He records too that though people fancy him a man of Western tastes, he was really a man of Chinese tastes; that he disliked blue-black ink and loved sepia; and anecdotes about how seldom he went out after Shuzenji, and about his operation for piles. He has not read the recent long novels but likes the early short pieces such as "The Java Sparrow"; and he closes by calling him a man of keen senses, a man who never made a pun, a man of a high taste in conversation, and a man of refined wit.

Characters

  • Natsume Sōseki　Properly Natsume Kinnosuke. Rumored by the world to be difficult, but to the author a warm-hearted and open man
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who was acquainted with Sōseki for more than ten years from soon after I Am a Cat appeared
  • Ōtani Jōseki　The man whom the author names as perhaps Sōseki's best successor

The opening (original text)

I have had no chance of meeting Mr. Natsume lately. I hardly read his works either. By what people say, Mr. Natsume's writing grows better year by year; but somehow I do not think so — though since I do not read him at all nowadays I have of course no right to criticize. By what one sees in the newspapers and the like, Mr. Natsume is made out to be a man who turns away at the door those he does not like, and who, even if he receives one, says something like "Our business is done, so you may go home now"; but I do not think so at all. It was soon after I Am a Cat had come out that I met Mr. Natsume.

Onjō no yutakana natsume san by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In short, Mr. Natsume was a man of keen senses, a man who never made a pun, a man of a high taste in conversation, and a man of refined wit. In our literary world he stood alone in this. Mr. Natsume has a great many pupils, but they seem to me all of a bent that cannot carry on after him. If I am to name one here, it is only Mr. Ōtani Jōseki, though he is at present far from the central literary world. May he not be Mr. Natsume's best successor? And if I may add one thing more, I think Mr. Natsume read almost nothing, one may say, of the new work of the West.
