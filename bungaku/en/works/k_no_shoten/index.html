About this work

A short story published in the coterie magazine Aozora in 1926, subtitled as the drowning of K. It takes the form of a letter answering the family of K, whom the narrator knew only for a month at a seaside resort, and who has drowned — was it accident or suicide? K had become possessed by the fancy that on a full-moon night one may find one's double in the shadow the moonlight casts, and that by following the shadow the soul rises to the moon. The narrator explains K's death as just such an ascension. Moon, shadow and double, set down with clear lyricism and logic; with "Lemon," one of Kajii's best.

Summary

A letter reaches the narrator with the news that K, with whom he was on close terms for only a month or so by chance at the N beach where they were both convalescing, has drowned. To the family, who are troubled by the question whether it was an accident or a suicide, he takes up his pen to reply. On a certain night of full moon, unable to sleep for his illness, the narrator had gone out on the beach and found K under the moonlight, staring fixedly at his own shadow on the sand and repeating a strange series of movements. K had found in the shadow the moonlight cast "another self" — a doppelgänger — and had been possessed by the fancy that, by staring at that shadow, the soul gradually leaves the body and climbs along the shadow into the world of the moon. Having left N, the narrator concludes that K too, on another moonlit night by the sea, was carried off by the waves while following his shadow as usual, and lost his life. It was neither a mere accident nor a suicide: it was the ascension of K's soul to the moon. A distinguished piece in letter form, unlike anything else in Kajii's work, in which a fancy about the moon and shadows and doubles is set down with clear lyricism and quiet logic.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, who came to know K by chance at the convalescent place, and who unravels the riddle of his death in a letter to the family
  • K　A young man who found a double in his own shadow under the moonlight and was possessed by the fancy of climbing that shadow to the moon. He drowns on a night of full moon

The opening (original text)

From your letter it seems that you are troubled over K's drowning in every way: whether it was an accident, or a suicide, and if a suicide what the cause of it may have been, and whether he may not have died in despair at an incurable illness. And I suppose that is why you have written to me, a man you have never met, who happened to be acquainted with K for only a month or so at that convalescent place, the N beach. It was from your letter that I first learned of K's drowning there. I was greatly astonished. And at the same time I thought: "So K has gone to the world of the moon at last."

K no shōten by Kajii Motojirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

K called it a fall. If it was a fall this time as well, then, since K could swim, he ought not to have drowned. K's body, as it went down, was carried out to sea. Sensation had not yet returned. The next wave dragged him up onto the beach. Sensation had still not come back. Again he was pulled out to sea, and again dashed against the shore. And all the while the soul was ascending toward the moon. In the end the body finished without feeling. Low tide is recorded as at eleven fifty-six. Leaving the husk to be tossed about by the violence of the waves at that hour, K's soul flew away, toward the moon and toward the moon.

K no shōten by Kajii Motojirō (Aozora Bunko)
