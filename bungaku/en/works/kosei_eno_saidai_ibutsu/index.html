About this work

A lecture Uchimura Kanzō gave at a Christian summer school held at Hakone in 1894. What can we leave to this world? After naming money, enterprise and thought, he offers as the greatest bequest — the one anyone can leave — a brave and noble life. A classic of the lecture form, carrying the heat of his youth in its very delivery, and read again and again since the Meiji era.

Summary

"I have one thought. It is this: what shall we leave behind us in this world when we die?" — so Uchimura Kanzō, at thirty-three, put the question at a summer school beside Lake Ashi at Hakone. Everyone wishes to leave behind some proof that he has lived. What, then, is that legacy? The first thing named is money. To leave money is by no means a base thing; to use wealth honestly gained for the good of the world is a noble undertaking. The second is enterprise — laying railways, opening up wilderness, the work that moves society. The third is thought, that is, writing and education. Uchimura speaks warmly of each, with examples from every age. But those who can leave money are few, and those who can accomplish great enterprises are few, and those who can leave their thought in writing are few as well. Is a man with neither talent nor money to leave nothing at all? And there Uchimura sets out the "greatest legacy," which anyone can leave. It is "a brave and noble life" — to show, by one's own life, that this world is not after all ruled by the devil, and that righteousness wins in the end. Though we can leave neither money nor enterprise nor thought, he closes, I wish to leave to those who come after just so much that they may say, "That man, while he lived in this world, lived an earnest life." The hall broke into applause.

The opening (original text)

Foreword This small volume is the printed text, published with the consent of the members of the committee, of the address I gave at the Sixth Summer School of Christians, opened at the post town of Hakone in Sōshū in July of the twenty-seventh year of Meiji. Much of it concerns Christianity and students; but it is not that it does not also touch to some degree upon the general problems of human life.

Kōsei heno saidai ibutsu by Uchimura Kanzō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Though we have nothing to leave to those who come after, though we have nothing by which those who come after may remember us and say, "That is it," I wish to leave to those who come after just so much that they may say, "That man, while he lived in this world, lived an earnest life." (Applause)

Famous passages

As I see it, there is a legacy that human beings can leave to later ages—one that anyone at all can leave—which brings only benefit and no harm. And what is it? I believe it is a brave and noble life.

The conclusion of a lecture given at a summer school in Hakone. Even if one cannot leave money, enterprises, or ideas, it argues, the greatest thing anyone can leave to later ages is a brave and noble way of living.
