About this work

An essay by the poet Wakayama Bokusui. In 1927, reaching forty-two, the year the Japanese count unlucky, he looked back over his life so far and spoke frankly of how inseparable poetry and drink had been for him. Regretting how recklessly he has drunk, he hides nothing of his inexhaustible attachment to it, and vows to face both poems and drink more seriously from now on. The state of mind of the man who loved travel and drink, compressed into a short piece.

Summary

Wakayama Bokusui reflects that of all he has done in his life, the only things that have drawn any notice at all are, first, the composing of tanka, and, second, the reckless drinking of sake. As for his poems, he confesses that he can hardly say he threw himself into their making with real resolve — rather, he made them because he found he could, because something drove him to it, and he has never felt he gave them his full strength; whenever he has seen or heard of someone laboring over their work so intently as to forget food and sleep, he has always felt a twinge of guilt. As for sake, he has gone on drinking so recklessly, all the way up to the ill-omened age of forty-two, that he is himself astonished his body has held out this long — but of late he has come to dislike his own way of drinking. His attachment to sake itself has not faded in the slightest, and yet he cannot help feeling that in drinking it like so much hot water, or as a kind of medicine, he has been fouling the very virtue of the drink. And so Bokusui resolves that, in gratitude for having lived safely to this age, he will, from this second year of Shōwa onward, apply himself more earnestly to poetry, and drink sake, too, as sake ought properly to be drunk. Surely, he tells himself, this old friend of twenty years' standing will, in return, lend its own earnest help to his work.

The opening (original text)

Of all I have done up to now, it seems the only thing that has drawn any notice at all, however slight, is simply that I have gone on composing tanka. And one thing more: that I have gone on drinking sake, recklessly, without rhyme or reason. Though I say I have "composed" poems, it would be truer to say that, before I quite knew it, I found myself composing them — I can hardly claim I ever set out to compose them with real resolve. I cannot say I have given them my full strength. It has simply been a matter of making them because I found I could, because something drove me to it. Whenever I have seen or heard of someone laboring so intently over their work as to forget food and sleep, I have always felt, somehow, a twinge of guilt.

Sake to uta by Wakayama Bokusui (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And so, in gratitude for having lived safely through to the age of forty-two, I find myself wanting, from this second year of Shōwa onward, to apply myself more earnestly to poetry. To compose, and to read — I mean to give myself over to both with all my heart. If I devote myself wholly and press onward, my world still seems to me a thing exceedingly fresh, and deep and remote besides. And along with this, I mean to make it my resolve to drink sake, too, as sake ought properly to be drunk. If I do, then surely this old friend of twenty years' standing will, in earnest, lend its help to this determined work of mine.

Sake to uta by Wakayama Bokusui (Aozora Bunko)
