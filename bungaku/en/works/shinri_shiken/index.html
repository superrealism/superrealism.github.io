About this work

An important early short detective story by Edogawa Ranpo, published in the magazine Shin Seinen in February 1925. One of the cases of his detective Akechi Kogorō, it turns on a psychological test from criminal psychology — an experiment in detecting lies — with which Akechi picks apart the carefully prepared story of a murderer.

Summary

Fukiya Seiichirō, a student working his way through university and criminal by nature, lays a careful plan to murder an old woman for the fortune she has hidden. Learning that his classmate Saitō Isamu had once committed a small theft at the old woman's house, Fukiya arranges matters so that suspicion will fall on Saitō, kills the woman and carries off a large sum. Saitō is duly arrested as the suspect, and Fukiya is called in by the police as his friend and subjected to a word-association test in the presence of the examining magistrate, who is also a psychologist. Having anticipated every question and answered without a flicker, Fukiya is sure he has brought off the perfect crime. But the amateur detective Akechi Kogorō, also his friend, sees that in Fukiya's answers there is a point where he responded unnaturally smoothly about a thing he could not have known. His too-perfect composure over the folding screen that stood at the scene was the surest possible proof that he had seen it. With no way out of Akechi's demonstration, Fukiya admits his crime at last and signs his confession.

Characters

  • Fukiya Seiichirō　A gifted student working his way through university, who plots the perfect murder of an old woman. The criminal of the story
  • Saitō Isamu　Fukiya's classmate, who is nearly convicted of a crime he did not commit
  • Akechi Kogorō　The amateur detective, who sees through Fukiya from a contradiction in his answers at the test

The opening (original text)

Why Fukiya Seiichirō conceived the terrible crime that I am about to set down, I do not know in detail. And even if I did know, it would have little to do with this story. Since he was attending a certain university while more or less working his way through, one might suppose that he was pressed by the need for his school fees. He was a scholar of rare gifts and a very hard worker, and it is certain that he resented having his time taken up with dull side work to get his fees, so that he could not read and think as much as he liked. But do human beings commit so great a crime for a reason like that? Perhaps he was a criminal by birth. And perhaps it was not his fees alone but various other desires that he could not hold down. However that may be, it was half a year since the thought first came to him. In that time, after hesitating and hesitating and thinking and thinking, he had at last resolved to go through with it.

The ending (original text)

"Then, Fukiya, will you sign this? A thumbprint will do. Surely you will not say you would rather not. You did promise a moment ago that you would testify about the screen at any time — though you may not have imagined that the testimony would take this form." Fukiya knew well enough that there was no use in refusing to sign now. He signed and set his seal, meaning by it to acknowledge Akechi's astonishing reasoning as well. And then he hung his head like a man who has given up entirely. "As I said before," Akechi explained at the end, "Münsterberg says that the true value of the psychological test is decisive only in testing whether a suspect knows a certain place or person or thing. In this case that was the point of whether Fukiya had seen the screen. Leave that point aside and a hundred psychological tests would probably be useless. We were dealing, after all, with a man like Fukiya, who had foreseen everything and prepared minutely. And there is one more thing I should like to say: that a psychological test does not have to be conducted, as the books have it, with a fixed set of stimulus words and a fixed apparatus, but can be done perfectly well through the most ordinary conversation, as I have just demonstrated for you. The famous judges of the past — a man like Ōoka Echizen no Kami — were all applying, without knowing it themselves, the very methods that recent psychology has discovered."

Famous passages

Why Fukiya Seiichirō came to conceive the dreadful crime I am about to relate, I do not know in detail. And even if I did, it would have little to do with this story.

The opening of the story. Brushing aside the criminal's motive as unknown, the narration narrows its focus to the steps of the crime and the battle over the psychological test, announcing an inverted detective story told from the criminal's side.
