About this work

The oldest tale in Japan, called "the ancestor from which all tales came," retold in readable prose by the bibliographer Wada Mankichi. Kaguya-hime, born from a stalk of bamboo, refuses five noble suitors and the emperor himself and returns to the moon. The original plot can be followed simply here, down to the closing account of how Mount Fuji got its name.

Summary

Long ago an old bamboo cutter called Sanuki no Miyatsukomaro found a beautiful girl only three inches tall inside a shining stalk of bamboo, and carried her home. He and his wife raised her with care, and in some three months she was full grown and so lovely that the house shone into every corner. The old man found gold in the joints of the bamboo and grew rich, and the girl was named Nayotake no Kaguya-hime. Men came thronging when word of her spread, and to the five noble suitors who stayed to the last she set impossible tasks: the Buddha's stone begging bowl, the jewelled branch of Hōrai, the robe of fire-rat fur, the jewel in the dragon's neck, the easy-birth shell of the swallow. Making counterfeits, or nearly losing their lives, all five failed. In time even the emperor wanted her, and, seeing her while out hunting, lost his heart; but she would not have him, saying that she was not of this country. After three years of letters, as the fifteenth night of the eighth month drew near she took to weeping her days away, and confessed to the old man that she was of the city of the moon and that they were coming for her. The emperor sent two thousand warriors to guard the house, but on that night, before the people of the moon who came down from the sky, no one had any strength to fight. Kaguya-hime gave the old man a letter to remember her by, and for the emperor a letter together with the elixir of immortality; then she put on the robe of heaven's feathers, took her place in the carriage and rose into the sky. The emperor had the elixir burned on the mountain in Suruga that stands nearest to heaven. And so this mountain came to be called the Mountain of Immortality, and its smoke, they say, rises into the clouds still.

Characters

  • Kaguya-hime　A princess of the city of the moon, born from a shining stalk of bamboo
  • The old bamboo cutter　Sanuki no Miyatsukomaro, the old man who raised the princess
  • The five noble suitors　The suitors to whom she sets her impossible tasks
  • The emperor　He wants the princess for himself, and at the last has the elixir of immortality burned

The opening (original text)

Long ago — in what age I cannot say — there was a man called the old bamboo cutter. His true name was Sanuki no Miyatsukomaro, but since he went nearly every day into the bamboo groves of the hills and fields, cutting bamboo and making all manner of things from it and selling them, he was commonly known as the old bamboo cutter. One day, going into the grove as usual, he saw a single stalk of bamboo that shone strangely.

The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter by Wada Mankichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The imperial messenger, for his part, went up to the palace and reported all that had happened that night, and presented the letter and the elixir. Hearing that the mountain nearest to heaven was in the province of Suruga, the emperor had his officers climb it and burn the elixir of immortality there. From that time the mountain came to be called the Mountain of Immortality, and the smoke of that elixir, they say, rises into the clouds even now.

Famous passages

Long ago—just when it was, I cannot say—there lived a man known as the Old Bamboo Cutter.

The opening of a retelling, in words gentle enough for children, of The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter, said to be Japan's oldest tale. It corresponds to the original's “Now long ago, there was one called the Old Bamboo Cutter.”
