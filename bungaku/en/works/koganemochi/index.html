About this work

Kinbei, having seen a neighbour in a poor tenement swallow his money as he died, shoulders the corpse and carries it all the way to the crematorium — a transcript of a piece known as one of the great items of rakugo. Famous for a thoroughgoing blackness that laughs off human greed at a distance, and for the celebrated recital of the place names all the way from Shitaya to Azabu in a single breath.

Summary

This is the tale of how a rice-cake shop called "Kogane-mochi" — the Golden Rice Cake — came to be built by Kanasugi Bridge in Shiba, and grew enormously popular. In a poor tenement by Shōgendono Bridge lived, side by side, Kinbei of the Kanayamajiya and a mendicant monk named Sainen. Hearing that Sainen had fallen ill, Kinbei goes to look in on him, and finds the sixty-four-year-old man saying it must surely be his deathbed illness, and asking Kinbei to go out and buy him some thirty daifuku rice cakes. What is more, Sainen, saying, "Ever since I was a child, it's been my nature that I can't eat a thing with anyone watching," tries to send Kinbei away. Suspicious, Kinbei peers through a hole in the wall, and sees Sainen digging the bean paste out of the rice cakes, stuffing them, one after another, with the ni-bu gold coins he has hoarded away, and swallowing them down. Before long Sainen is seized with agony and breathes his last. Kinbei stirs up the other tenants, and, without so much as calling for a priest, has the body carried off to the crematorium. After a journey in which the names of every district along the way — from Shitaya through Ueno, Hongō, and on to Azabu — are rattled off in a single breathless string, they arrive at the crematorium at Kirigaya. Kinbei begs the man in charge to leave the body only half-burnt, and returns the next morning, while it is still dark, to collect the bones. Chasing off the crematorium attendant, he pokes through the ashes with his chopsticks, finds a lump around where the belly had been, and cracks it open — and out comes gold, its color unchanged, bright as kerria blossom: koban coins. Stuffing them into both his sleeves, Kinbei goes running off, and with that money opens a rice-cake shop by Kanasugi Bridge in Shiba — the very shop, this tale tells us, that grew so tremendously popular.

Characters

  • Kinbei　A man of the Kanayamajiya. Carries off the corpse and gets hold of the money.
  • Genpachi (Sainen)　A mendicant monk who wraps his money in rice cakes and swallows it down.
  • The crematorium attendant　A low-ranking worker at the crematorium.

The opening (original text)

Long, long ago, a rice-cake shop called "Kogane-mochi" — the Golden Rice Cake — was built by Kanasugi Bridge in Shiba, and for a time it seems to have been enormously popular. As for how it came to be given that name — well, in a back tenement by Shōgendono Bridge in Shiba, where none but the wretchedly poor lived, there was a man named Kinbei, of the Kanayamajiya, and living next door to him was a mendicant monk who went by the name of Genpachi, who had, for some reason or other, lately taken to his bed with illness — and so Kinbei, thinking he ought to go look in on him, comes along—

The ending (original text)

Poking through the ashes bit by bit with his chopsticks, he found a lump around where the belly had been, and with his wood-and-bamboo chopsticks he gave it a good sharp crack — and out came old gold, its color unchanged, bright as kerria blossom. Flustered, he stuffed it into both his sleeves as he went. "You crematorium bastard," Kinbei snarled, "come in here and I won't let you off easy — come barging in without asking and I'll burn you up and shove you in myself!" — and, cursing him roundly, he slipped past the attendant and went running off. And with that money, Kinbei set up, by Kanasugi Bridge in Shiba, a rice-cake shop called Kogane-mochi — the Golden Rice Cake — which grew tremendously popular. And that is the whole of this tale.
