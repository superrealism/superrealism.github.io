About this work

An unusual long work that renders the few days in which a young runaway tries to become a miner at a copper mine purely as the movement of his mind. Written from a true story a young man brought him, it deliberately refuses both plot and consistency of character, and follows only the "heart" as it shifts from moment to moment. Having fled Tokyo after a tangle in love, the narrator lets a tout lead him to the mine and goes down into the dark. An experiment that breaks the conventions of the modern novel on purpose, and a provocation in pursuit of what psychological description can do.

Summary

A young man of nineteen, sick of everything after a tangled love affair, runs away from Tokyo, and walking north and north again with no destination is spoken to by a tout named Chōzō, who asks whether he would like to become a miner at a copper mine. Half meaning to throw his life away, he agrees carelessly, and with the others gathered in the same way he follows the mountain roads to the mine. Daunted by the squalor of the lodging house and the coarse lives of the miners, he goes down all the same into the dark shafts. Climbing ladders and pushing on through a maze of holes, his mind swings violently this way and that through what the miner guiding him and Yasu-san, an educated miner he meets down there, say to him. In the end he fails the physical examination and cannot become a miner at all; he gets work as a clerk instead, works some five months and goes back to Tokyo. The story closes curtly: this is the whole of his experience of miners, and all of it is fact.

Characters

  • I　A young man of nineteen who runs away from home after a tangled love affair and goes down into a copper mine to become a miner
  • Chōzō　A tout who gathers miners, and who brings the narrator to the mine
  • Yasu-san　An educated miner met underground, who urges the narrator to give up the work

The opening (original text)

I have been going through a pine wood for some time now, and a pine wood is a good deal longer than it looks in pictures. However far one goes there is nothing but pine, and one gets nowhere at all.

Kōfu by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I did that clerk's work for five months without trouble. And then I went back to Tokyo. — That is the whole of my experience of miners. And all of it is fact. You can tell as much from the fact that it has not turned into a novel.

Kōfu by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

People speak of something bringing them to tears, but if the tears come, you can rest easy. As long as tears come, you are sure to be able to laugh as well.

A passage in which a young man who ran away from home and nearly became a miner looks back on his cornered state of mind. A paradox from one who has known the depths of hardship: while you can still cry, you still have some room left.
