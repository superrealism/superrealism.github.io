About this work

Stir fermented soybeans more than three hundred times — a piece in which Kitaōji Rosanjin explains, from the procedure of stirring onward, the unexpectedly good way of eating natto over rice with tea. In the persistence of dropping just a few drops of soy sauce at a time and stirring again, this gourmet's attitude to food shows exactly. Short, and it makes one want to try it.

Summary

Natto over rice with tea is a surprisingly delicious thing, and yet almost nobody knows about it — even among gourmets, surprisingly few are aware of it, the piece begins. Rosanjin says it strikes him as strange that, though it is no invention of his own, the world at large should not know of it. What he explains first is the "preparation of the natto," that is, the stirring of it. Unless this stirring is done properly, the flavour of the natto never comes out. Add nothing to the natto turned out into a bowl, and simply stir it well with two chopsticks, just as it is. Threads will increase, something like the fine threads that come from a lotus root, and it will grow stiff and hard to stir. The more of these threads you draw out, the better the natto tastes, and so one should not be lazy or spare any effort, but keep stirring it as hard as one can. Once it has been stirred stiff, drop in a few drops of soy sauce and stir again. Drop in a few more drops and stir. Repeat this, adding just a very little soy sauce at a time and stirring, until the threads have vanished and the natto has turned gooey, and then add mustard. Put the natto made this way onto hot rice, and pour hot tea over the top — that is natto over rice with tea. Rather than measurements or heat control, the whole of the explanation concentrates on this one point — spare no effort — and in this short piece Rosanjin's own attitude toward food shows through exactly as it is.

The opening (original text)

Natto over rice with tea is a surprisingly delicious thing. And yet almost nobody knows of it. Even among gourmets, surprisingly few are aware of it. Not that it is any invention of my own — but it strikes me as strange that the world at large should not know of it.

Nattō no chazuke by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Once it has been stirred stiff, drop in a few drops of soy sauce and stir again. Drop in a few more drops of soy sauce and stir. In short, repeat this, adding just a very little soy sauce at a time and stirring, and once the threads have vanished and the natto has turned gooey, add mustard.

Nattō no chazuke by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)
