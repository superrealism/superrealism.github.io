About this work

The long novel that was Tokuda Shūsei's last. Serialized in the Miyako Shimbun in 1941, it broke off at the eightieth instalment when the author put down his pen after interference from the wartime Information Bureau, which called it too much concerned with manners. It follows in an even hand, with sentiment refused, the life of Ginko, a woman born into a poor house in Nihonbashi who drifts from service as a nursemaid to work as a bar girl and then a geisha. The title carries the intent of seeing in one woman's history a miniature of ordinary life from Meiji into Shōwa. Called the high point of naturalism, and held by many, unfinished as it is, to be Shūsei's finest work.

Summary

Ginza in wartime. Ginko, who runs a geisha house, dines with Kinpei, the man who has stayed by her side for so many years now, as the two of them look out on a world grown ever more constrained by wartime controls. From here the story reaches back into Ginko's past. Born into a back-alley tenement in Nihonbashi, Ginko saw her family scatter when her father's business failed, and was sent out, while still a small child, into service as a nursemaid. Drifting from one place of service to another, she is at last sold as a bar hostess, and wanders from place to place across the country. Toyed with by man after man while still barely more than a girl, undergoing abortion after abortion, she never once pities herself. Once she becomes a geisha, she takes patrons and parts from them in turn, tossed about by the schemings of the mistress of her geisha house, yet making her way through the world all the same, on nothing but her native simplicity and good cheer. Her involvement with the businessman Wakabayashi; her tangled, rootless bond with Kinpei, a man who drifts like duckweed. Through the figure of Ginko — who neither laments her circumstances nor regrets her past, but simply lives out, day by day, whatever lies before her — the life history of a woman at society's bottom, spanning the Meiji, Taishō, and Shōwa eras, is built up, layer upon layer, through minute and careful observation.

Characters

  • Ginko　A woman born into a poor family in Nihonbashi, who drifts from service as a nursemaid to bar hostess to geisha
  • Kinpei　A man who carries on a long, tangled bond with Ginko, and harbors resentment toward the family that adopted him
  • Wakabayashi　A businessman who becomes Ginko's patron
  • The mistress　The proprietress of Ginko's geisha house, forever scheming to look after her own interests

The opening (original text)

Living in the Shade One At dinnertime, the Shiseido in Ginza was, as ever, packed full, upstairs and down. Ginko and Kinpei, after waiting a while on the sofa in a corner of the second floor for a table to free up, at last managed to get seats over by the window, deep toward the back, and, at Ginko's preference, ordered the somewhat better of the set meals this dining hall offered.

The ending (original text)

When Ginko asked about it, it turned out to be nothing more than a whim of the mistress's — she had said that all the little things Ginko had been given up to now, the diamond ring, the old imported coral and jade obi clasps, the hair ornaments, the combs, the hairpins, the wristwatch, should all be bundled together and given back. Ginko, loath to have taken away from her the things she had finally been given, was displeased, though she did not let it show, and hurried home to make up a bundle almost too much for one hand to carry, and brought it along. The mistress took it from her, and, all but claiming the idea had been her own doing from the start, presented it to Wakabayashi. Wakabayashi turned his face away with a "Hm," but he was not so hard-pressed as to need to take back even the things he had given to a woman, and though he took it home with him for the time being, four or five days later he said, "This surely wasn't your idea," and quietly gave it all back to Ginko.
