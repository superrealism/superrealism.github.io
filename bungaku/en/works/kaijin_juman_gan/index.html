About this work

"Fire at Maruzen this morning" — an essay in which the author, hurrying to the news that the Maruzen bookshop had burned, laments the impermanence of the world before a hundred thousand volumes of rare books reduced to ashes. He sets the wretched sight of precious Western books smeared with mud and trampled under firemen's straw sandals against the din of people who come to offer sympathy and stay to eat oranges and drink, and writes the grief that only a lover of books could feel.

Summary

On the morning of a certain December tenth, as the author sat at his desk working on a manuscript, a telegram arrived from Maruzen: "STORE BURNED THIS MORNING." Rushing to the scene, still half in disbelief, he finds before his very eyes that the rare foreign books, once proudly bound in gold, silver, and five colors, have turned to ash and mud. The author, who as a consultant to Maruzen had long been deeply involved in the importing of foreign books, cannot help but feel the impermanence of this world at the sight of great art books worth a fortune trampled underfoot, their precious plates ground into ruin beneath the muddy straw sandals of unfeeling firemen. He grieves for the burned books, likening them to the historic burning of the Library of Alexandria. Meanwhile, in the office inside the store, well-wishers press in one after another, munching on mandarin oranges, gnawing rice crackers, and knocking back cups of sake in a raucous, noisy scene. Newspaper reporters ask only about the amount of the damage, the insurance, and when the shop will reopen — and one of them even asks, quite beside the point, "Do you have a catalogue of the burned books?" A sharply observant essay that sets the author's deep grief for the books against the crass, almost comic clamor of the human beings carrying on around him, oblivious to it all.

The opening (original text)

December tenth. It was unusually mild, fine walking weather, the kind of day that made one want to wander idly out to the suburbs — but, like it or not, the deadline I had promised for my manuscript was closing in, and so, gulping down breakfast, I sat myself at my desk when — a telegram! It was from Maruzen. Wondering what business they could have with me so early in the morning, I broke the seal and read: "STORE BURNED THIS MORNING." Now, what was this? I could make no sense of it. However many times I read it over, it could only be read as "the store burned this morning." It would hardly have been strange for Maruzen's shop, no impregnable fortress, to burn down someday — but since I had never imagined it burning down this very morning, these seven simple kana characters made no sense to me at all. What was more, I had been awake since half past four that morning. Had there been a fire, I should surely have heard the fire bell ring, at least — but I had not heard even so much as the alarm sounded for an outbreak of fire. How, then, could it have burned? However I turned it over, I could not believe it had burned. And yet, somehow, the feeling came over me that it had always seemed a house that might well burn down, and I hurried out at once, just as I was, in my ordinary everyday clothes.

Kaijin jūman gan by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Well-wishers kept arriving, one after another. The clerks at the reception desk bowed their heads, again and again, in turn. The reply postcards, just freshly printed, had their addresses written out by five clerks working as hard as they could. All the while, the advertising men came. The drapers came. The furniture dealers came. The gas company came. The telephone exchange came. The insurance men came. Crates of beer were piled up. Straw-wrapped casks of sake came rolling in. Sushi, bread, sweets, and rice crackers were carried in without a pause, and no sooner did a box open, one after another, than it was emptied in an instant. Not a single soul stood still. No sooner had one sat down than he rose again. Everyone chattered and clamored, stirring the whole room's air into motion, and amid it all — merchandise half charred black or drenched by the fire hose, a mountain of cardboard boxes bent and torn, half-broken chairs and stools, tin kettles, cement casks, bricks, scraps of lumber, empty beer bottles, mandarin-orange peels, wastepaper, bits of rope, muddy straw sandals, the whole room strewn about as though a rubbish heap had been overturned — the clerks, their faces sooty, in strange and disordered dress, went back and forth, back and forth, until at last the evening came, fading slowly away.

Kaijin jūman gan by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
