About this work

A short story that opens by noting that small pieces by Maupassant and Prévost take the form of reprinting a letter found in the drawer of an inn, and that the narrator has had the same experience. The letter he finds at an inn on a journey exposes the secret of his cousin Jūkichi, who is engaged to be married. The narrator meets Jūkichi with the letter still hidden, and hands it to him as a parting gift at the instant the train begins to move at the station platform.

Summary

Both Maupassant's "The Twenty-five Days" and Prévost's "Absence" take the form of reprinting just as they are a manuscript or a letter found in the drawer of an inn. The two are so much alike in their device that one might even suspect the newer of having followed the older; but that is nothing to me — so "I" begin. Only, I too have quite lately had an experience of the same kind. Since then it has come over me that there are a great many similar things in the world, and I have come rather to marvel at the coincidence, and so have felt like setting the works of the two men side by side here. ... The single letter found at an inn on a journey concerned my cousin Shigekichi. A match has been all but settled for Shigekichi. Saying nothing of the letter, "I" meet him, hear the whole story, and then insist that the match must be broken off. When Shigekichi clings to me in tears, I propose that if, as proof of his penitence and self-restraint until he takes the woman home, he will send ten yen a month out of his salary toward the cost of the marriage, I will settle it privately and forgive him. When he begs me to make it five instead of ten I do not give way, and it is at last settled on my terms. And then the station. Putting my head out of the window and looking with satisfaction at Shigekichi's habutae collar and stiff sash and white socks, I deliberately waited for the very moment when the wheels had begun to turn, and taking this morning's letter out of my pocket said, "Here — a present for you," and reached out my hand as far as ever I could.

Characters

  • I　The man who found the letter in the drawer of an inn
  • Shigekichi　My cousin, whose match is all but settled
  • My wife　Who is impressed, being told nothing about the letter

The opening (original text)

I In Maupassant's short piece entitled "The Twenty-five Days," there is an opening in which a man, having settled into an inn at a certain spa and being about to put his clothes and his shirts into the wardrobe, opens the drawer and finds a rolled paper in it, and unrolling it carelessly reads the title "My Twenty-five Days"; and after that the so-called twenty-five days of that anonymous writer are set down as if reprinted in their original form without a word altered.

Tegami by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

When the hour of departure came at last, I deliberately waited for the very moment when I thought the wheels of the carriage had begun to turn, and taking out of my pocket the letter I had read that morning I said, "Here, a present for you," and reached my hand out toward Shigekichi as far as it would go. By the time Shigekichi took it the train was already moving. I drew my head back into the carriage and never once looked back at the platform until we were out of the station.

Tegami by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)
