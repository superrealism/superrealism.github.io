About this work

A novella Yumeno Kyūsaku published in the detective magazine Shin Seinen in 1929. It takes the form of a long confessional letter written from her sickbed by the violinist Inokuchi Toshiko to Hishida Shintarō, also a violinist. The beautiful youth in a raised-cloth picture at which her mother gazed while carrying her — Toshiko was born bearing that face, and Hishida, for some reason, is her exact double. The strange karma that draws the two together, the curse of an illness that has clung to the family for generations, and the secret of blood that is at last disclosed. Heredity and fate, and a pure and doomed love, set down fantastically by a master of the heterodox detective story.

Summary

The woman violinist Inokuchi Toshiko, who collapsed coughing blood at a concert in Marunouchi and then vanished from sight, writes, from her sickbed, a long letter of confession to the young violinist Hishida Shintarō, who has likewise been hospitalized after spitting blood. From the moment the two first met, they had felt drawn to each other, sensing between them some strange bond of fate. What Toshiko now sets out to tell is the strange tale bound up with a single oshie — a padded cloth picture. Her mother, while carrying her, had spent her days gazing fixedly at an oshie depicting a beautiful young man, and Toshiko, born after, came into the world bearing that very young man's likeness — and Hishida, too, it turns out, is the living image of that same picture. Why should the two of them so closely resemble one another? Why do they both play the violin, and both fall ill with the same disease? As Toshiko traces back her mother's mysterious dying words, she arrives at a terrible truth: that she and Hishida may be siblings by blood, bound together by a love — pure and sorrowful beyond anything in this world — between her mother and Hishida's father. Bearing the curse of an incurable illness that has clung to her family for generations, Toshiko, having withdrawn from a love that could never be permitted, sets down her pen at last, praying only that their love may remain, forever, noble and pure. A masterpiece of unconventional detective fiction in which Kyūsaku, in dense and richly wrought prose, weaves together heredity and fate with the fantastical vision of a tragic love bound up in a single picture.

Characters

  • Inokuchi Toshiko　A violinist confined to her sickbed. In a letter to Hishida, she confesses the secret of her birth, bound up with a single oshie picture
  • Hishida Shintarō　A young violinist. The living image of Toshiko, he too falls ill with the same disease. The two may be siblings by blood
  • Toshiko's mother　Spent her pregnancy gazing at an oshie of a beautiful young man, and died leaving behind mysterious last words

The opening (original text)

Watching for the moments when the nurse is asleep, I set my clumsy woman's hand racing across the page, and so I am sure it will be difficult to read, and hard to follow in places — but I beg you, again and again, to forgive me, writing as I am in such haste. 　How you must have wondered, after all that happened, at my rudeness in vanishing from your sight without so much as a single word of explanation. My heart grows so full, thinking of how I might ever make amends, that it leaves me weaker and weaker, filled with a sad and wretched feeling.

Oshie no kiseki by Yumeno Kyūsaku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

　Your father's love, and my mother's — theirs was a love of the purest kind this world has ever known. 　And it remained, forever, a noble thing. 　And so I pray, with all my heart, that the love between my elder brother and myself may likewise remain, forever, noble, and pure, and sorrowful, to the very end... 　My thoughts grow too disordered now, and my pen will move no further. How I hate to part from you here. In haste, and with all respect, 　　　The twenty-ninth of March, the thirty-fifth year of Meiji From Inokuchi Toshiko 　　　　　To Hishida Shintarō 　　　　　　　　　　　To be delivered into his own hands

Oshie no kiseki by Yumeno Kyūsaku (Aozora Bunko)
