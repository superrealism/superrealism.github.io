About this work

A short piece of literary comment the critic Shimamura Hōgetsu, the theoretical support of naturalism, contributed to Bunshō Sekai in March 1907. He works out what the sketch prose that began with Masaoka Shiki and flourished in the literary world of the day actually is, comparing it against Western books. He says flatly that sketch prose is unfinished and formless, like the underdrawing of a picture, and precisely for that reason recommends it as the best exercise for young people who mean to write novels or plays, closing that to be a work of art a thing must have a centre. Short, but the frame of Hōgetsu's criticism is plainly visible.

Summary

Hōgetsu begins by citing the Englishman White's Natural History of Selborne. Its manner, setting down the events of each day in the form of letters, is descriptive from life, and the reader has the feeling of being in the place and touching the things at first hand. But this book is not the same as our sketching prose. Our sketching prose is literature, whereas this only borrows for the moment the form of literature to explain natural history; and since the purport is altogether different they cannot be set side by side, says Hōgetsu, dismissing it. The oldest sketching prose in the West must be the prologue to Boccaccio's Decameron — the passage that draws so clearly the horror of the plague raging in Florence, and starving dogs devouring the bodies that no one clears away, is beyond doubt sketching prose. But that too is only an old sketching prose, and has no particular relation to the sketching prose of our own day. What, then, is the sketching prose of today? Hōgetsu's answer is severe. It is a thing unfinished, without form, and cannot be regarded as complete in itself. It is good, so to speak, for practice; good as a limbering of the pen in preparation for making something finished another day; good for training the strength of the pen. If one is satisfied with it alone and does not go further, it may perhaps be of no use at all — though not everything unfinished need be uninteresting. Rembrandt's sketches, Sesshū's ink pictures, Menzel's sketches. A drawing has an interest of its own; and the interest of sketching prose is the same. Just for that reason it is the best thing for young people who mean hereafter to write novels or plays to do first as practice. And at the last Hōgetsu goes a step further. To be a work of art whole in itself, there must always be a single center. It need not appear in the form, but without it the work fails the terms of a work of art. In this sense the sketching prose of today divides of itself into two kinds: one wholly without form and unfinished, the other having a kind of form in the author's inner life — in his feeling. The latter, Hōgetsu allows, has form and is finished.

The opening (original text)

▲ Among the writers of present-day England there is a man named White. He is not yet known in our country, but in the reading world of England, and particularly in the home, he is made much of. Among his works there is a book called The Natural History of Selborne. The whole of it is in the form of letters, as though sent from Selborne. Today I went into the wood and found such and such a rare flower; today I saw such and such a tree; today I saw such and such a bird — it sets down the events of day after day; but its manner is so descriptive from life that the reader has the feeling of being in that place and touching the things at first hand.

Ima no shaseibun by Shimamura Hōgetsu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

▲ At all events, to be a work of art whole in itself there must always be a single center. Without that, if it stops at mere scattered description and has no center, we cannot feel pleasure in it. The center need not necessarily appear in the form, but it must be there. If it is wholly absent, the thing fails the terms of a work of art. ▲ In this sense one may say that the sketching prose of today divides of itself into two kinds. One is wholly without form and unfinished; the other has a kind of form in the inner life, that is, in the feeling. (March, the fortieth year of Meiji)

Ima no shaseibun by Shimamura Hōgetsu (Aozora Bunko)
