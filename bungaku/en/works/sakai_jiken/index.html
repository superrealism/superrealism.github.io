About this work

One of Mori Ōgai's historical narratives, published in Shinshōsetsu in February 1914. It reconstructs from documents, in a concise and cold hand, the Sakai Incident that actually occurred at Sakai in 1868, just after the Meiji Restoration. From the clash between French sailors who came ashore at Sakai and the guards of the Tosa domain, it draws the Tosa men ordered to disembowel themselves in consequence going to their deaths, some calmly and some terribly. With "The Last Testament of Okitsu Yagoemon" and "The Abe Family," written out of the shock of Nogi Maresuke's suicide to ask what a samurai's death means, one of Ōgai's central historical works.

Summary

In Meiji 1 (1868), the town of Sakai, fallen into a state of anarchy after the battles of Toba and Fushimi, had its guard entrusted to the Tosa domain. On February 15, French sailors who had come ashore caused a disturbance in the town, and a clash broke out with the Tosa guards posted there. When the sailors would not heed orders to stop, the Tosa troops opened fire, and a great many French sailors were killed or wounded. The affair swiftly grew into a diplomatic incident, and the new government, faced with the hard-line demands of the French side, ordered the Tosa retainers involved in the shooting to disembowel themselves. Gathered at Myōkokuji, the retainers, led by their captain, Minoura Inokichi, met their deaths without the slightest show of distress, and indeed with something like pride. At the terrible sight of them cutting open their bellies one after another, the French officials present to witness and verify the deaths were seized with horror, and once eleven men had died, they petitioned to spare the lives of the rest, and the disembowelments were halted. Ōgai sets down, evenly and without a trace of sentiment, even the various fates that afterward befell those who escaped death, and quietly hands down to later generations the figures of the samurai who died, exactly as they had been ordered to.

Characters

  • Minoura Inokichi　Captain of the sixth company of the Tosa domain, who ordered the shooting in the clash with the French sailors. Ordered to disembowel himself, and meets death calmly
  • The Tosa retainers　The men who kept guard at Sakai and were ordered to disembowel themselves for their part in the affair

The opening (original text)

In the first month of Meiji 1, the year of Tsuchinoe-tatsu, after Tokugawa Yoshinobu's army was defeated at Fushimi and Toba, unable even to hold Osaka Castle, and fled by sea to Edo, the various officials of Osaka, Hyōgo and Sakai abandoned their posts and went into hiding, and these cities fell for a time into a state of anarchy. Thereupon the domains of Satsuma, Chōshū and Tosa were assigned, by imperial command, to keep order in Osaka, Hyōgo and Sakai respectively.

Sakai jiken by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

As for Nishimura, since his father Seizaemon had died young while his grandfather Katsuhei still lived, the headship of the house was restored to the grandfather. Later an adopted heir came from the related Kakei family. As for the sons of the lower officers and common soldiers, most of them, even while still children, were taken on as soldiers themselves, and served once they had grown.
