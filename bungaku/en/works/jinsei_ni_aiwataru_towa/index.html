About this work

Kitamura Tōkoku's essay on literature, written in answer to the critic Yamaji Aizan, who argued that literature ought to be an enterprise that benefits the world. He criticizes sharply a utilitarianism that would reduce the value of literature to use and enterprise, and asserts that the proper ground of literature lies in a freedom and an ideal of the spirit beyond visible results. A famous essay declaring aloud the autonomy of art in modern Japan.

Summary

In reaction against a literature grown delicate and frail, the critic Yamaji Aizan swung the hammer of what he called "historical criticism," and proclaimed that writing ought to be a matter of "undertaking" — of accomplishment. According to Aizan, writing is noble only insofar as it accomplishes something, benefits the world, and engages with human life. Beautiful prose that has no bearing on human life, he held, is worthless, no better than a sword swung at empty air. Against this, Tōkoku objects that such a view would shut out of literature the workings of the invisible ideal and the invisible spirit. If human endeavor is to be measured only by visible results and practical utility, then only "duty," "undertaking," and "altruism" will be worshipped, and Sugawara no Michizane, Matsuo Bashō, Takizawa Bakin, Saigyō — all alike will be despised as useless men. In so narrow a chamber of utility, no truly spiritual life could ever dwell. Tōkoku rejects the notion that a man of letters should shrink himself down to immediate utility, and calls out instead: "Lift up your head, look, and seek." Carry a lofty ideal in your breast; look upon a spiritual realm broad, joyous, and grand; cast your Longing out into the vault of heaven, and from there, seize hold of your calling as a human being. Defending literature not as a means to some practical end or undertaking, but as a spiritual endeavor with value in itself, this essay is a central document of Tōkoku's debate with Aizan over "what it means to engage with life," and stands as a landmark essay proclaiming the autonomy of art in modern Japanese literature.

The opening (original text)

A literature grown delicate, fine, and frail invited, before long, the distaste of the wider public, and provoked, strangely enough, a countervailing force of reaction. That Aizan, taking up the legacy spirit of the great men of letters of the Tokugawa era, should come to wield the hammer he called "historical criticism" may be seen as one manifestation of this. That the Min'yūsha should have raised up Aizan, and that the public should have welcomed him, must likewise be put down to the pent-up force of this same reaction. Reaction carried Aizan along in its course. And now Aizan, in turn, seeks to carry reaction along with him. He has widened the target he means to smash with the hammer called "historical criticism," and now presses hard upon the domain of pure literature as well. That reaction should be allowed to run its own course, I have no objection to. But when I see him borne along by reaction, seeking to carry it so far as to stir up yet another reaction in turn, I cannot help but grieve that our literature should be given over to this fate of drifting from one reaction to the next. Aizan-sei has declared, at the very opening of his essay "On Rai Jō," his recognition that writing is itself an undertaking. And why an undertaking? Aizan-sei explains it thus: first, because it accomplishes something; second, because it benefits the world; third, because it engages with human life.

The ending (original text)

Lift up your head, and look, and seek. With a lofty and expansive vision, look upon a dwelling truly broad, a realm truly joyous and beautiful, an undertaking truly grand — and seek it. Cast your Longing out into the vault of heaven; from that vault of heaven, seize hold of the calling you, as a human being, must fulfill. Ah, men of letters — why should you shrink yourselves down, cramped and confined, to seek only to "engage with life"?
