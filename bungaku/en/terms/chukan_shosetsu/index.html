What is Chūkan Shōsetsu (Middlebrow Fiction)?

Category
Form / Genre

A distinctively Japanese term for fiction positioned between serious literature and popular fiction. After the war, as the reading public expanded and demand grew for entertaining reading matter, writers who had been writing serious literature began turning their pen to more accessible work, and this layer emerged. The term is said to have originated in 1947, when Hayashi Fusao remarked, at a roundtable in the magazine "Shinpū," that "the way forward for the Japanese novel lies midway between serious literature and popular fiction," and Kume Masao dubbed this "chūkan shōsetsu." The magazines "Nihon Shōsetsu" and "Shōsetsu Shinchō," both founded that same year, became its stage: Sakaguchi Ango serialized his full-length orthodox detective novel "Furenzoku Satsujin Jiken" ("The Discontinuous Murder Case") in "Nihon Shōsetsu," Hayashi Fumiko published "Shitamachi" ("The Old Downtown"), depicting the joys and sorrows of a peddling woman, and Yamamoto Shūgorō published period stories such as "Asunarō" and "Shūbun" ("Scandal") in "Shōsetsu Shinchō." In the 1950s, social-issue detective fiction by writers such as Matsumoto Seichō and Mizukami Tsutomu joined this stream, and, sustaining postwar publishing as reading matter that combined entertainment and literary merit, middlebrow fiction went on to unsettle the very distinction between serious and popular literature.
