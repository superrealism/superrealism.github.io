About this work

The work that made Kamura Isota's name, published in the magazine "Fudōchō" in 1928. Keiichirō, who left his wife and child in his home province and eloped to Tokyo with Chitose, earns thirty-five yen a month as a trainee at a liquor-trade newspaper and rents an attic over a rice-cracker shop in Morikawa-chō. A letter from his sister sets down in fine detail his father's illness and grief, and the little son he abandoned. His old teacher G urges him to part from her; Chitose grows thin over her piecework sewing. Guilt toward those he has thrown away and guilt toward the one he now lives with: a representative work of the self-destructive I-novel, staring without mercy at a self that can escape neither.

Summary

Keiichirō, who has let a cold run on until his fever reaches thirty-nine degrees, has missed only three days at the liquor-trade newspaper where he works when a special-delivery summons arrives. As he crawls out of the bedding for fear of being dismissed, Chitose clings to him: he will make it past mending. Four months have passed since the two of them left Y Prefecture and eloped. The letter from his sister at home, which he receives at the office, sets down in fine detail how their father has been in bed for a week; how he muttered, "While I am alive I must somehow settle matters for Keiichirō"; how he watches the postman from the orchard on the hill without taking his eyes off him; and how little Toshio, left behind, cries when he does not want his playmates to go home. His wife Sakiko is demanding ten thousand yen, and the family council is in an uproar. G, the teacher to whom Keiichirō turned first on reaching Tokyo, calls him in almost nightly and patiently reasons with him: this unnatural cohabitation must break down, put aside a delusion of the moment. Chitose, they say, would sob alone upstairs, listening for clogs in the alley. His wages are not enough, and Chitose has pasted up a paper — "Kimono made to order" — and taken in sewing, sitting up night after night until her hair falls out and she is worn to a shadow. Watching her sleeping face in the moonlight, Keiichirō turns over and over.

Characters

  • Ōe Keiichirō　A man who left his wife and child at home and eloped to Tokyo with Chitose. A trainee reporter at a liquor-trade newspaper
  • Chitose　The woman of twenty-three who eloped with Keiichirō. She supports the household by piecework sewing and ruins her health
  • Sister　She writes from home about their father's illness and the straits the family is in
  • Father　The adopted head of the house, who meant Keiichirō to succeed him. From his sickbed he waits for his son to come back
  • Toshio　Keiichirō's little son, the darling of his grandfather
  • Sakiko　Keiichirō's wife. She has gone back to her family and demands money over the divorce

The opening (original text)

It was a mistake to take it lightly, thinking it only a passing cold. In the end his fever went over thirty-nine degrees, and Keiichirō had to stay away from the liquor-trade newspaper in Hamachō where he worked. Even while he lay in bed tormented by the fever, the fear that he might have offended his employer never left him, so that it came out in his delirium. On the morning of the third day a special-delivery summons arrived.

The ending (original text)

With the moonlight that leaked through the gaps of the badly fitting rain shutters full on her face, her hair spread over the white bolster but not a single eyelash stirring, Chitose's well-behaved sleeping face was pale as a sick woman's. He could not help feeling toward Chitose a guilt heavier and deeper than any he felt toward the wife and child he had thrown away at home. And thinking so, a strong pity welled up from somewhere and went on for a while. And then again he would be seized by a blank, absent feeling. What on earth was he to do now? And what was to become of it all? Keiichirō turned over again and again. —
