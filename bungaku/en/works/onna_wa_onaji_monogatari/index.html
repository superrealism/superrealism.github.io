About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Kōdan Kurabu in 1955. Once you are married, all women are much the same — the ironic lesson in life that the chief retainer Kaji Ryūemon gives his son. Through the course of a commotion over a lady's maid, it draws with a bitter humour the nuances of marriage and of women.

Summary

"Well, resign yourself to it — you'll marry the Yasunaga girl," the castellan chief retainer Kaji Ryūemon tells his son Kōichirō. "Whatever girl she is, once you're married, she'll turn out much the same as any other. As unmarried girls they look different in all sorts of ways — and in a sense they truly are different — but in another sense, all women are much the same." The Kaji household chose a lady's maid every year for etiquette training and kept her in service. One year, the mistress of the house announces that she will assign a new maid named Yono to serve the unmarried son, Kōichirō. This arrangement, unusual by the customs of a samurai house and seeming to hint at some purpose, arises just as delicate negotiations for a marriage with the Yasunaga family are underway, and a small commotion breaks out over the maid. But a month later, Ryūemon asks his son, "It's been a month now — seems to have settled down peaceably, hasn't it." "Yes, it has gone peaceably." "Does it strike home now, what I told you — that once you're married, all women turn out much the same?" "Indeed," Kōichirō answers calmly, "just as you said. All women are the same." A piece of Yamamoto's, drawing the nuances of marriage and of women with a bittersweet humour.

Characters

"Well, resign yourself to it, there's no help for it — you'll marry the Yasunaga girl," Ryūemon told his son. "Whatever girl she is, once you're married, she'll turn out much the same as any other.
