About this work

A "novel in the manner of a prose poem," published in the magazine Serpent in 1935. The narrator, who has lost his taste for travel, describes hallucinations in which — under morphine or cocaine, or on a walk in which he has lost his bearings — he strays into another world hidden behind the everyday. Wandering a hot-spring town in the north country, he loses his way and then, quite suddenly, comes out into a beautiful and perfectly ordered street unlike anything on earth. An instant later, the town turns into a strange scene full of cats. A metaphysical dread appearing in the crack between the real and the imagined, drawn with the acute verbal sense only a poet could bring: the best known of Sakutarō's prose.

Summary

The narrator can no longer feel his heart lift at the thought of travel. Travel, he has come to see, is in the end nothing but "the movement of the same objects within the same space": go where one will, the same sort of people are living the same sort of monotonous lives in the same sort of villages and towns. What he kept as a private consolation was a journey of another kind — the journey into a world beyond the order of time and space that the intoxication of morphine and cocaine brought him. Even after he gave up the drugs, he went on having something like the same experience in ordinary life: walking until he lost his sense of direction, he would find a familiar scene turn suddenly inside out into an unknown world. One summer, staying at a hot spring in the north country, he loses his way on his usual walk and his idea of the points of the compass with it. And having strayed out that way, he comes unexpectedly upon a town of a dazzling beauty, elegant and perfectly harmonious — a town of a refinement like a dream, such as he has never seen anywhere. But the instant that moment of rapture passes, the air of the place changes utterly. All around him there are cats, cats, cats — cats in the shape of human beings, crowding the streets in a scene both strange and horrible. In terror he runs away. People will laugh and call it an illusion, a delusion. But the one thing he still holds to, as an absolute truth nothing can shake, is the fact that he did see that town where only cats live; and he believes that the peculiar settlement of which the legends of the northern coast tell must certainly exist somewhere in the universe. It is a work in which a poet fixes, in language of great clarity, the terror of a fantasy that opens in the crack where the order of the real world falls away in an instant.

The opening (original text)

Crush a fly and the "thing in itself" of the fly does not die. You have crushed only the phenomenon of the fly. — Schopenhauer. The lure of travel faded little by little out of my imagination. Once, merely to picture the images of it — trains, steamships, the towns of unknown countries — was enough to set my heart leaping. But past experience taught me that travel is no more than "the movement of the same objects within the same space." Go where one may, the same sort of people are living there, repeating the same sort of monotonous lives in the same sort of villages and towns.

Nekomachi by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But I certainly saw a town inhabited by nothing but cats, a town where cats in human shape crowded the streets. Say what you will by way of reasoning and argument: for me there is no fact so absolutely beyond doubt as that I "saw" it, somewhere in the universe. Standing before the mockery of any number of people, I still firmly believe it in my heart — that the peculiar settlement of which the legends of the north coast tell, the town where none but the spirits of cats live, must certainly exist somewhere in the universe.

Nekomachi by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Cats, cats, cats, cats, cats, cats, cats. Wherever I looked there was nothing but cats.

The scene in which the beautiful town the narrator has strayed into is suddenly overrun by a throng of cats. Through repetition, it makes visible the hallucination brought on by a derangement of the sense of direction.
