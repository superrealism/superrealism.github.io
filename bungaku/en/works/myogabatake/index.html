About this work

An early short story by Mayama Seika, published in "Chūō Kōron" in 1907. Yumura Mamoru, a novelist known young, has been sent a letter breaking off relations by his old teacher K, has had a failed love affair written up in the newspapers, and rages about unable to put pen to paper. He takes it out on the sister and brother-in-law who have moved in after failing in business, takes refuge in drink, and tells a young student sourly that "there is no faith left in the world today." Cutting across the myōga field at Waseda in a rickshaw, he cries inwardly that he will not be beaten — a piece in which Seika, who set out as a Naturalist writer, drew the bravado and the loneliness of the literary man by turning them on himself.

Summary

Yumura Mamoru, a novelist, is thirty and unmarried, and has taken in his sister and her husband after their business failed. That day his nerves have been on edge since morning, and he scolds his sister over everything from the saltiness of the dried horse mackerel to the washing of his underrobe. The night before, Tazumi, the close friend of his dead younger brother, had come with a student named Komura, and over the drink had asked him, "Tell me, what exactly is this thing called love?" Love is a painful fact one must touch, not a question to be discussed for amusement — so Yumura declares, and turns even the newspaper write-up of his own failed romance into a joke. There is no faith left in the world today, he says; there is only understanding. Between husband and wife, between friends, between lovers, there is no affection but the effort to understand each other's minds. From his old teacher K had come a letter breaking off relations: K had taken it into his head that the baseless gossip printed in the newspapers was Yumura's own mischief. It is always like this, he tells himself, leave it and it will pass — and yet he keeps on thinking, there is something, there is something. A visit to his friend Tatsuma does not lift his heart, and on the rickshaw home, cutting across the myōga field, his heart cries out that he will not be beaten, that he will write and show them. That night, listening through the sliding door to his sister and her husband squabbling affectionately, Yumura hurls his Bible at the door.

Characters

  • Yumura Mamoru　A novelist of thirty. Carrying a broken friendship with his old teacher and a failed love affair, he takes it out on his sister and her husband
  • Sister　Put out to a foster family early, she came back four years ago. She and her husband have taken shelter in her brother's house
  • Yui (Riku-san)　The sister's husband. Having failed in business, he looks for work and feels small in the house
  • Tazumi　The close friend of Yumura's dead brother, who stops in on his way to Fukuoka
  • Komura　The student Tazumi brings with him, who asks Yumura what love is

The opening (original text)

1 The evening before, young Tazumi had come to call. He had been the close friend of Yumura's younger brother Rokurō, who died at the end of the year before last. He had stopped in on his way to Fukuoka University. Because the railway was out after the recent floods, he said, he would go as far as Kobe by steamer. He wore an unlined kimono of coarse white kasuri with a black sash, and new trousers of striped cloth, neatly put on. In his provincial way he called Yumura, seven years his senior, "Mamoru-san, Mamoru-san."

The ending (original text)

"Give me your hand — your hand," he said, and gripped his frightened sister's hand hard, and shook it. "Ah — go on living like that all your life," he said, and let it go.
