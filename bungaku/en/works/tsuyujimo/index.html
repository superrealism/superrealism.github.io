About this work

Saitō Mokichi's fourth collection of tanka, holding seven hundred and six poems written between 1918 and 1921, while he was professor at the Nagasaki Medical College. Solitude in a strange place far from home, grief for friends dying one after another, and sketches from life of the country itself — Nagasaki's paving stones and roof tiles, the frogs filling the valley at Urakami — are laid one on another in a quiet resignation. The title was chosen for what answered his state of mind in those last Nagasaki days, and carries the Man'yōshū sense of dew and frost: what vanishes easily. Compiled in 1940 and published after the war, in 1946.

Summary

"The day draws near when I shall go away without seeing the white frost on the tiles of the house I live in" — beginning with a farewell poetry meeting on the eve of leaving Tokyo for Nagasaki, the collection goes on copying down, single-mindedly, the lying down and rising up of a life in a strange country. The smell of the mats in the house he has moved into, an evening when he goes out to buy a bathtub and comes home without one, thoughts of the small child left behind in Tokyo, the bell of the Shōfukuji, the frogs of the village of Urakami, the winged ants that swarm at the electric lamp and shed their wings. Every trifle of daily life becomes a vessel for loneliness just as it is. In time word comes of the death of a friend in Mutsu, and he writes: "Though I think I shall not, in the end, live at peace, why is it that of late my friends die one after another?" There are the sequences of the Nagasaki poetry meetings, poems of travel, and the nights when he lies down alone in fear of the Spanish influenza. Four years of looking at his own life in a strange country are piled up in a restrained measure, and the long afterword at the end tells quietly how his materials were lost in a fire after his return, and how this one volume came to be.

The opening (original text)

Farewell poetry meeting for Saitō Mokichi At Mokichi's house in Aoyama, Tokyo, the twenty-fifth of December, the sixth year of Taishō The day draws near when I shall go away without seeing the white frost on the tiles of the house I live in Made as occasion offered after taking up my post at Nagasaki Even the smell of the mats of the house I have moved to is sad to me, and so I have lain down and risen Going out to buy a bathtub, tonight again I come home without buying one, and sleep lonely Shigeta, the little child I left behind in Tokyo — has he grown big by now?

Tsuyujimo by Saitō Mokichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The seventh year of Taishō falls in my thirty-seventh year, so that the poems of this collection were almost all made between my thirty-seventh and my fortieth years. And since the number of poems in this collection is six hundred and ninety-seven in the text and nine in the afterword, the sum is seven hundred and six. (Written in September of the twentieth year of Shōwa.) For the publication of this collection I am deeply indebted to the good offices of Iwanami Shigeo, Nunokawa Kakuzaemon, Satō Satarō, Nakayama Takeo and Enomoto Junkō. I have been ill since March and am still in bed; and in the meantime I met with the sudden death of Iwanami Shigeo, and my grief has no end. (The twenty-ninth of May, the twenty-first year of Shōwa, at Ōishida. Written by Saitō Mokichi.)

Tsuyujimo by Saitō Mokichi (Aozora Bunko)
