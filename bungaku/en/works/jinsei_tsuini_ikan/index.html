About this work

A reflection in Chinese-flavoured prose that Takayama Chogyū wrote in 1891, at about twenty. What in the end is a human life? Does one live for fame, or for pleasure, or is one born to understand impermanence? Looking at the emptiness of the human world before the graves of a ruined old temple and going on being troubled by a question that has no answer, it sings the doubt and world-weariness of the young Chogyū.

Summary

"What, in the end, is a life for?" — Is a life spent, then, if one lives to accomplish some great deed and dies leaving one's fame to endure a thousand years? Or is all of it nothing more than the pleasure of a single cup of wine enjoyed while still alive? Or ought one, instead, to end one's days having awakened to the truth of impermanence? The author finds his heart led astray by every one of these in turn. Visiting once a ruined, desolate old temple, and seeing there a cluster of nameless ancient burial mounds crumbling away beneath the rain and dew, he reflects that even those who once held ambitions great enough to pierce the heavens now lie voiceless beneath moss-grown graves, and that even the monuments raised for later ages, once the stars have shifted and the world has changed, return at last to mere clods of earth. Whether a name perishes together with its bearer, or perishes a thousand years hence, it comes, in the end, to the very same thing — is it not folly, then, to seek fame and accomplishment at all? Drawing on Zhuangzi's dream and the Buddha's stillness, he laments that if life itself is to be regarded as a dream, then delusion and enlightenment alike are nothing more than dreams themselves, and asks whether man was born for empty fame, or born only for pleasure — and, finding no answer, loses himself, dazed, in the depths of the night.

The opening (original text)

What, in the end, is a life for? Is this not indeed one of the great questions? Should life be counted as fulfilled, if one lives to accomplish some heaven-turning feat of ambition and dies leaving one's fame to hang for a thousand years? I am greatly perplexed by this. To enjoy, while yet alive, a single cup of wine — what need is there of a name lasting a thousand years after death? Is man born only to seek pleasure, and nothing more? I am greatly perplexed by this. What is there to quarrel over, on the horns of a snail? This body lodges but for the flash of a spark struck from stone. Is man born only to awaken to impermanence, and so end his days? I am greatly perplexed by this. Alas — what, in the end, is a life for? Was man, perhaps, born only in order to die?

Jinsei tsuini ikan by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The Old Man of Nanhua (Zhuangzi) has said: only through great awakening does one come to know that this life is a great dream. The teachers of the Buddha instruct us: the stillness that comes from casting off desire is the very means by which the Four Noble Truths are realized. Enough of this — if life itself is to be taken as a dream, then are not delusion and enlightenment alike equally a dream? Even should I regard my own body as no more than grass rooted at a hedge along the shore, and my own life as no more than an unmoored boat drifting by the riverbank, is not the one destination that awaits us all a single grave's gate? If the deeds of a lifetime are no more than the appearance of things seen within a dream, and the fame that follows death no more than dew upon the grass, then to what, in this vast, empty life of mine, can I possibly attach myself? Must I not call this a great sorrow? Ah — what, in the end, is a life for? I look back upon what is past, and ponder what is yet to come, and in the middle of the night, dazed and lost, I lose myself entirely.

Jinsei tsuini ikan by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)
