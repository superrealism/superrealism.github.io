About this work

One of Mori Ōgai's central late stories, published in the magazine Chūō Kōron in January 1916. Through the escort of a man being sent into exile for killing his brother, it quietly raises the question of mercy killing and of the idea of "knowing what is enough." Written in the spare manner of his historical accounts, and generally taken as the furthest point of Ōgai's fiction of ideas.

Summary

On the Takase boats, which carried convicts from Kyoto down to Osaka, it was the custom to allow one relative of the convict aboard as well. One evening the constable Haneda Shōbei is to escort a man called Kisuke, sentenced to banishment to the islands for killing his brother; and, puzzled that a convict should look somehow serene, he asks him for the particulars. Kisuke tells him that his brother, in the pain of a grave illness, had cut his own throat with a razor and had not managed to die; that he had begged to be released quickly; and that Kisuke had drawn the razor out as he was asked. Thinking that it was done to save his brother from his suffering, Shōbei cannot judge whether what Kisuke did was a crime. Realizing that it is beyond his own authority, and settling in his mind that there is nothing for it but to leave the judgment to the magistrate, Shōbei goes down the Takase River with the boat in the late night.

Characters

  • Haneda Shōbei　The constable escorting Kisuke on the Takase boat. Kisuke's story leaves him troubled over the rights and wrongs of a merciful death
  • Kisuke　A man banished to the islands for killing his brother. His serene manner perplexes Shōbei
  • Kisuke's brother　In the misery of a grave illness he wishes for death, and dies with his brother's help

The opening (original text)

The Takase boat is a small boat that goes up and down the Takase River in Kyoto. In the Tokugawa period, when a convict of Kyoto was sentenced to banishment to a distant island, his relatives were called to the prison and were permitted to take their leave of him there. After that the convict was put aboard a Takase boat and sent round to Osaka. The escort was a constable under the Kyoto city magistrate, and it was the custom for this constable to permit one of the principal relatives to travel with the convict as far as Osaka. This was not a thing that had gone up through official channels; it was what is called turning a blind eye — permission by silence.

The ending (original text)

Shōbei listened as though he were seeing the scene before his own eyes; but from halfway through the account the doubt had arisen in him whether this was really the killing of a brother, whether it was really murder, and even when the account was finished he could not resolve it. The brother had said that if the razor were drawn out he would be able to die, and had asked for it to be drawn out. Kisuke had drawn it out and let him die; it can be said that he killed him. Yet even had it been left as it was, the brother was one who could not but die. That he said he wanted to die quickly was because he could not bear the pain. Kisuke could not bear to watch that pain. He ended the life meaning to save him from it. Is that a crime? To have killed is certainly a crime. But when one thinks that it was in order to save him from pain, the doubt arises there and cannot be resolved. After turning it over in one way and another, there arose in Shōbei's mind the thought that there was nothing for it but to leave the matter to the judgment of one above himself — that he had no choice but to follow authority. Shōbei meant to take the magistrate's judgment for his own. Even so, something in him still would not settle, and he could not help wanting somehow to ask the magistrate about it. Through a hazy night that deepened as it went, the Takase boat, carrying its two silent men, slid over the black surface of the water.

Famous passages

The takasebune is a small boat that plies up and down the Takase River in Kyoto.

A quiet opening that begins with an explanation of a small boat on a Kyoto canal. It sets the frame for a short story that asks what crime is, and what mercy killing is.

That he had killed him was surely a crime. And yet, thinking that he had done it to free his brother from suffering, a doubt arose that could not, however he turned it over, be resolved.

Shōbei's reflection after hearing the full circumstances of a brother's killing. It hands the question of mercy killing to the reader, unresolved.

Thinking on like this, from one thing to the next, there was no knowing how far a man might go before he could stop. And it was this Kisuke, Shōbei realized, who was now showing him, before his very eyes, what it was to stop.

Shōbei's reflection after hearing the story of the unacquisitive Kisuke. Contrasting human nature, which extends its desires without end, with Kisuke, who knows contentment, it shows the work's other theme.
