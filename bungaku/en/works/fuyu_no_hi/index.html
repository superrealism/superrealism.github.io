About this work

A short story Kajii Motojirō published in two parts, in February and April 1927, in the coterie magazine Aozora. Takashi, a young man ill in the lungs who has lost his appetite for living, passes his days from a rented upstairs room watching the faint sunlight of the approach to the winter solstice, set down in razor-sharp sensory description. With "Lemon," one of Kajii's central works, raising to the level of prose poetry the beauty of light and shade as a weakening body catches it, and a transparent consciousness inside a presentiment of death.

Summary

It is nearly the winter solstice. From the window of Gyō, who suffers from consumption, he can see the trees losing their leaves day by day. The phlegm he coughs up each time he washes his face has begun to be streaked with blood, and Gyō no longer feels any eagerness for life at all. The shade that steadily fills the sky, the last rays of sun falling into the alley, the night in which he can only recover himself in darkness. His mother's letters repeat, again and again, that he must go to a doctor, but Gyō's heart cannot answer to them. Neither a friend's visit nor the bustle of the town streets can save him from his solitude. The fragile winter sunlight fades before even an hour has passed since he rose from bed. On the evening of a certain year's end, murmuring "ah, I want to see a great sunset," Gyō walks on and on through an unfamiliar town in search of some distant view. But at every vantage point there is only the silhouette of some great rooftop, and all that reaches his heart, achingly, is the hidden shape of the sun. In the sunset sky, drifting clouds burn, one after another, beautifully, and then turn to dead ash. "Why must a time so beautiful be so short?" A heavy weariness suddenly presses down upon him, and on an unfamiliar street corner of an unfamiliar town, Gyō's heart would not brighten again.

The opening (original text)

The season was nearing the winter solstice. From Gyō's window he could see, day by day, the leaves stripping away from the trees standing in the gardens and by the gates of the low-lying houses below. The scraggly sesame shrub had gone as wild and tangled as an old woman's windblown hair; the last leaves of the cherry tree, beautifully scorched by frost, had fallen away; and each time the zelkova trembled, rustling, in the wind, some further portion of the landscape that had lain hidden behind it came into view.

Fuyu no hi by Kajii Motojirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In the clear, blue-transparent sky, drifting clouds burned, one after another, in beauty. And in time that fire caught, too, at the smoldering embers of Gyō's unsatisfied heart. "Why must a time so beautiful be so short?" Never did he feel life more fleeting than at such a moment. The burning clouds began, one after another, to turn to dead ash once more. His feet would carry him no further. "I wonder whose shadow on the earth that shade now steadily filling the sky will become. Unless I go to that cloud over there, I shall not see the sun again today." A heavy weariness suddenly pressed down upon him. On an unfamiliar street corner of an unfamiliar town, Gyō's heart would not brighten again.
