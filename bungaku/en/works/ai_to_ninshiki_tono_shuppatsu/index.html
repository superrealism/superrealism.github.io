About this work

A collection holding nearly all the reflections and essays Kurata Hyakuzō wrote from the age of twenty-one, while at the First Higher School, to the end of his twenties. Published by Iwanami Shoten in 1921, it became, with The Priest and His Disciples, his central work. "The Cognitive Effort of Life," guided by Nishida Kitarō's An Inquiry into the Good; "The Heart That Would Find Itself in the Other Sex," which asks head on what love and sexual desire mean; "Love as Toward a Neighbour," on Christian love — the fundamental questions of a young person, truth, friendship, love and faith, are set out one after another. Read from the students of the old higher schools to those of the postwar years, and known as a central book of Taishō culturalism.

Summary

The opening piece, "Longing — a letter from Sannosuke —" is a letter written at twenty-one to a friend, comparing truth to "a lover not yet seen" and setting down his longing for philosophy. In "The Effort of Life to Know," which follows, guided deeply by Nishida Kitarō's An Inquiry into the Good, he asks how living and knowing can become one. "The Heart That Would Find Itself in the Other Sex" looks at love and at physical desire without separating them, and holds that at the bottom of the hunger for the other sex there is a heart seeking the completion of oneself — a piece that struck the young men of the day with great force. After passing through the pain of a lost love in "The Road Walked by One Who Has Lost His Love," the argument goes on to "Love as of a Neighbor" and "The Two Functions of Love," and the author comes deliberately to put aside the language of scholarship and to choose words that reach the heart directly. In "The Prayer to Become Good," "The True Way and the False Way" and "Man and Woman on Earth," concrete habits of the heart — dependence, irony, purity — are examined one by one; and in the closing piece, "Looking at a Picture of the Thousand-Armed Kannon," the question how far one may serve one's neighbor within the limits of a human body converges upon prayer.

The opening (original text)

This book is offered to the young who come after Brethren, I write no new commandment unto you, but an old commandment which ye had from the beginning. The old commandment is the word which ye have heard from the beginning. Again, a new commandment I write unto you. — from the First Epistle of John, chapter two — On the occasion of a new edition Since its publication this book has been read widely among young people who love life and truth, and has gone through many editions; and even now, amid the hurry of the times, it goes on being read among the deep-hearted and pure young who mean not to lose the true shape of their own nature.

Ai to ninshiki tono shuppatsu by Kurata Hyakuzō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In that sense it seems to me that the service of a human being must in the end become prayer. One cannot help calling down blessing upon mankind in the name of God, or coming to Shinran's feeling that he would "become a Buddha quickly and save all beings as his heart desires." Looking at the picture of the Thousand-Armed and Thousand-Eyed Kannon, I have had all these thoughts of mine stirred anew. And I cannot help kneeling before this image with its fine body and its necklace of jewels. And I cannot help feeling the sorrow of the human body and the longing for the further shore. I pray that, by striving in my own art and deepening my faith, I may at least make amends for my neglect of this direct service to my neighbor. (15 December 1920, at Ōmori)
