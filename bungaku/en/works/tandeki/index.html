About this work

Iwano Hōmei's representative work, published in "Shinchō" in 1909. A novella carried entirely in the first person, telling how "I," having moved to the seashore at Kōzu to write a play, sinks into an obsession with Kichiya, the geisha kept by the restaurant next door. At home there is a sick wife; the woman has a disease of the eyes; money and feeling alike wear through. And in the hero who brazens it out all the same, declaring that "weakened nerves need a sharp injection," the thought of the "mystical half-bestialism" that affirms the instincts of life exactly as they are appears directly. One of the representative works of Naturalist literature.

Summary

Wanting to write a complicated play, "I" avoided the noisy inns and took a room in an ordinary house by the sea at Kōzu. Next door is the restaurant Izutsuya, where the real power is the old mistress Otei, with her niece Okimi, who is sixteen, and one geisha. Because "I" know English, I am asked to teach the children simple conversation, and one day, from the well, a woman in a slovenly nightdress smiles across at me. That is Kichiya. At first her dark skin repelled me; before long I sink into her past all extricating. At home in Tokyo there is a sickly wife, and Kichiya has troubles of her own that must be taken on. I spend money, I am lied to, I am cheated, and still I cannot get free. The talk of buying out her contract goes wrong, and Kichiya goes up to Tokyo under the new name Kikuko — and there her eyes fall ill. My wife's hysteria, Kikuko's diseased eyes, and my own exhaustion. Setting out with a friend for "revenge," "I" treat the piteous appeals of Kikuko's mother coldly and leave the house, thinking of my "goodbye" as a song of triumph.

Characters

  • I　A writer staying at Kōzu to write a play. The narrator, who sinks into an obsession with Kichiya
  • Kichiya (Kikuko)　The geisha kept by the Izutsuya. She later goes up to Tokyo and falls ill in the eyes
  • Old Otei　The mistress who holds the real power at the Izutsuya. She calls the master and his wife by their bare names
  • Okimi　The sixteen-year-old daughter of the Izutsuya. Like her aunt in temper, she orders her parents about
  • Wife　"My" wife, at home in Tokyo. She suffers from hysteria

The opening (original text)

1 I was to spend a summer on the shore at Kōzu. I had meant, through a friend's introduction, to borrow a room at a certain temple; but when I called there I found they had troubles of one kind and another and could not look after a guest that summer, so that, with an introduction from the abbot, I arranged to be taken in at an ordinary house instead. I wanted to write a somewhat complicated play, and so avoided the noise of an inn.

The ending (original text)

"Please give my regards to your wife," the old woman said. Kikuko, feeling, as well she might, how helpless she was, gave us a forlorn smile and said, as if reluctant to see us go, "Sensei, if only my eyes were better I would come with you —" I made no answer to that, but with my friend, thinking of "goodbye" as a song of triumph, took my leave of the place.
