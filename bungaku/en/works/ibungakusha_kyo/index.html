About this work

If your heart is set on living without working, change your religion at once and become a man of letters — a scathing piece of parody Uchida Roan collected in How to Become a Man of Letters. Borrowing the form of a sutra, he sets out at length just how easily the writers of the day live and how the vulgar exalt them. At once a merciless satire on the literary world of Meiji and a piece to be enjoyed for the sharpness of Roan's invective.

Summary

"You who wait for a bean-jam bun to fall from the shelf; you who ape the Chinese style so cleverly, third generation of the house; you blind turtle searching for a floating log" — from the very opening lines, words tumble forth that could as easily be curses as invitations, all leading to the conclusion: "All you good men and women of this world, if it is your fondest wish to live at your ease, then change your faith at once and become a man of letters." The "man of letters" Roan speaks of here is not the lofty scholar of whom Fichte spoke, but rather today's crop of "gentlemen authors, thoroughly schooled in their trade." They revile the vulgar masses even as they live off that very vulgar mass's adoration. The faith of the vulgar is the very lifeblood of these gentlemen authors, and to receive the vulgar's appreciation is the crowning glory of their careers — so to criticize them for it, Roan says with biting irony, is simply to show one's ignorance of the literature of the present age. What follows, in a tone modeled on Buddhist sutras, is an account of just how easy a trade it is to be a man of letters these days — how one may laugh off natural disasters, make light of human and social affairs, and drift, quite naturally, into a kind of hypnotic trance under heaven's own special favor. "A man of letters, oh, a man of letters!" And then, declaring himself under the name "Sanmojiya Kinpei," who long ago took the great vow to save the world, Roan proclaims that it is precisely in order to make every good man and woman, without exception, into a man of letters, that he now preaches this "Sutra for Becoming a Man of Letters." Let the worrier fretting over the falling price of silver, and the nervous gentleman anxious over his reduced salary, both come and listen to this sermon — and only then become men of letters.

The opening (original text)

You who wait for a bean-jam bun to fall down from the shelf; you third-generation heir so clever at aping the Chinese style; you blind turtle searching for a floating log; you idiot who swallows ginseng and then means to hang himself; you clever set who put your faith in a sardine's head; you brood of earthworms longing to climb into the clouds; you mountain monkey trying to snatch the moon reflected in the water; you man of no skill, no talent, indigestion in every limb, whose wits will not go round his whole body — all you good men and women of this world, if it is your fondest wish to live at your ease, then change your faith at once and become a man of letters.

Ibungakusha kyō by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Look at it from the right, or look at it from the left — the man of letters is the most fortunate, most blessed creature of all. If you doubt nothing of this sermon of mine, which lifts away suffering and grants bliss, and single-mindedly hold it in blind and grateful faith, then rebirth into paradise, even in this very life, will be no difficult thing at all. Let the worrier fretting over the falling price of silver, and the nervous gentleman anxious over his reduced salary, or the piglet made sluggish and bloated by his own indigestion, or the papier-mâché tiger forever needlessly wagging its head — let all of them come and listen to this sermon, and only then become men of letters.

Ibungakusha kyō by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
