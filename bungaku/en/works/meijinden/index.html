About this work

A short story published in the magazine Bunko in December 1942. A parable drawn from the Chinese classics: Ji Chang sets out to become the greatest of archers, exhausts every refinement of technique, and arrives at last at "shooting without shooting." Told in an easy, wry voice, it turns on the paradox that true mastery lies in forgetting technique altogether. The last work Nakajima published in his lifetime.

Summary

Ji Chang, who has set his mind on becoming the first archer under heaven, becomes the pupil of the master Fei Wei, trains himself never to blink and to see the minute as though it were large, and at last acquires an aim that never misses. But when his plot to do away with his master, the only rival he has left, comes to light, Fei Wei sends him to study under Gan Ying, the legendary old man who lives on the summit of Mount Huo. Under Gan Ying, what Ji Chang sees is a divine skill that brings down birds in flight by will alone, without bow or arrow. Nine years later, coming down from the mountain, Ji Chang has lost all expression, as though he were another man, and no one has ever seen him shoot. In his old age, seeing a bow at someone's house, the aged Ji Chang could not remember what it was or what it was used for. In a man who had forgotten even the name of the bow and what it was for, people were struck with awe: here indeed was the furthest reach of a master without equal in any age.

Characters

  • Ji Chang　The protagonist, who sets his mind on becoming the first archer under heaven
  • Fei Wei　The great archer who is Ji Chang's first master
  • Old Gan Ying　The legendary archer who lives on the summit of Mount Huo. Ji Chang's second master, who shows him the archery of not shooting

The opening (original text)

A man called Ji Chang, who lived in Handan, the capital of Zhao, set his mind on becoming the first archer under heaven. Looking about for a man to take as his master, he could not think that anyone in the world of the bow at that time could match the great archer Fei Wei. He was an adept, they said, who could hit a willow leaf a hundred paces off a hundred times in a hundred. Ji Chang travelled far to find Fei Wei and entered his school.

The ending (original text)

"Ah — that the master, the master who is the archer without equal in any age, should have forgotten the bow! Ah, the very name of the bow, and what it is used for!" For some while after that, they say, in the capital of Handan the painters hid their brushes, the musicians cut the strings of their instruments, and the carpenters were ashamed to take up their rule and compasses.

Famous passages

A man named Ji Chang, who lived in Handan, capital of the state of Zhao, set his mind on becoming the greatest archer under heaven.

An opening that introduces its protagonist in the tone of a storyteller's tale. It begins a whimsical fable that leads, at the far end of mastering the bow, to “the shooting that shoots not” — the forgetting of the bow itself.

Ah, the master — the master, unrivaled in archery through all ages — has forgotten the bow entirely? Ah, even the very name of the bow, and what it is for!

The closing, in which people marvel at the old master who has returned home having forgotten even the name and use of the bow. It depicts one who has reached the utmost extreme of mastery, with a touch of comedy.

The highest action is no action; the highest speech has no words; the highest shooting is not to shoot.

Ji Chang's answer when asked why he has come home without a bow. It shows a Taoist state in which the ultimate act is not to act, and the ultimate shot is not to shoot.

“I no longer know the distinction between self and other, nor the difference between right and wrong. My eyes seem like ears, my ears like a nose, my nose like a mouth.”

The reflection of the aged Ji Chang in his last years. Speaking of a state in which the distinctions of self and other, right and wrong, and even the senses have melted together, it depicts the master's culmination with a strange stillness.
