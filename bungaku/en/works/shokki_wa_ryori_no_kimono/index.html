About this work

A piece based on a talk Kitaōji Rosanjin gave to a gathering of cooks. He explains why, once he had begun to cook, he built a kiln and started making ceramics and lacquerware himself — all from the single point that food and the vessel cannot be separated. It is the source of the famous line that tableware is the clothing of food, and states concisely the heart of his thinking about eating: that to make food look beautiful is nothing other than to make it taste good.

Summary

Why have I come to make porcelain and lacquerware? — so Rosanjin puts it to his hearers. That, having begun in cookery, he should build a kiln and make his vessels himself may look to an onlooker like an excess of eccentricity; but to him it is only natural. Even in a dish of sashimi, one takes pains over the sharpness of the knife and over the color and shape of the garnish, precisely because in doing so a beautiful feeling arises in the food and the dish as a whole becomes better to eat. If that is so, the vessel on which the food is set cannot possibly be a matter of indifference. The vessel is the food's clothing, and it is the same as when clothes that do not suit a person fail to set the person off. And yet the vessels that the restaurants of the world use are all of them out of proportion with the food. Then one has no choice but to make them oneself — that is why he built his kiln. At the end Rosanjin laments the tendency of a man who can handle a fish a little to think himself a full-fledged cook and to look at nothing else; and he closes his lecture by saying that this is not a matter for fine, high-class restaurants alone, but that an oden shop can do it in the way of an oden shop, and that the same spirit is needed in shaping an entrance hall or in sprinkling water on the ground.

The opening (original text)

How have I come to make porcelain and lacquerware? Most of you, I think, already know it: having begun in cookery, I have built a kiln here as you see and make porcelain and lacquerware with my own hands. Why I should be so absorbed in making pottery and set my own hand to it may look to an onlooker like a very great excess of eccentricity; but I myself think it perfectly natural, and today, by way of a story to take home with you, I should like to say a word about the reason.

Shokki wa ryōri no kimono by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I have talked in very rough terms; but this is by no means a matter for fine, high-class restaurants alone. An oden shop can do it in the way of an oden shop, interestingly and to good purpose; and in shaping so much as an entrance hall, or in sprinkling so much as a little water on the ground, I believe the spirit must be the same.

Shokki wa ryōri no kimono by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)
