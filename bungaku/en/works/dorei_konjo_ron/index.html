About this work

A captive is not eaten but kept alive and set to hard labour — from that, Ōsugi Sakae argues, the slave was born, and with private ownership of land, class. Setting out records of societies before civilization, he explains how blind obedience to the strong cut so deep a groove in the human brain as one great moral law. All the morality of today, resting on obedience, is in the end a relic of that slave temper. His conclusion — that it is easy to change the articles of a constitution but not easy to erase this temper — states the starting point of his own movement.

Summary

Cut down, burned, or eaten — a prisoner who by rights must lose his life in one way or another has his life alone spared and is set to hard labor. This, says Ōsugi, is the most important origin of slavery in primitive times. The development of agriculture took the pleasure of cannibalism from the savage, and prisoners came to be used as beasts of burden in the fields. With the same development of agriculture arose the system of private property in land, and the poor man who could not repay his debt became the slave of the rich. Thus master and slave, upper and lower classes, were born within the free primitive villages of anarchic communism, and men began to differ in their morality according to their social position. ... The right of the victor over the vanquished is absolute and without limit. Slaves are treated like beasts of burden and cattle: thrown away when they fall ill, killed when they give offense, bartered in place of money. And they do not think this unreasonable; they are resigned to it as the fate proper to them. And first of all they imitated the dog among the domestic animals. The Kaffirs greet their chief at every meeting with "I am your dog"; and in many parts of Africa there are ceremonies of stripping off one's clothes, lying face down on the ground, pouring sand over one's head, and crawling forward on all fours. What began in terror ceases in time to be painful, and turns at last into religious worship. The chief becomes the brother of the sun, makes the rain fall, and stands above the law. When a chief dies his wives and slaves follow him, or are thrown into a pit and starved. Having set down a string of absurd facts, Ōsugi asks leave for a word of conclusion.

The opening (original text)

Cut down, or burned, or else eaten — a prisoner who in any case must lose his life has his life alone spared and is set to hard labor. To put it in a word, this is the most important origin of slavery in primitive times.

Dorei konjō ron by Ōsugi Sakae (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

To change the form of a government or to alter the articles of a constitution is nothing of a task. But to make this slave spirit vanish, graven as it has been into the brains of our species for tens or hundreds of thousands of years, is by no means an easy undertaking. And yet, if we are truly to be free men, this is a thing that must be accomplished.

Dorei konjō ron by Ōsugi Sakae (Aozora Bunko)
