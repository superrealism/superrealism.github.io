Famous passages by Niimi Nankichi

Well-known lines from the works of Niimi Nankichi.

7 passages

Gon, the Little Fox

This is a story I heard, when I was small, from an old man in the village named Mohei.

The opening of this best-known of children's stories frames it as a folk tale handed down in the village. It leads into the poignant bond between the mischievous fox Gon and Hyōju.

“Gon, was it you? You were the one who always brought me chestnuts.” Gon, his eyes shut tight, limp, gave a nod.

The final scene, in which Hyōju finally understands, kneeling beside the fox he has shot. All the sorrow of a bond that ends without ever quite meeting gathers into this one line.

Ojiisan no ranpu

“This is how I'll quit my trade.”

The words of Minosuke, who has realized that the age of electric light has come, as he hangs the lamps he sold on the trees by a pond, lights them, and breaks them one by one with stones. It lingers as the graceful way a man left behind by his times takes his leave.

Buying Mittens

Cold winter came down from the north, even to the forest where the mother fox and her cub lived.

A children's story that opens with the arrival of winter. From this single line begins a night shared by a mother fox who fears humans and a cub who knows nothing of that fear.

Can it really be true that humans are good creatures? Can it really be true that humans are good creatures?

The closing, after the cub returns safely with his mittens. The murmured question of the mother fox, who has always feared humans, leaves its echo without ever being answered.

“Mama, my hands are cold. My hands are all tingly and stinging,” he said, holding out his hands, wet and turned peony-red, to his mother fox.

The words of the little fox, having touched snow for the first time, to his mother. The babyish expression conveys the cold as a sensation on the skin, and the story begins to move from the mother's worry for her child.

Dendenmushi no kanashimi

“Everyone carries sorrow. It isn't only me. I must bear my own sorrow.” And so the snail stopped lamenting.

The ending, in which the snail who lamented that its shell was full of sorrow comes to a realization after visiting friend after friend. The story is also known for having been introduced by Empress Michiko (as she then was) in her keynote address to the 1998 world congress of the International Board on Books for Young People (IBBY).
