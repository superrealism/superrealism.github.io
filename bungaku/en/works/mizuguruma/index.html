About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Omoshiro Kurabu in 1954. Wakao, a girl performer who shows the halberd trick called the water wheel and falling blossom in a booth at Okazaki in Mikawa. A piece drawing freshly, in the rain, a love across rank between her and Hirota Wajirō, a young samurai who has succeeded to the headship of an elder's house.

Summary

In the spring of Meiwa 5, in the precincts of Kōenji temple in the castle town of Okazaki, Mikawa, the Iwamoto Shin'nojō troupe put on a booth performance. Hirota Wajirō is invited by his cousin Taniguchi Shūri to go and see it. The Hirota family holds an elder's post worth six hundred and fifty koku, but two years before, his elder sister's suicide brought misfortune down on the family, his father retired, and Wajirō, at eighteen, succeeded to the headship of the house, though he still held no official post. As Wajirō grows bored with the dancing and acrobatics, Shūri holds him there, saying, "I want you to see this one they call Miwaka Tayū." Before long, Miwaka Tayū's performance of "the Water Wheel and Falling Blossom Return" begins — the performer was a girl of fifteen or sixteen. A round, chubby, innocent face, and large eyes that seemed to carry the scent of milk still about them. Dressed in warrior's hakama with a sash tied back and a headband, holding a practice halberd, she struck back, without a single miss, the red and white balls that three men threw at her one after another — a strikingly brilliant skill. From this meeting with the girl performer Wakao, a love crossing the boundary of rank begins for Wajirō. At the close of the story, the two of them walk off side by side, in the rain, toward her lodgings. To Wajirō, who teases her, "Wakao might even end up teaching that trick to some young lady of rank, at this rate," Wakao answers, tossing her head, "As if," and hoists the halberd onto her shoulder. Wajirō turns and smiles quietly. A piece of Yamamoto's, drawing fresh and vivid the love between a wandering girl performer and a young samurai, in the rain.

The opening (original text)

The second month of spring, Meiwa 5. — In the precincts of Kōenji temple, at the western edge of the castle town of Okazaki in Mikawa Province, a troupe calling itself the Iwamoto Shin'nojō company put on a booth performance. Hirota Wajirō, invited by his friend Taniguchi Shūri, went to see it one day.

Mizuguruma by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Now really, whatever you may think of me, that's — as if, I say." Wakao tossed her head and hoisted the halberd onto her shoulder. Wajirō turned to look at her and smiled quietly. The two of them walked off side by side, in the rain, toward her lodgings.
