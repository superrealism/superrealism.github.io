About this work

A short story set in Tsugaru just after the defeat, published in the magazine Shinchō in December 1946. A man calling himself a primary-school classmate turns up at the narrator's family house, where he has taken refuge after being burned out. The visitor drinks his whisky dry without ceremony and asks for blankets as well, and the narrator plays the shallow man of society throughout — half a day recounted with a bitter self-mockery. A central late story, sharp on the manners and the power relations of the postwar.

Summary

At the beginning of September 1946, a big fellow in farm clothes comes plodding up to the narrator, who has been burned out and has taken refuge at his family house in Tsugaru. The man says he is Hirata, a classmate from primary school; he brings up the scar of a fight the narrator cannot remember, proposes a class reunion, and before long comes out with, "Have you no drink?" He empties one bottle of the narrator's treasured whisky after another, tells him to have his wife pour, reels off his boasts about his days in Tokyo, and asks for the blankets issued to those who lost their homes. Steer clear of a wild horse — and so the narrator plays "the shallow man of society" from start to finish, and sees him out at the front door with the last bottle for a parting gift. And just as they are about to part, the man whispers fiercely into his ear.

Characters

  • I　A writer burned out of his home and taking refuge at the family house in Tsugaru, who plays "the shallow man of society" throughout
  • Hirata　A farmer who calls on him claiming to be a classmate from primary school, and asks without hesitation for drink and blankets alike
  • My wife　Who comes out to greet the guest when called, and then makes the children an excuse to leave the room

The opening (original text)

At the beginning of September 1946, I received a visit from a certain man. This affair is very nearly not romantic at all, and it is not in the least journalistic either; and yet it is such an oddly unbearable affair that I think it may leave in my breast a mark that will not be erased until I die. Affair. But then, to call it an affair is perhaps to make too much of it. It amounts to nothing more than this: that a certain man and I drank together, that there was no quarrel or anything of the kind, and that we parted, in appearance at least, in perfect harmony.

Shin'yū kōkan by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

A classmate from primary school he may have been; but though I had five or six real friends from that time, I have almost no recollection of this man. And he can hardly have had any memory of me in those days either, apart from that business about the fight. And yet we had spent a good half day in friendly intercourse between old friends. The extreme word rape even came into my mind. But that was not yet the end of it. One further touch was added to crown the whole. He was a man one can only call splendid, superb. I saw him as far as the front door, and just as we were to part he whispered fiercely into my ear: "Don't give yourself airs!"

Shin'yū kōkan by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)
