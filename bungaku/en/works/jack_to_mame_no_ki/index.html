About this work

Jack, an idle boy living poorly with his mother, gets strange beans in exchange for their one cow — and overnight a beanstalk grows up to the sky. The English folk tale retold by Kusuyama Masao in Japanese plain enough for a child to read aloud.

Summary

Long ago in England, in the days of Good King Alfred, a widow lived with her only son, Jack, in a little cottage far from London. Easygoing, careless, and lazy though he was, she loved him so dearly that she let him do no work at all, only play. But year by year their poverty deepened, until at last all that remained to them was a single cow. Sent to market to sell her, Jack was talked round by a clever butcher he met on the road, and came home instead with a sack of magic beans. His mother, furious, flung the beans out of the window, and the next morning a beanstalk had shot up in the garden, reaching clear out of sight into the sky. Climbing its ladder-like stalk to the very top, Jack found himself in a strange country. There a fairy in a red hood appeared and told him that his father had once been slain by a man-eating giant, who had stolen away his castle and his treasure — and that it was now Jack's task to win it all back. Slipping into the giant's castle, Jack made off, on three separate visits, with a hen that laid eggs of gold, a bag bursting with gold and silver, and a golden harp that played of its own accord. On the last of these the giant woke and gave chase down the beanstalk, until Jack, with an axe his mother brought him, cut through the stalk at its root and brought the giant crashing down. This is Kusuyama Masao's rendering of the English folk tale — one piece among his broader work of retelling the world's fairy tales in gentle Japanese a child could read aloud.

The opening (original text)

Once upon a time, long, long ago in England, in the days of Good King Alfred, there lived, in a little cottage far from the city of London, a poor widow all alone with her small son, Jack. He was her only child, and though he was a terribly easygoing, careless, lazy sort of boy, he was truly good-hearted underneath it all, so that his mother thought of nothing morning or night but "Jack, Jack," and loved him so dearly she would gladly have kept him safe as the apple of her eye. She never once set him to work, but only let him play.

Jack and the Beanstalk by Kusuyama Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"It was the very moment you saw that beanstalk ladder and made up your mind, then and there, to climb it as far as it would go — that was the true beginning of Jack's good fortune. Had you only stood gazing at it, wondering vaguely how strange it was, and let the moment pass, then even if the cow you traded away had somehow found its way back to you, you would still be living in poverty, just as before. And so, in climbing that ladder of beanstalk, you were climbing, at one and the same time, the ladder of your own good luck." So the fairy told them, and, taking her leave of Jack and of Jack's mother both, she went on her way home.

Jack and the Beanstalk by Kusuyama Masao (Aozora Bunko)
