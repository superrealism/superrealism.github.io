About this work

The children's story that is the forerunner of "Matasaburō of the Wind." The red-haired boy who transfers into the small school on the bank of the mountain stream admits from the first, in this version, that he is a spirit of the wind, and tells the children of winds all over the world. The freedom and the loneliness of the wind itself, cut from the finished version, are drawn here head on: a piece worth reading against the later one.

Summary

"Do-do do do-dō-do do-dō-do do-dō, / blow away the sweet pomegranates too" — on the morning of the first of September, in the classroom of the little school on the bank of the valley stream, a red-haired child nobody knew was sitting there, calm as could be. While Kasuke and the others are making a fuss, the child announces that he is Matasaburō of the wind — a spirit of the wind, one who blows all round the world. From then on, every day after school Matasaburō tells the children what he has seen and heard. The winds of the high sky, the currents of the upper air, the times he blew in Russia and in America and over the Indian Ocean, how he flattened the rice and was hated for it, even the reasons why the wind blows — and Ichirō and Kasuke and the rest are drawn into his talk, now astonished, now vying with him, now angry. Through days of picking grapes with the children, swimming in the river and running about the fields, Matasaburō gradually becomes one of them; but being the wind, he cannot stay in one place. One stormy morning Matasaburō was gone. "Do-do do do-dō-do do-dō-do do-dō, blow away the sweet pomegranates too" — when Ichirō shouts it, the song comes back from within the far-off wind. "Good-bye, Ichirō." Away beyond the hedge Matasaburō's glass cape flashed, and his red cheeks and tangled red hair were seen for an instant — and then they had gone clean away, and there was nothing but the clouds flying on and on.

Characters

  • Matasaburō　The spirit of the wind. A child with red hair and a glass cape
  • Ichirō　A sixth-year boy, the eldest of the children
  • Kasuke　The boy who argues with Matasaburō

The opening (original text)

Do-do do do-dō-do do-dō-do do-dō, ah, blow away the sweet pomegranates too, blow away the sour pomegranates too, do-do do do-dō-do do-dō-do do-dō. On the bank of a valley stream there was a small square school. School though it was, it had an entrance and one classroom with three glass windows and nothing else — no assembly room and no teachers' room, and a playground about the size of a tennis court.

Kaze no matasaburō (sakigata) by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Ichirō looked toward the chestnut tree the voice had come from. Suddenly, over his head — "Good-bye, Ichirō" — and no sooner had it been said than the voice was away over by the cypress hedge. Ichirō shouted loudly. "Matasaburō! Good-bye!" Away beyond the hedge Matasaburō's glass cape flashed, and then his red cheeks and his tangled red hair were seen for an instant, and then they had gone clean away, and there was nothing but the clouds flying on and on; and Ichirō stood there taking the wind full on his back, with his hand stretched out that way.
