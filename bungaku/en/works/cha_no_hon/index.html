About this work

The Japanese translation, by Muraoka Hiroshi, of The Book of Tea, which Okakura Tenshin published in English in New York in 1906. Beginning from a single cup of tea, it speaks of Taoism and Zen, the plainness of the tea room, the way of looking at flowers and at art, and the death of Rikyū, and sets out for Western readers an Eastern sense of beauty and philosophy of life. The sentence that the essence of the tea ceremony lies in the worship of the imperfect is famous beyond quoting.

Summary

"Tea began as a medicine and grew into a beverage" — the first chapter, "The Cup of Humanity," explains how tea, which in China entered the realm of poetry, was in fifteenth-century Japan ennobled into a religion of aestheticism, Teaism. Teaism is a cult founded on the adoration of the beautiful among the sordid facts of everyday existence, and its essence lies in the worship of the Imperfect. The second chapter, "The Schools of Tea," follows the three ages of boiled tea under the Tang, whipped tea under the Sung and steeped tea under the Ming, and the work of Luwuh, the sage of tea. The third, "Taoism and Zennism," shows that the sources of the ideal of Teaism are Taoism, which resigns itself to this world of sorrow and yet tries to find beauty in it, and Zen, which sees greatness in the smallest incidents of life. The fourth, "The Tea-Room," speaks of the simplicity and purity of a tea-room that is no more than a thatched hut, and of the symbolism enclosed in it; and the fifth, "Art Appreciation," sets out the secret understanding and the value of suggestion that pass between master and beholder, while criticizing the shallow enthusiasm of the present day. The sixth, "Flowers," laments the waste of flowers in the West and follows the minds of the masters of flower arrangement. And the last chapter, "Tea-Masters," draws the last tea of Sen no Rikyū, who was ordered to die by Hideyoshi. With Rikyū shattering his favorite bowl, leaving a death poem and taking his own life, the book closes.

The opening (original text)

Tea began as a medicine and grew into a beverage. In China, in the eighth century, it entered the realm of poetry as one of the polite amusements. The fifteenth century saw Japan ennoble it into a religion of aestheticism — Teaism. Teaism is a cult founded on the adoration of the beautiful among the sordid facts of everyday existence. It inculcates purity and harmony, the mystery of mutual charity, the romanticism of the social order. It is essentially a worship of the Imperfect.

Cha no hon by Okakura Tenshin (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The Philosophy of Tea is not mere aestheticism in the ordinary acceptance of the term, for it expresses conjointly with ethics and religion our whole point of view about man and nature. It is hygiene, for it enforces cleanliness; it is economics, for it shows comfort in simplicity rather than in the complex and costly.

Cha no hon by Okakura Tenshin (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Tea began as a medicine and grew into a beverage.

The opening line of this celebrated work, in which Okakura Tenshin explained the Eastern aesthetic sensibility to the world in English. In a single sentence it traces the book's arc from medicine to beverage to, eventually, an ‘aesthetic religion.’

Teaism is essentially a worship of the Imperfect.

A line from the first chapter. In a single sentence it distills the spirit of the tea ceremony — that beauty is found precisely in what is imperfect — carrying the Eastern aesthetic sensibility to readers around the world.
