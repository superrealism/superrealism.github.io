About this work

A novella published in two parts in Kaizō in October 1934 and January 1935, subtitled "In Order to Live." The title lays a mountain over that of his central work The People Who Live on the Sea, and the setting is the railway construction site in the Tenryū gorge where Hayama himself worked. Ōyama, a navvy whose head runs hot over demands for repayment; the fuss among the young men over Omutsu, the woman who cooks at the camp; and the small man Hayashida, who powders his face and pipes the Tokyo Ondo — after the life of the shanty quarter at the bottom of the valley has been built up, the mountain directly above the Midoriya inn begins to slide. A record of daily life by a writer of labour at sea, moved bodily to the mountains.

Summary

A railway construction site cutting through the gorge of the Tenryū River. A postcard reaches Ōyama, a laborer, from his aunt, saying his elder brother has died in Korea. The dream of unknown origin that had come to him a dozen-odd years before, when he lost two children, rises again to haunt him. In this valley bottom, dark above and white with nothing but bare soil, light seems to crawl upward from below, and smoke seems to crawl downward from above. Amid this reality where everything is turned upside down, Ōyama grows hot-headed over dunning letters demanding repayment of his debts, talks money with his wife, and turns loose his eight-year-old son, who cannot manage the steep mile-and-a-half climb, to fend for himself on the mountain. Life in the workers' quarters is a noisy business. A contest of favors breaks out among the young men over Omutsu, a cook who has come from Tokyo, and Hayashida, a small man soon due for his conscription physical, dons Omutsu's face powder and kimono and pipes out a Tokyo Ondo tune to liven up the room. There is friction with the local farmers over an irrigation channel and a latrine post, and once typhoid season comes, smoke rises twice a day from the crematorium across the river. One day, at the dam construction, a large stone gives way, and two men, locked in each other's arms, fall into the spillway. One claws his way up out of the whirlpool; the other is swept away — past Hamamatsu lies the Pacific Ocean. And then June comes. Directly above the inn called the Midoriya, in a rock face cut away to a height of forty or fifty shaku, an onion-like pattern of fault lines is found running through the stone. Sand begins to fall, pattering down, and Kobayashi, the head of the workers' quarters, cries out: 'The mountain's coming down!' Ōyama leaps down a slope too steep even for a goat to descend, and drives the people of the Midoriya out into the open. As everyone looks up from beneath a camellia tree, the mountain gives a low, rumbling roar and slides down.

Characters

  • Ōyama　A laborer working at the railway construction site on the Tenryū River. Burdened by debt and by his past, he holds the post of blast-signal man
  • Kobayashi　The head of the workers' quarters. Sees the mountain collapse coming before anyone else, and cries out, "The mountain's coming down!"
  • Omutsu　A cook who has come from Tokyo. Dislikes staying under any one roof, and stirs up the young men of the workers' quarters
  • Hayashida　A small man come of age for his conscription physical. Dons face powder and pipes out Tokyo Ondo tunes; on friendly terms with the Korean laborers too
  • The mistress of the Midoriya　The proprietress of the inn, who has lived directly beneath the mountain for forty years. Comes to lodge complaints every time there is a rockfall or a water outage

The opening (original text)

What a thing this was. Ōyama had been waiting for his own elder brother's death. This he now understood clearly, a dozen-odd years later. It was while Ōyama was working at the power plant on the Ochiai River, a tributary of the Kiso River, that he had received word that his two children had died. And now, a dozen-odd years later, at a railway construction site along the banks of the Tenryū, separated by mountains like Kiso-Komagatake and Ena-san, he learned — this time through a letter from his aunt — that his elder brother had died in Korea.

The ending (original text)

The sun moved slowly on toward the peaks of the Central Alps. The day was still high. And yet a strange feeling took hold of the people — a sense of relief, as though some great task had at last been completed, mixed with a hollow, spiritual exhaustion. Those who had gone downstream to search for the body, over by the dam, had not yet come back. If the river boats went up, and rain fell, and the sleeping Tenryū woke and called the clouds and brought down the rain and set the stones on the riverbed rolling, then the search for the body would become not merely difficult, but nearly impossible.
