About this work

A flat, unhurried account of the life of Major Ishida Kosuke, a staff officer posted to Kokura, from his arrival to the end of summer. A single chicken he is given brings to light the fact that his groom has been calmly mixing his master's rice and miso and pickles with his own and using them; but Ishida does not reproach him, and hands over the utensils and the chickens together. Ōgai's own years in Kokura lie behind it, and with "Dokushin" and "Futari no Tomo" it is called one of the Kokura trilogy. The character of a hero who knows he is being cheated and is not moved by it rises out of a plain style and the life of the place.

Summary

On the twenty-fourth of June, Ishida Kosuke arrives at Kokura as a staff major. Settling into a rented house in Kajimachi, he begins a life as bare as a campaign, with the old servant Otoki, the groom Torakichi whom he has brought from Tokyo, and his orderly Shimamura. One day Asō, a former transport soldier he looked after at the front, brings him a fine cock. Saying that he does not mean to eat it, he keeps it alive, and buys a hen himself; but before long the groom has mixed in a hen of his own and keeps them all, and takes every egg they lay. The old servant tells him so in a lowered voice, but Ishida will not take it up: "When I want an egg I buy one and eat it." Then the chickens get into the neighbor's field, and a woman abuses him at length from the other side of the hedge, while Ishida listens in silence with a faint smile. When a friend informs him that the old servant has been carrying things out of the kitchen in a bundle, he quietly changes his maid. The new girl, Haru, tells him that the groom puts his own rice into his master's rice bin and measures it out as he pleases, and uses his master's miso and soy sauce and pickles and firewood and charcoal stove in exactly the same way. Ishida feels for the first time as though his eyes had been opened, and finds himself almost admiring a system of accounts so methodically developed. The next day he buys a new rice bin and pickling tub, and gives Torakichi the old vessels with their contents and the chickens and chicks as well, without so much as saying that he is dismissed. That evening, while he is watching the fireworks at Asahimachi, Haru comes to tell him that the groom is carrying the chickens away tied up. "Tell him we have no use for the chicks," says Ishida, and goes on watching the fireworks.

Characters

  • Ishida Kosuke　A staff major newly posted to Kokura. Plain and methodical, he is rarely moved to any feeling
  • Torakichi　The groom he has engaged from Tokyo, who mixes his master's things with his own and uses them
  • Otoki　The first maid he engages, an old woman
  • Haru　The maid of sixteen who comes afterward, whose brisk temper pleases Ishida
  • Shimamura　An orderly sent from the regiment
  • Usui　The old man who owns the house
  • Nakano　An infantry major on close terms with Ishida

The opening (original text)

It was on the twenty-fourth of June that Ishida Kosuke came to take up his post at Kokura as a staff major. He came ashore at three in the morning from the steamer that runs between Tokuyama and Moji. Knowing that troops in the provinces are very attentive in seeing men off and meeting them, Ishida was in what was then called ordinary dress uniform, with his decorations on. A senior captain on the staff had come out to the pier on behalf of his colleagues. The rain was coming down hard. From there it is less than an hour by train to Kokura. They had rice cooked for them at a house called Kawau and ate. After daybreak the captain ran about seeing to tickets and to the luggage. From the train window one could see small houses packed close together along the top of a cliff. Every one of them had its doors thrown open, and the women and children were all but naked. In some of them the people were just at their breakfast. They were the houses of laborers doing work like stevedoring, the officer explained to him. They came out among the paddies. The planting of the rice was already done. Now and then one saw a farmer in a straw cape walking along the path between the fields with a basket over his shoulder.

The ending (original text)

Torakichi listened to what Ishida said with an air of astonishment, and when Ishida had finished he seemed about to say something. Ishida did not let him speak, but said: "No. You may have your own arrangements to make, but I have settled it so, and I need not hear what you have to say." Ishida got up at once and went into the inner room. Torakichi asked Haru to go and find out whether the master had dismissed him or not. Ishida laughed and said, "Tell him I said no such thing." That evening, because it was the waiting for the moon of the twenty-sixth night, there were fireworks at Asahimachi. Ishida stood on the veranda at the front of the house watching the fireworks break above the dusky flowers of the crape myrtle. Haru came out to him and said: "The groom is carrying the chickens away tied up now. He asks whether he should leave the chicks. Shall I tell him to leave them?" "Tell him we have no use for the chicks." And Ishida went on watching the fireworks.
