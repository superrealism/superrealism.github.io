Famous passages by Dazai Osamu

Well-known lines from the works of Dazai Osamu.

51 passages

Ha

I had been thinking of dying. This past New Year, someone gave me a bolt of cloth for a kimono. As a New Year's gift. The cloth was linen, woven with fine gray stripes. It must be a kimono for summer. I thought I would live until summer.

The opening of this early work made of linked fragments. A heavy first line about thoughts of death turns, at the trivial prompting of a gift of summer cloth, into “I thought I would live until summer” — Dazai's style in miniature.

The ecstasy and the anxiety of being chosen— both are in me.

A line of Verlaine set at the head of the opening story in Dazai's first collection, The Final Years. It is known as a phrase symbolizing the self-consciousness of Dazai's writing, which holds both the rapture and the anxiety of the chosen.

Dōke no hana

“Through here, the city of sorrow.”

The opening. A phrase based on the inscription on the gate of Hell in Dante's Divine Comedy, it leads the reader through the gate of the city of sorrow into the story of a young man who survived a failed double suicide.

Ah, why am I in such a hurry to pass judgment on everything? Who on earth taught me this mean spirit that cannot go on living without tying every thought up into something neat?

A passage in which the author himself steps into the middle of the story. The voice interrogating his own haste to reach conclusions breaks through the frame of the novel to reach the reader — a sign of the experimental methods of the early Dazai.

One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji

Mount Fuji is well suited by the evening primrose.

A line recalling a single flower seen from a bus bound for the Misaka Pass. Setting a tiny evening primrose against the vastness of Mount Fuji, and taking the side of that small, brave life, it is one of Dazai's finest passages.

Fuji really is great after all, I thought. It's doing a fine job, I thought.

A passage as well known as the scene with the evening primrose. Watching Fuji stand silently beyond the window of his inn, the narrator is ashamed of his own shifting loves and hates, and frankly acknowledges Fuji's greatness.

Ōgon fūkei

I stood there and wept. The harsh agitation melted away in my tears, almost pleasantly. I have lost. This is a good thing. It must be so. Their victory will also give light to my setting out tomorrow.

The close of the story: the monologue of the narrator, who overhears O-Kei, the maid he bullied as a child, still speaking of him fondly and with pride. In his words accepting defeat as “a good thing” lies a prayer for renewal.

Schoolgirl

The feeling, in the morning, when I first open my eyes, is a curious one.

The opening of a work that records a girl's day in the first person. In a light tone, as if speaking directly to the reader, it moves on into a wavering interior monologue.

Happiness comes one night too late.

Words that come to mind for the girl as she collapses into bed at the end of her day. A sense that happiness always arrives one step behind hangs faintly over the whole work.

Tomorrow, too, the same kind of day will come. Happiness will never come in my whole life. I know that. But it's best, I suppose, to go to sleep believing that it will surely come, that tomorrow it will come.

The monologue of the girl as she gets into bed at the end of her day. Knowing happiness will not come, she still tries to fall asleep believing in tomorrow—a fragile yet brave heart.

Good night. I am a Cinderella with no prince. Do you know where in Tokyo I am? We shall never meet again.

The ending. The girl's monologue of a single day, addressed to the reader throughout, closes with a self-portrait as a Cinderella without a prince and a light-footed farewell.

I like taking off my glasses and looking into the distance. Everything goes hazy, like a dream, like a peep-show picture — wonderful. I can't see anything dirty at all. Only the big things, only vivid, strong colors and light come into my eyes.

The monologue of the girl as she sits at her dressing table in the morning. Her feeling that the world blurred without her glasses is “wonderful” holds all the charm of this work's fresh, lively narration.

Chikukendan

When it comes to dogs, I have confidence. Confidence that, someday, without fail, one will bite me.

The opening of an essay-like story that discusses a dislike of dogs in dead earnest. Dazai's humor is distilled into the way he turns the word “confidence” inside out.

Heed My Plea

I have something to tell you. I have something to tell you, my lord. That man is cruel. Cruel. Yes. A hateful man. A wicked man. Ah. I cannot bear it. He must not be let to live.

An opening that pushes forward entirely through Judas's own breathless, first-person accusation. The rapid-fire short sentences give the very sound of love flipped inside out into hatred.

My name is Judas, the merchant. Heh heh. Judas Iscariot.

The closing, in which the narrator reveals his own name at the end of his monologue. Only now is the reader told whose confession this has been all along.

Run, Melos!

Melos was enraged. He resolved that the wicked, tyrannical king must be done away with, without fail.

The opening line that sets the story in motion. In a single breath it establishes Melos as a man who acts before he reasons, a character who runs before he thinks.

The brave man blushed deep red.

The last line, in which, having run the whole way, he is handed a cloak by a girl and realizes he is naked. It brings the heroic tale down to earth with human comedy.

That is why I run. I run because I am trusted. Whether I make it in time or not is not the question. A man's life is not the question either. I am running, somehow, for something far more terrible and great.

Melos's answer to Philostratus, who urges him to stop because he can no longer make it in time. The heart of the story is spoken here: running not merely to keep a promise, but for trust itself.

Faith, truly, was never an empty delusion.

The king's words on seeing Melos, who returned as he promised, and Selinuntius, who waited trusting him. The story's culmination, in which a tyrant who could trust no one has a change of heart.

I must repay their trust. Now there is only that one thing. Run! Melos.

The scene in which Melos, having nearly given up, drinks from a spring and rises again. This single word of self-exhortation became the very title of the work.

With the mouth, a man can say anything, however pure. But I cannot help seeing straight through to the very bottom of a man's bowels.

What the tyrant King Dionys flings at the captured Melos. The suspicion of a king who can no longer believe in people stands directly against the faithfulness that the story puts to the test.

“Selinuntius,” said Melos, tears in his eyes. “Strike me. Strike my cheek with all your strength. Once, on the way, I had a bad dream. If you do not strike me, I am not worthy even to embrace you. Strike me.” Selinuntius nodded as though he understood everything, and struck Melos on the right cheek so loudly that it rang through the whole place of execution. Then, smiling gently, he said, “Melos, strike me. Strike my cheek just as loudly. Once in these three days — only once — I doubted you for a moment. For the first time in my life, I doubted you. Unless you strike me, I cannot embrace you.”

The scene in which the two friends, reunited at the place of execution, have each other strike them on the cheek. Their integrity in refusing to forgive themselves for doubting the other even once forms the climax of this story about faithfulness.

Kirigirisu

I am leaving you. You have told nothing but lies. Perhaps there is something wrong with me, too. But I don't know what it is about me that is wrong.

The opening of a wife's letter of farewell to her painter husband, who turns worldly as he becomes successful. It is one of the representative works in which Dazai writes entirely in a woman's voice, drawing her mind through her monologue alone.

Seigi to bishō

Do justice with a smile!

The motto Serikawa Susumu sets down as he begins his diary. Around this single phrase the story vividly shows both a boy's earnest ambition and the shyness with which he immediately makes fun of it himself.

Udaijin sanetomo

Could brightness be the very form of ruin? So long as they are still dark, neither men nor houses have yet perished.

Words the shogun Sanetomo murmured, as if to himself, after hearing the Tale of the Heike chanted to the biwa, as recalled by the former attendant who narrates the story. In the novel Sanetomo's words are set apart in katakana, and this line leaves the image of a man who foresees ruin yet never loses his brightness.

Return to Tsugaru

I have practiced no vanity. I have not deceived my reader. Farewell, then, reader — if I am still alive, we shall meet again some other day. Let us go on in good spirits. Do not despair. And with that, so long.

The closing of this travel account through his native region. These few lines, addressing the reader directly in farewell, leave behind the brightest aftertaste of any of Dazai's works.

“Tell me, why are you going on a journey?” “Because I'm suffering.”

The opening of the main narrative, “Pilgrimage.” In this light exchange — answering “Because I'm suffering” when asked why he travels — the author's unease and self-conscious humor show through as he sets out on a journey around his native region.

Otogizōshi

It is what one might call the tragicomedy of character. Beneath human life, this problem is always flowing.

The close of “The Wen,” one of the folk tales retold in this sequence. The author's answer to the question of why misfortune occurs in a story without a single villain.

Pandora no hako

“I know nothing at all. But it seems the sun shines in the direction I grow.”

The ending. Saying that how one should live is best asked of a plant's vine, the work sets these words as the vine's answer—a line of faint hope addressed to postwar youth.

Even when thrown into the depths of misfortune and rolling about there, a person somehow gropes his way to a single thread of hope.

A passage from the letters written to a friend from the Health Dōjō, a tuberculosis sanatorium, just after the defeat. Likening it to the hope left at the bottom of Pandora's box, it speaks brightly of the human strength to find a thread of hope even at rock bottom.

Tokatonton

From the barracks behind me came the faint sound of someone driving nails with a hammer: tokatonton.

The sound of hammering that reaches the ears of a young man resolved to die, on the day of Japan's surrender. From then on, each time he hears it, all his passion drains away into blankness—a scene symbolizing postwar listlessness.

Villon's Wife

“It's all right if we're not even human. All we have to do is go on living.”

Sachiko's final words to her dissolute husband. Taking on the full weight of society's morality and still choosing to live regardless, it marks a high point of Dazai's later work.

The Setting Sun

Human beings are born for love and for revolution.

Kazuko's declaration that we are born for love and revolution. It distills the passion of a woman living within a declining aristocratic household, and stands at the very heart of the novel.

But we intend to fight the old morality to the very end, and to live like the sun.

The closing of Kazuko's letter to Uehara. Her resolve to bear a child out of wedlock is declared, flatly, as a war against the old morality.

At breakfast in the dining room, Mother took a spoonful of soup, sipped it lightly, and— “Oh,” she gave a faint cry.

The opening. From an elegant gesture of sipping soup and a small cry arise the refinement of an aristocratic mother in decline and the uneasy premonition that envelops the whole story.

I am an aristocrat.

The close of the suicide note left by Kazuko's younger brother, Naoji. A piercing final line in which the young man, who tried and failed to live among the common people, at last declares his origins.

Is there any human being who isn't a delinquent?

A line from Naoji's notebook, the “Yūgao Diary.” A cry from his heart, mixing rebellion against a world that pretends to decency with a defiant acceptance of his own decadence.

Just because you have a title doesn't make you an aristocrat. There are people with no title at all who have a nobility bestowed by heaven and are true aristocrats, and there are people like us, who have the title and nothing more — far from being aristocrats, we're closer to outcasts.

Words that Naoji once said to his sister Kazuko, recalled near the opening. By separating rank from true refinement, the remark sets off their mother as the last real aristocrat of a declining house.

When I pretended to be precocious, people whispered that I was precocious. When I pretended to be a lazybones, people whispered that I was a lazybones. When I pretended I couldn't write a novel, people whispered that I couldn't write. When I pretended to be a liar, people whispered that I was a liar. When I pretended to be rich, people whispered that I was rich. When I put on a show of coldness, people whispered that I was a cold fish. But when I was truly suffering and groaned in spite of myself, people whispered that I was putting on a show of suffering.

A passage from “The Moonflower Diary” that Naoji leaves behind. Only the roles he performs are believed, and only his real suffering is taken for an act: the loneliness of a man who plays the clown is drawn through relentless repetition.

Nyozegamon

Let me say it in a word: you are wholly lacking in the capacity to love, to the same degree that you lack the capacity to suffer. You may caress, but you do not love.

A passage from the fierce rebuttal aimed at Shiga Naoya and other established masters, written right up until Dazai's death. Linking the capacity to suffer with the capacity to love, it states plainly what Dazai demanded of literature.

Cherries

I want to believe that parents matter more than children.

The opening of a story published in the year of his death. In softening a flat statement into “I want to believe,” rather than asserting it outright, lies a self-concealment typical of Dazai, exposing the weakness of a father overwhelmed by his family.

Living is a terrible business. Chains are wound around you from every side, and if you move even a little, blood spurts out.

A monologue of the narrator, cornered by the weight of family life. Echoing the opening line, “I want to believe that parents matter more than children,” it states the hardship of living nakedly through the images of chains and blood.

No Longer Human

Mine has been a life of much shame.

The opening line of the protagonist Ōba Yōzō's notebooks. This single sentence sets the tone of self-negation that runs through the entire confession that follows.

Now I have neither happiness nor unhappiness. Everything simply passes by.

A line placed at the close of the notebooks. A resignation that happiness and unhappiness alike simply flow away is shown as the endpoint of Yōzō's long confession.

I ask God: is non-resistance itself a sin?

A question posed by Yōzō after he is committed to a mental hospital. In a single line, he lays his whole life of drifting without resistance before God.

What I came up with, then, was clowning. It was my last courtship of human beings.

The way of getting by that Yōzō, unable to understand human life and afraid of it, devised as a child. The starting point of the protagonist's way of living: holding on to others by playing the clown.

Disqualified as a human being. I had now ceased completely to be human.

The verdict Yōzō passes on himself after being committed to an asylum. The passage in which the title itself appears; from here the notebooks move toward their quiet end.

What, after all, is this thing called “society”? Is it the plural of human beings? Where is the substance of this thing called society?

The doubt Yōzō harbors when his friend Horiki tells him that “society won't allow it.” It leads to the thought, “Isn't society just you?”—a view that finds the true face of society in the individual.

Cowards are afraid even of happiness. They can hurt themselves on cotton wool. They can even be wounded by happiness.

The mind of Yōzō as he tries to flee happiness with a woman who loves him. The metaphor of being hurt by cotton wool expresses a timidity that cannot bear even happiness.

“The Yō-chan we knew was so open and thoughtful, and if only he hadn't drunk—no, even when he drank . . . he was a good boy, just like an angel.”

The final line of the work. Spoken by the bar madam who was entrusted with the notebooks as she looks back on Yōzō, it ends by gently redeeming, through another's eyes, a man who judged himself disqualified as a human being.

Ah, human beings understand nothing of one another; they see each other completely wrongly, yet think of each other as the dearest of friends, never notice it their whole lives long, and when the other dies, weep and read eulogies over him — isn't that so?

Words that escape Yōzō as he looks back on his relationship with Horiki. In this picture of people who live out their lives misunderstanding each other while believing themselves the closest of friends lies Yōzō's despair at being unable to reach others.
