About this work

Kitamura Tōkoku's central essay, arguing for the reality of the inner life that dwells within a person. In the mysterious response by which the human heart is called up before changing nature, he finds an exchange with a spirit of the universe beyond human understanding. It states the core of Tōkoku's thought, which sought to lead literature toward the truth of the inner life, and left a large mark on the intellectual history of modern Japan.

Summary

Man is by no means a withered, desiccated thing, nor is the universe something dry and tasteless — so Tōkoku begins. Before the same unchanging nature (zōka, the workings of creation), the hearts of men differ endlessly, one from another, and according to the state of the heart, the very aspect of nature itself changes. Nature governs man, but the free spirit within man, too, refuses simply to submit to nature in silence. And yet nature, too, is a manifestation of the spirit of the universe, an outward form of the shape of God, and there can be no doubt that within it lies hidden a beauty of the highest, purest order. That the human heart, touching it, should of itself feel awe, and give rise of itself to a spiritual experience, is surely no strange thing. Tōkoku grasps this as the working of an "inner life" operating beyond human wisdom and reasoning. The mysterious sympathetic response that poets and religious men feel in the depths of nature and of life, he holds, is neither given from without nor consciously produced from within, but is nothing other than the workings of a communion with the great spirit of the universe, by which the "inner life" is awakened, and remade anew. Seen through the eyes of that reborn life, the ultimate beauty and meaning of all things stands revealed. Seeking true reality in the inner depths of man, this essay marks the culmination of Tōkoku's thought, and stands as a landmark work that brought spiritual depth to modern Japanese literature.

The opening (original text)

Man is by no means a withered, desiccated thing. The universe is by no means a tasteless thing. Even a single flower, examined closely, holds within it thoughts as old as the ages. Nature (zōka) is eternal and unchanging, and yet the human heart that faces it differs endlessly, one from another. Nature is unchanging; and yet, because the human heart that faces it differs, nature too changes its aspect accordingly. The nature seen by the Buddhist pessimist-poet is, in every part, transient and pessimistic. The nature seen by the Christian optimist-poet is, in every part, hopeful and optimistic. It is not my present purpose here to judge the one false and the other true. But if it is indeed the human heart that makes of nature — unchanging as it is — a thing so variable as this, then how could we possibly be careless in studying that human heart? Nature governs man; and yet man, too, governs nature. The free spirit dwelling within man will not consent to submit to nature in silence. Great as nature's authority is, man's freedom, too, is great. How could man ever be satisfied merely by submitting himself wholly to nature? And yet nature, too, is one expression of the spirit of the universe, a manifestation of the shape of God, and it is beyond doubt a fact that within it is gathered a beauty of the highest and purest order; that the human heart, before this, should of itself give rise to a feeling of awe, and of itself produce a spiritual experience — how could this be anything but fitting? On this point, even I myself am not entirely without some feeling for the universal, the all-embracing.

On Inner Life by Kitamura Tōkoku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This sympathetic response is that which remakes the inner life of man; this sympathetic response is that which remakes man's inner experience and inner self-awareness. Through this response, for a fleeting moment, the eye of man departs from the sensual world; it is this very thing that we mean when we say we leave the flesh behind, and forget the real. And yet it is not like the somnambulist who forgets the "self" and walks forth from it; it is, in every respect, still with the eye of life that one beholds what lies beyond nature — with the eye of life, remade anew. When one looks with the eye of life thus remade, is there anything at all, among the ten thousand things of creation, that lacks its own ultimate essence? And yet that ultimate essence is not some absolute idea; it is that which has taken on concrete form in some particular thing — this is its ultimate essence. To the eye that sees the universal, that ultimate essence is seen within the whole of creation; to the eye that sees the psychological, that ultimate essence is seen upon the human heart.

On Inner Life by Kitamura Tōkoku (Aozora Bunko)
