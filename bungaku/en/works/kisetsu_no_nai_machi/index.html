About this work

A linked sequence serialized in the evening edition of the Asahi Shimbun in 1962. It draws as a group portrait the comedy and grief of the people living in one poor patch at the edge of a city — the place they call simply the town. A boy who goes on driving a streetcar that does not exist, people clinging to dreams that will not be answered, others passing their days between resignation and laughter. In a town fixed to no time or place, it gathers up the absurdity and the sorrow that people keep in the middle of poverty and disappointment. Known also as the source of Kurosawa Akira's film Dodes'ka-den, and one of Yamamoto's central late works.

Summary

On the outskirts of a city there is a poor quarter called "the street." One streetcar line runs there; but it has no rails and no overhead wires and no car either, and a boy named Roku-chan, the only man on the crew, drives the car alone by gestures and nothing else. To Roku-chan, who lives on sufferance with his mother O-Kuni at their fried-vegetable shop, driving that imaginary car faithfully every day is the whole of the world. The story has no one hero: chapter by chapter it draws the people who live on this street. A woman left by her husband, bringing up her child alone; a man who raises his hand to his family whenever he drinks; someone who goes on hanging out his shop curtain for customers who never come; a father and his sick child, living on next to nothing. In their poverty and disappointment they are by turns pitiful and comical, and still they get through their days as hard as they can. "There is here no limit of time and no limit of place," the author writes. In every age, whatever the period or the country or the state of society, there are people living the same sorrows and the same resignations just beside the reader — and it is as an appeal to that, and with a boundless affection for every last person on the street, that the book is written.

Characters

  • Roku-chan　The boy who goes on driving an imaginary streetcar all by himself. He lives with his mother O-Kuni
  • O-Kuni　Roku-chan's mother. She keeps a fried-vegetable shop and worries endlessly over her son
  • Old Tanba, Shima-san and Hira-san　People who live on the street, passing their days each with his own disappointment and resignation
  • Katsuko and O-Tane　The women of the street, living as hard as they can in their poverty

The opening (original text)

There was one streetcar line for getting to that "street." There were several other roads as well, but only the one streetcar ran there; and since it had no rails and no overhead wire, and no car body either, and had only one man on the crew, a driver, there was no way for a passenger to ride on it. In short, apart from the driver, a boy named Roku-chan, and a few pieces of equipment, that streetcar was, objectively speaking, entirely imaginary.

Kisetsu no nai machi by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

— While setting these notebooks down again as a novel, I felt a boundless affection and longing for every one of the people in them. These people once lived close beside me, and their laughter and their lamenting and their anger and the sound of their weeping have now come back to me again. Without distorting it, as far as I could I have copied it down as it was. And I want to say too that although these people belong to the past, there are people even now, close beside the reader, passing their days in the same disappointment and despair, the same sorrow and resignation.

Kisetsu no nai machi by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
