About this work

A recollective work in which Akutagawa tried to draw himself and those around him in his university days exactly as they were. The days of his youth with the friends who founded the coterie magazine Shinshichō — Matsuoka Yuzuru, Kume Masao, Kikuchi Kan and Naruse Seiichi — are set down vividly.

Summary

"What follows may not be the kind of thing called a novel" — Akutagawa, prefacing his piece with these words, sets down himself and those around him from four or five years before, as far as possible without reservation, just as it was. The setting is Tokyo Imperial University. With the launch of the coterie magazine Shinshichō ahead of him, Akutagawa spent his days talking of literature with friends such as Naruse, Matsuoka, Kume and Kikuchi, visiting one another's lodgings, and groaning over manuscripts. On a certain day with a cold wind blowing, Akutagawa calls on Matsuoka, who has stayed up all night writing a play, at his lodgings. Seeing Matsuoka, fast asleep, with tears welling among his eyelashes, an unbearable, helpless feeling rises in Akutagawa's breast — how hard he must have suffered to come to this — and before he knows it, he too finds himself close to tears. The single-minded, and somehow both comical and poignant, youth of these young men of letters is drawn in a warm hand. A fine piece of Akutagawa's reminiscent prose.

The opening (original text)

What follows may not be the kind of thing called a novel. And yet, what it ought to be called instead, I confess I do not know myself. I have only tried to write out, as far as possible without reservation, myself and those around me from four or five years ago, just as it was. One A fine morning in November. Wearing my stiff school uniform for the first time in a while, I went to school, and met Naruse, likewise in uniform, in front of the main gate. When I said, "Hey," he said "Hey" back.

Ano koro no jibun no koto by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Just then, happening to look at Matsuoka's face, I saw that even in his sleep, his eyelashes were brimming with tears. No — now that I looked again, there were the traces of tears down his cheeks as well. (...) "What a fool you are. Don't take on work so painful that you cry in your sleep over it" — in the midst of that helpless feeling, I wanted to scold Matsuoka like this. But behind that wish to scold him, I wanted, all the same, to praise him quietly to myself: how hard he must have suffered to come to this. And thinking that, before I knew it, I too was close to tears.
