About this work

Murō Saisei's third collection, published by Kanjō Shisha in 1918 and, with The Poems of Love of the same year, representative of his early work. It gathers five years of poems written around the age of twenty, singing the fresh sentiment of boyhood and a longing for his native Kanazawa and the Sai river. Kitahara Hakushū and Hagiwara Sakutarō contributed prefaces. One passage in particular, from "Small Scene, Strange Feeling" — that home is a place to think of from far away, and to sing of sadly — has been loved as the poem of homesickness and long carried in school readers.

Summary

A collection of the lyric poems Murō Saisei wrote over five years around the age of twenty. The lovesick, half-mad feeling of boyhood in Kanazawa in the north country, memories of a home town deep in snow, scenes along the Saigawa, and the days of dissipation and remorse in Tokyo ("The Dark Age") are all sung in clear, clean rhythms. Hagiwara Sakutarō called them "poems like beautiful shells with a transparent blue in them," and their delicate cadence brought a new sharpness to the Japanese lyric. The oldest poem in the book, "Small Scenes, Strange Feelings," holds the lines that made Saisei's name imperishable: one's home town is a thing to think of from far away, and to sing of with sorrow; even should one fall into beggary in a strange land, it is not a place to go back to. It is a monument of the modern lyric, in which the guileless heart of boyhood and the shadow of youth cross each other.

Famous passages

One's hometown is a thing to long for from afar, a thing to sing of with sorrow, and even should I become a beggar, fallen low in a foreign land, it is nowhere I should think to return.

The opening of the second of the “Small Landscapes, Strange Feelings” poems. Naming the love and resentment of one who has left home, it is among the most widely recited passages in modern Japanese poetry.
