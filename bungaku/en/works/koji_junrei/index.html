About this work

A travel essay setting down the impressions of the philosopher Watsuji Tetsurō when he visited the old temples around Nara with friends in May 1918. First published in 1919 and later revised by the author. He looks at the sculpture and temples of the Asuka, Hakuhō and Tenpyō periods, the Hōryūji murals and the pensive bodhisattva of Chūgūji with young enthusiasm and a fresh sensibility, and extends his thinking as far as their exchanges with Indian, Greek and Persian art. A celebrated book that brought cultural history and comparative art into the appreciation of old art, and drew many people to the buddhas of Nara.

Summary

The author sets out with a few friends to visit the old temples and old art of Nara. He begins from his thoughts on the coloring and the realism of the copies of the Ajanta murals he saw on the eve of departure, and his reflections widen to the relation between Indian and Greek art and to the question of religious painting and the senses. Once in Nara, he goes with a deep gaze over the architecture and the images of the Hōryūji, the wall paintings of the Golden Hall, the Yakushiji and the Tōdaiji, the Tōshōdaiji, the eleven-headed Kannon at the Shōrinji, and the Kannon in the half-lotus posture at the Chūgūji. In the Chūgūji image above all he finds an incarnation of compassion that is neither mother nor virgin, and argues that the starting point of Japanese culture, whose core is the pathos of things and a quiet tenderness, is bound up with the gentle nature of these islands. The nuns he meets on the road and the peaceful country scenes are drawn with a clean touch, and the book closes as a travel record in which emotion before old art and reflection on the history of culture have become one.

The opening (original text)

Last night, in the short time before we set out, Z showed me copies of the Ajanta murals at his house. The pictures were larger than I had expected, and the colors far more beautiful than I had imagined from photographs. I looked at them in a hurry and no detailed impression remains; and yet, once I was on the train, I found my mind held by those pictures without a break. This morning at Kyoto station, after parting from T and from F, I sat alone in the dining car, and the thought of those pictures rose again strongly to the surface of my mind.

Koji junrei by Watsuji Tetsurō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

From the Chūgūji we went round to the Hōrinji. The peaceful country along the way, a pond where the water shield was in flower, the gentle scenery with its many small hills — all of it was very fine. The old pagoda of the Hōrinji and the large-eyed images were beautiful too. The air of the ruined precincts was interesting as well. Straw was stacked in the bell tower as though it were a barn, and in the shade of the trees behind the main hall a loom had been set out on a spread mat.
