What is Mukashibanashi (Folk Tales)?

Category
Form / Genre

An orally transmitted tale, beginning with the formula "Once upon a time, in a certain place," and closing with a set concluding phrase. It is distinguished from legends, which are believed as fact and tied to a particular place or object, and from myths, which recount the origins of the gods, in that it is told keeping to a fixed pattern of story without specifying a time or place. Yanagita Kunio, avoiding the loanword "minwa" ("folk tale," a translation from Western terms), used the term "mukashibanashi," and argued in "Momotarō no Tanjō" ("The Birth of Momotarō," 1933) and elsewhere that folk tales arose as myths broke apart and branched off. Later Seki Keigo, in "Nihon Mukashibanashi Taisei," organized tales from across the country by type, laying the foundation for comparative study. Momotarō, Kachikachi Yama, Saru Kani Gassen ("The Battle of the Monkey and the Crab"), Shitakiri Suzume ("The Tongue-Cut Sparrow"), and Hanasaka Jiji ("The Old Man Who Made the Flowers Bloom") are called the five great folk tales, and all had taken their present shape by the end of the Muromachi period or the start of the Edo period. Much of the prose familiar to us today is the work of modern retellings, and the sequence of retellings Kusuyama Masao wrote for children in 1921 stands as their representative example. Dazai Osamu's "Otogizōshi" ("Fairy Tales") likewise shows an attempt to borrow the skeleton of a folk tale to retell it as a story for adults.
