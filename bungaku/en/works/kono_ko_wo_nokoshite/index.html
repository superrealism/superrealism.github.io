About this work

The record Nagai Takashi, a physician exposed to the bomb at Nagasaki and given his own death sentence in leukaemia, wrote for the two small children he would leave behind. It begins with the cry — must he leave this child, and go from this world? — and sets down quietly, from his sickbed in the two-mat hut he called Nyokodō, the day he lost his wife to the bomb, his thoughts on science and faith, and his hopes for his children. One of the most widely read books of postwar Japan.

Summary

As he is dozing, Kayano, home from play, presses her cold cheek to his and says, "Ah... the smell of Father..." Because she has forgotten her mother's smell, she wants at least her father's — and Nagai is struck to the heart by the piteousness of that childish mind. "Leaving this child behind — must I soon go out of this world!" The fate he had felt coming ever since he chose radiology for his specialty was now becoming real. The atomic bomb took the life of his wife Midori, and he himself has little time left with leukemia. Behind him he will leave Makoto, a middle-school boy, and the little Kayano. Nagai pictures the child burying her face in the bedding and weeping on the day he is gone, and wishes that she might at least learn to fasten the buttons of her work trousers by herself. From there the scene of the day the bomb fell, the days of running about with the relief work, the gathering of his wife's bones, small exchanges with his children, his self-examination as a scientist, and his thoughts on war and peace pile up in the form of fragments. Truth and love, the meaning of work, how one is to accept death — since it would be no use telling them now, he writes it down for them to read later, and he has set it down just so. Outside the window of the Nyokodō, his two-mat hermitage, Makoto was carrying off pieces of roof tile, and Kayano was playing house alone with climbing-rose flowers.

Characters

The opening (original text)

I had been dozing when Kayano, back from play at some time I had not noticed, pressed her cold cheek against mine and, after a while, said, "Ah... the smell of Father..." Leaving this child behind — must I soon go out of this world! Because she has forgotten her mother's smell, she longs for her father's at least, and watches until I am asleep to come stealing up — the piteousness of that childish mind.

Kono ko o nokoshite by Nagai Takashi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

From where I sit I can see Makoto going off to throw away pieces of roof tile carried in a hod, and Kayano playing house by herself with climbing-rose flowers heaped in a fragment of Arita ware. When this brother and sister have grown up, how will they criticize the thoughts I have set down? In fifty years they will be far older than I am now, and perhaps the two of them will open this book together and talk it over, clicking their false teeth: Father's ideas were young, weren't they.
