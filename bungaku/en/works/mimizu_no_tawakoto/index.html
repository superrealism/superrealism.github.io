About this work

The record of six years in which Tokutomi Roka, made a popular writer by The Cuckoo, gave up his fame, moved to the village of Kasuya in Musashino, and worked his fields with a hoe in his own hands. The farm work of the four seasons, his dealings with the villagers, travel and reading, and the joy and the hardness of living by the soil are set down exactly and with a great deal of poetry. Called the gospel of the soil, it went through many printings and passed a hundred thousand copies: a central work of Roka's.

Summary

"My life in the village has now reached a full six years" — Roka is forty-five, and, looking in the mirror, finds more and more white in both his hair and his beard. Returning from a pilgrimage to Jerusalem and a visit to Tolstoy, Roka leaves Tokyo and settles in the village of Kasuya in Musashino (now Setagaya Ward), and, calling himself an "aesthetic farmer," actually takes up the hoe, sowing wheat, piling compost, and keeping chickens. This book is the record of that life. The transformation of the house, from the ramshackle hut he first moved into, through the addition of a bathroom, a detached study, and a connecting corridor. The spring plowing, the summer weeding, the autumn harvest, the winter raking of fallen leaves. Conversations with the farmer working the neighbouring field, the village policeman, peddlers, and the young literary men who come to visit. Thoughts of Tolstoy, a rift with his elder brother Sohō, memories of a dead friend, and a merciless self-examination of his own vanity and idleness. The shapes of life and death that come into view through contact with the soil pile up, entry by entry, in an unadorned hand. "Earthworm" — one who works silently within the soil, enriching it without ever standing out — was the name he gave himself, a man who had stepped down from the world of fame and reputation. After publication, the book went through printing after printing, passing a hundred thousand copies, and the author wrote: "So long as people remain bound to the chain of life — living on the earth, having life, having death, and having life again after death — The Mutterings of an Earthworm ought to be read."

The opening (original text)

My life in the village has now reached a full six years. By the calendar, I am forty-five, and, looking in the mirror, I am struck all over again at how much more white has come into my hair and the full growth of hair on my face. I was, to begin with, a slow, countrified fellow, but of late I find myself becoming more and more of a bumpkin. The other day, walking about Ueno, a rickshaw puller came up and asked if he might show me around. Whenever I go shopping around Ginza or Nihonbashi, I am treated as a country bumpkin, and it always makes me cross.

Mimizu no tawakoto by Tokutomi Roka (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And so, for me, they are children of my own vain attachment. It is only because there is The Mutterings of an Earthworm that there can be a "New Spring" at all. So long as people remain bound to the chain of life — living on the earth, having life, having death, and having life again after death — The Mutterings of an Earthworm ought to be read. November, Taishō 9. Recorded by the author.
