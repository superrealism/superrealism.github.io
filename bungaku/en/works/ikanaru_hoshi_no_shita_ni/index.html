About this work

Takami Jun's central work, set in Asakusa and serialized in Bungei in 1939 and 1940. The narrator, unable to settle to his work, rents a room in a flat in the amusement quarter and, carrying a feeling for Koyanagi Masako, a young revue dancer, mixes with all sorts of people who live in Asakusa. A parent and child from a ball-balancing troupe, revue writers and dancers, the bosses of the quarter — the air of a district in which brilliance and squalor are mixed is caught in a light, feeling hand. A fine work of urban manners, on the wavering of a heart that is neither love nor longing, and on nostalgia for an ordinary people's quarter that is disappearing.

Summary

Novelist "I" rents a six-mat workroom in an apartment in Asakusa's entertainment district, hoping the stimulation of the crowds will drive me to work, since left to myself I only drift into a daze. But in truth I sit at the desk without the manuscript ever advancing, find it a bother to go out or to go home, and sit stewing in misery in the chilly room. The reason I cannot tear myself away from Asakusa is that the revue dancer Koyanagi Masako lives in this town. My feelings for Masako, still only seventeen and utterly guileless, are too elusive to be called love outright, and even when my friends laugh at me for mooning over "a mere child," I cannot quite put it into words. I frequent the revue where Masako appears, call at the dressing room, and grow close to the dancers and writers there, working my way ever deeper into the lives of the people who live in Asakusa. The parent and child of a ball-spinning troupe, the district's local bosses, the women of the bars — the sorrows and small joys of ordinary people crowding behind the glittering stage are drawn out, one after another, through my own eyes. Rather than any dramatic turn of plot, it is the town itself, and the very air of the people living and breathing in it, that is the true protagonist here, and running through the whole work is a loneliness that drifts through the bustle of the entertainment district, and a fondness for all that is passing away. Before long the times tilt toward war, and the shadow of conscription and departure for the front creeps stealthily into the streets. Amid the blare of the Patriotic March trumpet, "I," watching this changing Asakusa, bring the story of this strange, dear town to its close.

The opening (original text)

— Beyond the window of my forlorn workroom on the third floor of the apartment building, the sky directly above the entertainment district hung dark and heavily overcast. Beneath a bare light bulb, strung up on a length of cord I had found lying about near the window, I had set my small work desk facing the window, and there, before that desk, I sat like a fool, doing nothing at all, simply staring blankly. There was nothing to be seen in the sky, and yet my own eyes, too, seemed to see nothing at all. But then, as I sat, something strange caught my eye — something like an odd bit of dust, flying across that sky, which looked clouded over with the dust rising ceaselessly from the entertainment district below. That dust formed itself into a cluster, jumbled together like some kind of mold, but as I watched, it stretched out long and thin, tracing the shape of a bent line. It was wild geese.

The ending (original text)

Asano stood in the doorway, looking down at Goro-chan with a thoroughly sour expression on his face. Just then, the sound of a trumpet playing the Patriotic March came drifting in. Not played especially well — which, if anything, only lent it a greater sense of solemnity — that familiar trumpet call conjured up the image of the townspeople's procession to the shrine, led by the men called up for service. No doubt that old fellow from the ball-spinning troupe was among their number too. Before long, I rose, unsteadily, to announce the beginning of this strange gathering.
