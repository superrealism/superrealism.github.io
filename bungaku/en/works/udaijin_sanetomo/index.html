About this work

A historical novel written to order and published in 1943. It gives the life of Minamoto no Sanetomo, third shogun of the Kamakura government and also a poet, as the recollections of an aged former attendant who served close to him. Each chapter opens with a passage from the historical record Azuma Kagami, and behind those terse entries there rises a Sanetomo who is clever, elegant and somehow above it all, holding a quality Dazai calls brightness. Deep affection for a young shogun who kept a clear heart in the middle of political struggle runs through the whole, and Dazai's idealist view of human beings, written under wartime, shows through. Regarded as his central historical work.

Summary

"Concerning the Minister of the Right of Kamakura, of whom you ask — then, what I saw and what I heard..." The narrator is an old man who once served close to the shogun Minamoto no Sanetomo, and who after Sanetomo's violent death took orders and withdrew into the mountains. As he is questioned, he begins to speak of the memories of the dead master he reveres beyond all measure. The Sanetomo whom he first saw when he entered the palace at the age of twelve was a boy of a strange brightness beyond the interests of this world — a boy who watched with an untroubled smile as an old retainer wept and collapsed over precious documents burned in a fire. Setting a passage of chronicle from the Azuma kagami at the head of each chapter, and against the background of Kamakura's bloody struggles for power, from the Wada rebellion onward, the story quietly draws, through the eyes of a personal attendant, a Sanetomo who lets his mind play in poetry, dreams of sailing to Sung China, and lives aloof from the calculations around him. Clever and elegant, but far removed from the pitilessness of politics, that young nobleman is at last struck down by his nephew Kugyō on the stone steps of the Tsurugaoka Hachiman shrine on the day of the ceremony for his appointment as Minister of the Right, and closes his life at twenty-eight. "The world was as if a light had suddenly been put out" — with that sentence, announcing the emptiness of a capital that has lost its shogun, the story ends. The finest of Dazai Osamu's historical fiction, written in a lofty archaic prose out of reverence for a young shogun who kept a clear heart in the very midst of political strife.

The opening (original text)

Concerning the Minister of the Right of Kamakura, of whom you ask — then I shall inform you of what I saw and what I heard, avoiding embellishment as far as I can and just as it was. I mean to be as careful as possible to make no mistake; but even so, if by any chance I should be wrong about a date, or should have forgotten someone's name, please laugh it off lightly as the poorness of a head worse than most men's, and overlook it.

Udaijin sanetomo by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

On the second day of the second month the officer Katō came riding to Rokuhara and reported that the Minister of the Right and Shogun had departed this life; and hearing it, high and low, men and women throughout the capital lost east from west in their grief. There was nothing to do but be astounded; in the capital too they heard it and were shaken; the world was as if a light had suddenly been put out. (Masukagami)

Famous passages

Could brightness be the very form of ruin? So long as they are still dark, neither men nor houses have yet perished.

Words the shogun Sanetomo murmured, as if to himself, after hearing the Tale of the Heike chanted to the biwa, as recalled by the former attendant who narrates the story. In the novel Sanetomo's words are set apart in katakana, and this line leaves the image of a man who foresees ruin yet never loses his brightness.
