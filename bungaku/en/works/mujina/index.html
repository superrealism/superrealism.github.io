About this work

A very short piece in Lafcadio Hearn's 1904 collection Kwaidan, about a faceless spectre. On the Kii-no-kuni slope in Akasaka in Tokyo, a man speaks to a woman crouched weeping in the dark — and collapses when she turns and shows him a smooth face with no features on it at all. He escapes to a soba stall and begs for help, and then — a two-stage punchline that terrifies the reader in a few pages, and the piece that fixed the nopperabō in the popular imagination.

Summary

In the days before street lamps and rickshaws, the Kii-no-kuni-zaka in Akasaka in Tokyo was deserted and lonely after dark, and no one would climb the slope alone late at night — because a mujina walked there. One evening an old merchant hurrying up the slope found a young woman crouched by the moat, weeping alone. Pitying her, well dressed as she was, he spoke to her and came near to comfort her; and the woman suddenly stroked her face downward — and on that face there were no eyes, no nose, no mouth, only a smooth surface like an egg. Terrified out of his wits, the merchant ran up the slope, fled on through the darkness, and at last made out the light of a soba seller far ahead. Rushing to the light as though to cling to it, and gasping out that a woman had shown him something dreadful, he heard the soba man say, "Was it anything like this that she showed you?" — and stroke his own face. And the soba seller's face too became like an egg, and at the same moment the light went out.

Characters

  • The old merchant　A merchant of the Kyōbashi quarter, who speaks to the weeping woman on the Kii-no-kuni-zaka at night and meets the faceless thing
  • The faceless woman　A young woman crouched weeping by the moat on the slope. When she strokes her face it becomes smooth, without eyes, nose or mouth
  • The soba seller　The night noodle seller from whom the fleeing merchant begs help — and who proves to be another faceless thing

The opening (original text)

On the Akasaka Road, in Tokyo, there is a slope called Kii-no-kuni-zaka — which means the Slope of the Province of Kii. I do not know why it is called the Slope of the Province of Kii. On one side of this slope you see an ancient moat, deep and very wide, with high green banks rising up to some place of gardens; and on the other side of the road extend the long and lofty walls of an imperial palace. Before the era of street lamps and jinrikishas, this neighborhood was very lonesome after dark; and belated pedestrians would go miles out of their way rather than mount the Kii-no-kuni-zaka, alone, after sunset. All because of a Mujina that used to walk there. The last man who saw the Mujina was an old merchant of the Kyōbashi quarter, who died about thirty years ago. This is the story, as he told it:

Famous passages

“Hé! Was it anything like THIS that she showed you?” cried the soba-man, stroking his own face — which therewith became like unto an Egg.... And, simultaneously, the light went out.

The close of the story: a double twist in which the merchant who has fled from a faceless woman is shown the same face by the soba seller he turns to for help. The brief ghost story ends in a sudden blackout.
