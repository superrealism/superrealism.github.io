About this work

A poor young man, following the Kannon's instruction, sets off holding carefully the single straw he grasped at the temple gate. The straw turns into a horsefly, a mandarin orange, a length of cloth and a horse — the Japanese folk tale known as the Straw Millionaire, retold by Kusuyama Masao.

Summary

In the province of Yamato there once lived a poor young man with no family and no work. In his helplessness he shut himself up in the hall of Hase Temple and prayed for twenty-one days that something be done for him, whether he was to live or die. An old man who appeared to him in a dream toward dawn told him that whatever he first laid his hand on when he left the temple, however worthless a thing it might be, he should treasure and keep with him. As the young man stepped over the great gate he stumbled, and what he grasped without thinking was a single straw. As he walked along the road, tying up with that straw a horsefly that kept buzzing about and pestering him, and carrying it fastened to a branch, a child riding by in an ox-carriage wanted it, and he was given three mandarin oranges in exchange. Those oranges, in turn, helped an attendant woman who had collapsed from the heat, and became three bolts of fine cloth; the cloth was exchanged for a fine horse that had collapsed by the roadside. When the young man prayed to Kannon, the horse breathed again, and near the capital that horse turned into three chō of rice paddies and rice, and even a house with someone to keep it for him while he was away. One of the representative folk tales of Japan, in which faith, honesty, and small kindnesses piled one upon another draw down good fortune. A retelling by Kusuyama Masao, known for a plain style that children can read straight out loud.

The opening (original text)

Once upon a time, in the province of Yamato, there lived a poor young man. He was all alone, with no parents, no wife, no children, and as yet no master to employ him either. The young man grew more and more helpless, until he thought there was nothing for it but to pray to Kannon, and shut himself up in the hall of a great temple called Hase Temple.

Ippon no wara by Kusuyama Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In time, the young man took a fine wife, and had many children and grandchildren. And so he came to lead a lively, happy life to the end of his days. A single straw, in the end, had drawn to him good fortune as great as this.

Ippon no wara by Kusuyama Masao (Aozora Bunko)
