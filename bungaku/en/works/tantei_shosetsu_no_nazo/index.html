About this work

A collection of essays in which Edogawa Ranpo, having read the detective fiction of every age and country, classifies and explains its tricks systematically. The locked room, the unexpected culprit, the faceless corpse, ciphers, the crime of probability — from "Locked Room Tricks," written for this book, he sets out the devices of the mystery with an enthusiast's heat. A book showing the true ground of Ranpo, at once a writer and one of the world's leading scholars of detective fiction.

Summary

"At the suggestion of the publishing division of the Society for Social Thought Research, I have gathered together, from among my own essays, those that explain the tricks of detective fiction" — so Ranpo explains in his preface, adding that since his separately written "Classified Compendium of Tricks" was laid out item by item and unsuited to the general reader, he has instead gathered the essays that broke that compendium down into readable form, and newly written a thirty-five-page piece, "Locked-Room Tricks," to round the book out. The essays collected here follow the skeleton of that "Compendium," which divides more than eight hundred tricks into nine major categories: tricks concerning the criminal's own person (playing two roles at once, the unexpected culprit), the crime scene and its traces (locked rooms, footprints, fingerprints), the timing of the crime, weapons and poisons, ways of concealing people and objects, and more. In "Locked-Room Tricks," Ranpo ranges widely over locked-room mysteries from Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" down to Carr and Chesterton, sorting them into types — mechanical devices, deceptions staged by the victims themselves, illusions of timing. In "The Faceless Corpse," "The Desire to Change One's Identity," and "Strange Motives for Crime," the structure of the mystery is discussed from the angle of criminal psychology, while "Meiji-Era Fingerprint Fiction" and "Early Forensic-Medicine Texts and Detective Fiction" excavate the earliest sproutings of detective fiction in modern Japan. Kindred essays such as "Magic and Detective Fiction" and "On the Thrill" are collected as well, and the table of contents of "A Classified Compendium of Tricks" is appended at the end for reference. At once a taxonomy of tricks and a book of ardent love for detective fiction as a literary form.

The opening (original text)

Preface — How This Book Came to Be At the suggestion of the publishing division of the Society for Social Thought Research, I have gathered together, from among my own essays, those that explain the tricks of detective fiction. On the subject of tricks I have separately written a piece called "A Classified Compendium of Tricks" (collected in the Hayakawa Shobō edition of Zoku Genei-jō), but that work is set out item by item for readers already well versed in detective fiction, and is unsuited to general reading; and so, in this book, I have added only its table of contents at the end for reference, and have not included its full contents.

Tantei shōsetsu no nazo by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"A Classified Compendium of Tricks" sets out and explains more than eight hundred tricks of every kind, divided among nine major categories, and below I set down, for the reader's reference, how those categories relate to the essays collected in this book. For this, please refer to the table of contents of "A Classified Compendium of Tricks" appended at the end.

Tantei shōsetsu no nazo by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)
