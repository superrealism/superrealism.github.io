About this work

The oldest Japanese diary written in kana, set down by Ki no Tsurayuki around 935. "Diaries are things written by men, I am told; I shall see whether a woman cannot do the same" — so the author begins, in the borrowed voice of a woman. His term as governor of Tosa over, Tsurayuki records the fifty-five days of the voyage from Tosa back to the capital: what he saw each day, the people aboard, the poems exchanged. Humour and elegance run through it, and beneath them the unspent grief for the small daughter he lost in Tosa. It opened the way for prose in kana and shaped the diaries, essays and tales that came after.

Summary

His term as governor of Tosa at an end, the author takes on the person of a woman and begins, in kana, a diary of the journey home to the capital. At the close of the fourth year of Jōhei the party leaves the provincial seat of Tosa and receives at the harbor the farewells and parting feasts of the people. The voyage will not go as they wish, at the mercy of the weather and of rumors of pirates, and they are held up for days at a time waiting for wind and tide as they draw slowly nearer the capital. On board, poems are exchanged, bad poems raise laughter, some grow drunk on wine, and the joys and griefs of the traveling party are drawn with a light hand. But under that cheerful brush there rises again and again a parent's grief for the small daughter he lost in the province where he served. After more than fifty days they enter the capital at last, and the house they see again after so long has fallen into ruin. Looking at the little pine that has grown in the garden, the author thinks of the daughter who was born in this house and died away from it, and laments in a poem: if only the one I knew could have been seen with the thousand years of the pine. Carrying more than he can set down, he closes the diary.

The opening (original text)

It is said that men keep things called diaries; and so, thinking to try it, a woman does the same. On the twenty-first day of the twelfth month of a certain year, at the hour of the dog, we set out; and I set down something of the circumstances here in brief. A certain person, his four or five years in a provincial post at an end and all the customary business done, having received his certificate of discharge and the rest, left the residence where he had lived and went over to the place where he was to board the ship. People of every sort, some he knew and some he did not, came to see him off. Those who had been much in his company over the years found it hard to part, and all that day they busied themselves this way and that with a great deal of noise, until the night grew late.

The ending (original text)

Even this, it seems, is not enough; and so, once more: "Had the one I knew been seen with the thousand years of this pine, would I have had to make so distant and sorrowful a parting?" There is much that I cannot forget and much that I regret, but I cannot set it all down. Be that as it may — I shall make an end of this at once.

Famous passages

Diaries, they say, are things that men write; but I mean to see whether a woman cannot write one too.

An opening that frames the diary — a genre held to belong to men — as written by a woman's hand. This witty conceit opened the way for diary literature written in kana, and shows Ki no Tsurayuki's ingenuity at its sharpest.

There is much I cannot forget, much that grieves me, but I cannot write it all down. Be that as it may, let me be done with this quickly and cast it away.

The closing of the diary. After writing at length of his grief for the daughter he lost in Tosa, he sets down his brush with the words “let me be rid of this quickly.”

She who was born here has not come home with us — and to see the young pines growing in our garden is so sad.

A poem from the closing scene, on arriving home in the capital. The daughter lost in Tosa does not return, yet young pines have grown in the garden while they were away: it voices the grief of a homecoming that should have been joyful.
