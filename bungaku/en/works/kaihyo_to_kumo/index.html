About this work

A collection Kitahara Hakushū compiled in his mid-forties, eight years after Suibokushū. From "New Hymns of Antiquity," which tries to bring the diction of the Kojiki and the Shinto liturgies back into the present, through poems in a mysterious manner drawing flowers and birds, to the title piece singing his travels in Hokkaido and Sakhalin — a book of his maturity, in which he prayed to the spirit of language for the revival of the ancient gods.

Summary

"New Hymns of Antiquity," placed at the head of the book, opens with a piece like a repeated prayer, "The water's source is a thing to ponder," and continues through "The Solitary God," "Word Inquiry," "The Hunter," and "The Wolf." Drawing on the diction of the Kojiki, the Nihon Shoki, the fudoki, and the Shinto liturgies, Hakushū tries to cast a modern light upon them. In the following "Pictures of White Flowers and Birds," subjects from Chinese-style painting — waterfowl, white herons, white peonies, magnolia, magpies, doves — are set in short lines with much white space, mixed in with a rougher piece like "The Old Rooster." In "Valley of Moonlight" and "Celebrating the Wind," the night view looked down from the hills of Ōmori Magome, and a midday landscape crossed by a bridge, are sung, and a modern rhythm appears in the call, "Fly, kite, fly." The title piece, "The Seal and the Clouds," is a sequence stringing together memories of a journey north — Shiokubi Cape, the mountains of Sakhalin, the Sea of Okhotsk under a cloudy sky, the song of an old Ainu. In the latter half, "Early Spring in the Water Country" returns to the waterside of his home, and copies down, in soft colloquial language, the reeds along the marsh, a red cow carried on a small boat, and the voices of frogs. In the pieces of "Mist of Gold Dust," near the end of the book, a quiet day spent before a larch grove and an old walnut tree is told, and the collection closes with three lines looking up at a tree under the moon. In the preface and afterword, Hakushū sets down his own poetics: that even a single sound of a word holds within it a vast universe.

The opening (original text)

New Hymns of Antiquity The Water's Source The water's source is a thing to ponder. Moss-clear water wells and drips, Sunlight seeps through and drips, Oak and andromeda spread their branches over, And the sacred camellia of mirror-leaves forms a hollow cave — The water's source is a thing to ponder.

Kaihyō to kumo by Kitahara Hakushū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

A Fine Night On a moonlit night A white birch, so thoroughly withered. On a moonlit night, it seems to brush against some old man's grass. Shadow On the larch The shadow of a larch falls. It must be a moonlit night. Tree Within a grove of white birch A single walnut tree stands old. How blue its blossoms.

Kaihyō to kumo by Kitahara Hakushū (Aozora Bunko)
