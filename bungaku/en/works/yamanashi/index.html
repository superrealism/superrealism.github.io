About this work

A short children's story published in the Iwate Mainichi Shimbun in 1923. Subtitled "two blue lantern slides of the bottom of a small mountain stream," it gives two scenes, "May" and "December," seen through the eyes of two young crabs living on the streambed. The riddling line "the kramubon laughed!", the sudden shadow of death and the quiet joy of abundance are crystallized in vivid colour and sound. Among the best-loved of Kenji's stories and long taught in schools.

Summary

Kenji projects the story of two young crabs at the bottom of a mountain stream as "two blue lantern slides." — "May." At the bottom of the pale water two crab children exchange idle words: the kramubon laughed, the kramubon died. Then suddenly a shadow like a bullet shoots through the water, swallowing a fish whole. It is a kingfisher. Into the mild world of the water the terror of death has abruptly passed. — "December." The crab children have grown a good deal. Into the water on a moonlit night something drops with a plop: a ripe wild pear. The sweet-smelling fruit floats along a while and comes to rest caught in a branch. In two or three days it will make fine wine, the father crab says, calming them. Beneath waves that flicker with pale flame the crab family is wrapped in a quiet abundance. "And with that my lantern show is ended." Life and death, terror and blessing, are drawn apart from one another within fresh colour and sound in this, one of the finest of Kenji's stories for children.

Characters

  • The crab children　Two brother crabs living at the bottom of the mountain stream. They talk over the kramubon, and meet the shadow of the kingfisher in May and the wild pear in December
  • The father crab　The crab children's father. He calms them when the kingfisher frightens them, and tells them where the fallen wild pear has gone

The opening (original text)

Two blue lantern slides that took the bottom of a small mountain stream. 1. May Two crab children were talking at the bottom of the pale blue water. "The kramubon laughed." "The kramubon laughed kapu-kapu."

The Wild Pear by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The waves lifted their pale blue flames higher and higher, swaying; it was as though they were breathing out diamond dust. * And with that my lantern show is ended.

The Wild Pear by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Two baby crabs were talking at the bottom of the pale blue-green water. “Kurambon laughed.” “Kurambon laughed, kapukapu.”

A scene from a story that depicts the bottom of a mountain stream as “two blue slides of a magic lantern.” With nothing more than the unexplained word “kurambon,” the world of the young crabs springs vividly to life.

And with that, my magic-lantern show comes to an end.

The closing of a story told as “two blue slides of a magic lantern.” As the narrator draws the curtain, the world of the mother and baby crabs quietly recedes.
