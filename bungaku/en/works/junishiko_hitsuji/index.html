About this work

The piece for the year of the sheep — the folklore and legends of the sheep — published in Taiyō in 1919, and the shortest in the series. Noting that since sheep were few in Japan there are almost no Japanese sheep stories, he begins from a Swedish famine legend, introduces the European folk belief that treats the sheep as a spirit of the grain, and lays it against the Japanese boar-day god and the Inari cult.

Summary

There is a comic verse: "a patchwork screen, a sheep's five-grain rice." To write idle talk about sheep in a season when both rice and paper are dear is truly, he laughs at himself, as much a waste of paper as making a patchwork screen to feed to a sheep; and with a head still unsteady from the influenza Kumagusu takes up his pen. Let us begin with the high price of rice, he says — and what follows is the legend of the great famine in Sweden. Knowing that the famine could not be escaped, the national assembly resolved to kill all the old and the sick; but the king could not bear the cruelty of it and inquired of the will of heaven. Heaven told him that if a beautiful maiden should come to the palace neither walking on her feet nor riding on horse or in carriage, neither by day nor by night, neither clothed nor naked, the calamity might be avoided. From there the talk spreads to sheep legends of many countries, and in the latter half moves to the belief in the "corn spirit." Citing the researches of Mannhardt and Frazer, he explains that the life of the grain has been believed to exist apart from the grain itself, and to appear in the shapes of ox, horse, hare, deer, sheep, goat, fox and rat. Giving the instances of the last sheaf-cutter being called "the corn goat" in Switzerland and the last sheaf "the lame goat" in Scotland, he concludes that the Genchō god and the god Inari of Japan are likewise nothing but corn spirits.

The opening (original text)

There is a comic verse that runs, "a patchwork screen, a sheep's five-grain rice." To set down idle talk about sheep in this season when rice is dear and paper is dear seems truly as much a waste of paper as making a patchwork screen to feed to a sheep; but thinking it awkward to break off now the talk of the twelve beasts that has run these six or seven years in Taiyō, I begin to write as things occur to me, with a head still unsteady from the influenza.

Jūnishikō (hitsuji) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

All these are survivals of the belief that the corn spirit appears in the shape of a goat; and in some places the beast taken for the corn spirit is killed when spring comes and its blood and bones mixed with the seed grain in prayer for a good harvest.... The difference between goat and boar apart, a belief in the corn spirit that closely resembles our own Genchō god exists in Europe to this day; and that such a beast-shaped corn spirit advanced into a human-shaped god of agriculture like Demeter is the same thing as the fox's becoming the old-man-shaped Inari Daimyōjin.

Jūnishikō (hitsuji) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
