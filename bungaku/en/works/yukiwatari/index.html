About this work

A children's story known as the only work for which Miyazawa Kenji was paid in his lifetime. On a clear day when one can walk anywhere over the frozen snow, the brother and sister Shirō and Kanko meet the fox cub Konzaburō and are invited to a magic-lantern show of the foxes. The ring of the chanted refrain — hard snow kanko, crusted snow shinko — and the clean ending, in which human children and fox children come to trust one another, have made it particularly loved among Kenji's stories.

Summary

On a day when the snow had frozen quite hard, harder than marble, Shirō and Kanko put on their little snow boots and went out into the fields. Over the fields, where they can never walk, over the plain of pampas grass, they can go anywhere at all. As they come near the woods, singing "Hard snow kanko, frozen snow shinko," a little white fox named Konzaburō appears before them. That foxes deceive people, he pleads earnestly, is a complete lie; and he offers them millet dumplings he has grown and ground and steamed himself, and invites the two to a magic-lantern show to be held on the next moonlit night when the snow has frozen. On the appointed evening Shirō and Kanko go to the show in the woods. In the hall, which no one over eleven may enter, the pupils of the fox school are waiting. On the screen appear great letters — "Thou shalt not drink liquor," "Beware of traps," "Thou shalt not make light of fire" — and pictures follow one after another of men who came to grief while drunk and of foxes caught in traps. The two ate the fox's dumplings without the least hesitation. In his closing address Konzaburō says: "Tonight there is something you must all take deeply to heart. It is that human children, wise and not drunk in the least, were kind enough to eat what a fox had made." The fox pupils were all so moved that they stood up with glittering tears. On the way home the foxes put acorns and chestnuts and blue-glowing stones into the children's breasts. And in the middle of the pale white snowfield the two meet the black shadows of their brothers, come to fetch them.

Characters

  • Shirō　The boy who goes out into the fields
  • Kanko　Shirō's little sister
  • Konzaburō　The little fox who pleads that foxes do not deceive people. The host of the magic-lantern show

The opening (original text)

The snow had frozen quite hard, harder than marble, and the sky too seemed to be made of a cold, smooth board of blue stone. "Hard snow kanko, frozen snow shinko." The sun burned pure white, scattering the scent of lilies and blazing on the snow again. The trees and everything else glittered with frost as though sugar had been sprinkled over them. "Hard snow kanko, frozen snow shinko." Shirō and Kanko put on their little snow boots and went kick, kick, kick out into the fields.

Yukiwatari by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Konzaburō came before the two of them and said, bowing politely, "Well then. Good-bye. I shall never forget your kindness tonight." The two bowed too and set off home. The fox pupils came running after them and put acorns and chestnuts and blue-glowing stones into their breasts and their pockets, saying, "There, we give you these!" "There, please take them!" — and then fled home like the wind. Konzaburō watched them, laughing. The two came out of the woods and went across the fields. And in the middle of that pale white snowfield they saw three black shadows coming toward them. They were their brothers, come to fetch them.

Yukiwatari by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

The snow had frozen through, harder than marble, and the sky too seemed made of a cold, smooth slab of blue stone. “Hard snow, kanko; frozen snow, shinko.”

The opening of the story. The children's chant bounces with the joy of a winter day when the snow is frozen hard enough to walk anywhere across the fields, and it leads on to their meeting with a young fox.
