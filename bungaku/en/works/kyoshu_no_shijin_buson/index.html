About this work

A critical work Hagiwara Sakutarō published in 1936: a fine piece of literary criticism in which the poet Sakutarō discusses the haiku of Yosa Buson from the standpoint neither of a haiku poet nor of a scholar but of a poet. In verses that had been disposed of with the word sketching since Masaoka Shiki, he finds with a modern poet's sensibility their essence as lyric poems of longing, and opens up their beauty and their poetry brilliantly. It includes an essay on Bashō as well, and opened new ground in haiku criticism.

Summary

In recent years a great deal of scholarship on the haiku of Buson and Bashō has come pouring forth. And yet — Sakutarō begins by observing — the existing discussions of Buson and Bashō have all been written by professional haiku poets, and while thorough in their command of rhetoric and textual research, a surprising number of them have forgotten the true spirit of the lyric — the very essence of what haiku is. Buson's haiku above all have, ever since Shiki, been settled once and for all under the single label of "shasei" (sketch-from-life realism), and their true poetic spirit — the philosophical principle that is the very esprit of haiku — has gone uncriticized. Though no professional haiku poet, "the author," possessed of a free and new eye for appreciation as a poet, reads into Buson's verses the poignant sentiment of "kyōshū" (nostalgic longing) that the modern person feels. Not from the standpoint of a master's view of rhetoric, but from the poesy pulsing beneath the surface of each verse, and the philosophy running through its subjectivity, he lays open, vividly, the poetic beauty of Buson. He argues for the very essence of haiku as an unmistakable lyric poetry, and concludes that no literature in the world, save the Japanese haiku, can hold so profound and complex an emotion within a mere seventeen syllables. Overturning the received wisdom of "shasei" that has held since Shiki, and reviving Buson as "the poet of nostalgic longing," this is a landmark work of haiku criticism unique to the poet Sakutarō.

The opening (original text)

In recent years a great deal of scholarship on the haiku of Buson and Bashō has come pouring forth. In an age such as this, that a single poet — neither a professional haiku poet nor a professional scholar, as I am — should bring out a book of this kind may seem no more than a useless dilettante's pastime; and yet there is a necessary reason why this is not so. For the discussions of Buson and of Bashō that have hitherto appeared in the world have, every one of them, been written by so-called haiku poets — books of the utmost professional care in their exposition of rhetoric and textual research, and yet a great many of them have, surprisingly, forgotten the true spirit of the lyric that is the very essence of haiku itself.

Kyōshū no shijin Yosa Buson by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This autumn — why, I wonder, do I grow old, before the clouds, before the birds? The approach of old age is a sorrowful thing. And yet old age has, too, its own profound and mysterious state of mind. To grow old and be transformed into the divine harmony of the universe, to wander in a realm of poetry vast and dim, is itself a kind of joy. White clouds float in the sky, birds fly high above, and yet time flows on and waits for no one, and I find myself only growing steadily older — such is the lament this verse contains. ...No literature in the world, save the haiku of Japan, can express so complex and so profound an emotion in a mere seventeen characters. This cannot be translated, nor can it be explained. There is nothing for it but for us Japanese to read it directly, in Japanese characters, to recite it aloud in the Japanese tongue, and to savor it within the Japanese tradition.

Kyōshū no shijin Yosa Buson by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)
