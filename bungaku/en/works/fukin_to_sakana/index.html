About this work

An autobiographical short story Hayashi Fumiko published in Kaizō in 1931, and one of the works that made her name after Diary of a Vagabond, drawn from her own childhood. A father who peddles medicine playing a reed organ, a mother who follows her husband reciting sutras, and the narrator, a spirited girl with bobbed hair — it draws through a child's sharp eyes the family's days in Onomichi, the fish town by the sea where they wash up. Poverty and toughness, and even the scene in which the father is humiliated at the police station, stand out in a fresh hand mixing pathos with humour.

Summary

Father, who played the reed organ skillfully, was a peddler who sold medicine wearing a worn-out military police uniform. Mother followed her husband while chanting sutras, and "I," Masako, a headstrong thirteen- or fourteen-year-old girl with cropped hair like a boy's, had grown weary of the family's endless traveling. Catching sight, from the train window, of a seaside town where the rising-sun flag fluttered, Father, on a sudden impulse, got off there. It was a port town thick with fish-shop signboards — Onomichi. Playing the reed organ and calling out "Oichini, oichini" to the fishermen as he sold his medicine, business went well, and the family settled into a rented room on the second floor of a house. "I" begged to eat octopus legs and was scolded for it, was struck by Mother until her nose bled, and yet, in this settled life so unlike traveling, found a fleeting peace. She was even enrolled in school, but was taunted as "the daughter of Oichini, the new fool-general," and poverty followed the family everywhere. Before long, Father was hauled off to the police station for selling a fake beauty lotion. That night, climbing up to peer through the back window of the police station, what "I" saw was Father, slapped across the face by a policeman before Mother's eyes, made to play the reed organ in a strange voice and sing out his sales pitch. Shouting "You fool! You fool!" "I" ran off toward the shore, and deep in my ears, something like the grinding of gears went on creaking, without end. A celebrated autobiographical short story that captures, through a girl's clear eyes, the sorrows and joys of a poor peddler family.

Characters

  • I (Masako)　A headstrong thirteen- or fourteen-year-old girl with cropped hair. Travels from place to place with her peddler parents. The narrator of this story
  • Father　A peddler who plays the reed organ and sells medicine. Good-natured, he is hauled off by the police for selling fake medicine
  • Mother　Follows her husband while chanting sutras. Scolds "I" amid their poor life, but also cherishes her
  • The couple downstairs　A former sailor, lame in one leg, and his wife, who live on the ground floor of the house in Onomichi where the family rents the upper room

The opening (original text)

Father was skillful at playing the reed organ. My memory of music begins with this reed organ of my father's. We had long been jolted along on the train, bored to tears; Mother, beside me as I ate a banana, chanted sutras and wept. "It's only because I entrusted myself to you that I must suffer like this" — perhaps that was what she was saying, in her way. Father, now and then pressing with his hip against the reed organ wrapped in its white cloth bundle, smoked tobacco shredded down to nothing but powder.

Fūkin to sakana no machi by Hayashi Fumiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

A wave of sorrow welled up in me. Father was being slapped across the face, again and again, without mercy, by the policeman. "You fool! You fool!" I let out a cry like a monkey's, and ran off toward the shore. I heard Mother's voice calling, "Masako, wait!" but deep in my ears, something like the grinding of gears went on, far off, endlessly creaking.
