About this work

The hare pays the badger back, one punishment after another, for killing the old woman — one of the five great folk tales of Japan, retold by Kusuyama Masao. From the crackling of the fire set to the brushwood on the badger's back to the sinking of the mud boat, the merciless procedure of the vendetta is told in plain prose a child can read.

Summary

Long ago an old badger who had been spoiling the old man's field was caught in a trap. Hung from the roof beam, the badger proposed to the old woman, who was pounding wheat, that he would help her, and got her neatly to untie the rope. But the moment he was free the badger struck the old woman dead with the pestle, took her shape, made the old man eat soup of her, and ran away. To the old man in his grief came a hare, who had long been friendly with him, and promised to take his revenge. With an innocent face the hare lured the badger out to gather brushwood, and set fire with a flint to the load on his back. "What is it that goes click-clack behind me?" "That is Mount Kachi-Kachi over there, so the kachi-kachi bird is crying." Before long the brushwood blazed up and the badger was badly burned. Next the hare disguised himself as a medicine seller and rubbed pepper paste into the burns. To the badger leaping about in agony he then proposed a fishing trip. The hare made himself a boat of wood and the badger one of clay, and out at sea they began a race. The clay boat soaked through before their eyes and began to crumble. To the badger crying, "Ah, I'm sinking, I'm sinking, help me," the hare pronounced: "Serve you right. That is your payment for tricking and killing the old woman and making the old man eat old-woman soup." And before the badger could so much as gasp, he sank at last.

The opening (original text)

Long, long ago, in a certain place, there were an old man and an old woman. When the old man was out working in his field as always, an old badger would come out of the hill behind and spoil the things in the field that the old man had taken such pains to grow; and besides that he would throw stones and clods of earth at the old man's back, one after another.

Kachikachiyama by Kusuyama Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Serve you right. That is your payment for tricking and killing the old woman and making the old man eat old-woman soup." When he said it, the badger said he would never do such things again and begged to be saved, and put his hands together to the hare. Meanwhile the boat crumbled away and away, and before he could so much as gasp, the badger sank at last.
