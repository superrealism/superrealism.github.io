About this work

The testament of some hundred and fifty characters that Fujimura Misao, a student of the First Higher School, carved into an oak tree beside the Kegon falls at Nikkō before throwing himself in, in 1903. The sentence saying that the truth of all things is settled in a single word — unknowable — shocked the young men of the time and produced the phrase the anguished youth. One of the shortest pieces of writing cut into the intellectual history of modern Japan.

Summary

"Boundless indeed are heaven and earth. Vast indeed are past and present." Against the boundlessness of heaven and earth, and the vastness of past and present, he means to take the measure of this great thing with a body no more than five shaku tall. What authority, in the end, does the philosophy of Horatio really hold? Drawing on the passage in Shakespeare's Hamlet where it is said that there are things in heaven and earth that the philosophy known to Horatio alone cannot explain, Fujimura Misao writes: "The truth of all creation is exhausted in a single word: 'unknowable.'" All the truth of this world, he says, can be said in one word — that it is "unknowable." Bearing this grievance, he falls into anguish, until at last he resolves upon death — but standing now at the cliff's edge, he finds no unease at all in his heart. It was then, he says, that he first came to know: "great pessimism is one with great optimism." These hundred-odd characters, carved by a sixteen-year-old First Higher School student into an oak tree, dealt an immeasurable shock to the young men of the day, and brought on a string of copycat suicides at the Kegon falls. Many men of letters, Natsume Sōseki — who taught English at the First Higher School — among them, were strongly affected by this death.

The opening (original text)

Boundless indeed are heaven and earth. Vast indeed are past and present. With a small body five shaku tall I mean to take the measure of this greatness. What authority, in the end, does the philosophy of Horatio hold?

Gantō no kan by Fujimura Misao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The truth of all creation is exhausted in a single word: "unknowable." Bearing this grievance, I fell into anguish, until at last I resolved upon death. Now that I stand at the cliff's edge, there is no unease at all in my heart. I know for the first time that great pessimism is one with great optimism.

Gantō no kan by Fujimura Misao (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

How vast is heaven and earth! How distant, past and present! With this body of five feet I would take the measure of their greatness. What authority, after all, can Horatio's philosophy claim? The truth of all things is summed up in a single word: “incomprehensible.” Bearing this grief, my anguish has at last brought me to resolve on death. Now that I stand upon the rock, there is no unease in my heart. For the first time I know that great pessimism is one with great optimism.

The full text of the farewell that Fujimura Misao, a student of the First Higher School, wrote on a tree beside the Kegon Falls before throwing himself into them in 1903. Summing up life in the single word “incomprehensible,” it caused a great stir and became a symbol of the anguish of Meiji youth.
