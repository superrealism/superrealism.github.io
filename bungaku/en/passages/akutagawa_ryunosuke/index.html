Famous passages by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke

Well-known lines from the works of Akutagawa Ryūnosuke.

48 passages

Rashōmon

It was toward evening on a certain day. A servant was waiting under the Rashōmon gate for the rain to stop.

A spare introduction that fixes time and character in a single breath, in one corner of a ruined Heian capital. From here the story of a servant's quiet step into evil, taken in order to survive, begins to move.

No one knows where the servant went.

The final line that closes the whole story. It lets the servant, having stepped into evil, vanish into the darkness, refusing to tell what became of him and casting the reader off, unanswered.

“Then you won't hold it against me if I strip you. I'm another who'd starve to death if I didn't.”

Words the servant flings out after hearing the old woman's excuse, as he resolves to become a thief. Justifying evil for the sake of survival by the old woman's own logic, the story heads into darkness.

The Nose

There is no one at Ikenoo who does not know of the nose of the priest Zenchi Naigu. It is some five or six inches long, and hangs down from above his upper lip past his chin.

An opening that explains a long nose in matter-of-fact terms. Depicting the “egoism of the bystander,” who feels let down when another's misfortune is cured, it was the breakthrough work that won Sōseki's lavish praise.

Now that it had happened, surely no one would laugh at him anymore.

The scene of relief as he looks in the mirror at his now-shortened nose. But the mockery does not stop, and Naigu's heart begins to waver once again.

There are two contradictory feelings in the human heart. Of course, no one fails to sympathize with another's misfortune. Yet when that person somehow manages to escape it, we in turn feel vaguely dissatisfied.

The narrator's reflection follows the scene in which people laugh all the more at Naigu after his nose has become shorter. It exposes the attachment to another's misfortune hidden beneath sympathy, leading to the phrase "the selfishness of the onlooker" shortly afterward.

Yam Gruel

But the thing the Fifth Rank had dreamed of—“to have my fill of yam gruel”—came true surprisingly easily.

A sentence announcing that the Fifth Rank's modest dream of eating yam gruel to his heart's content will come true in an unexpected way. It leads to the story's ironic ending: a dream fades the moment it is fulfilled.

Yet he was also the happy man who had cherished, all alone, the desire to have his fill of yam gruel.

A passage near the end, in which the Fifth Rank, having lost his appetite before a vast quantity of yam gruel, looks back fondly on himself when he still cherished that dream. It reveals that holding on to an unfulfilled wish was itself happiness.

Hell Screen

A lord such as His Excellency of Horikawa, I dare say, has never been seen before, and shall surely never be seen again in ages to come.

An opening narrated as the account of one who served the lord. It begins a story of art and karma, in which the painter Yoshihide fixes his gaze on his own daughter's death by fire in order to complete his screen.

Yoshihide, who a moment before had seemed to suffer the torments of Hell, now stood there—had he forgotten even the presence of His Lordship?—with an indescribable radiance, the very radiance of rapture, spread over his wrinkled face, his arms folded tightly across his chest.

The expression of the painter Yoshihide as his daughter burns inside the blazing carriage. The climax, in which the narrator relates with dread the figure of a man possessed by art beyond a parent's love.

The Spider's Thread

It was on a certain day. The Buddha was strolling alone along the edge of the lotus pond in Paradise.

An opening as gentle as a fairy tale, depicting a morning for the Buddha in Paradise. It begins a parable about a single thread lowered down into Hell.

Hey, you sinners! This spider's thread is mine. Who gave you leave to climb up on it? Get down. Get down!

The scene in which Kandata screams down at the sinners climbing the thread after him. At these very words the thread snaps, and he falls back down into Hell.

But the lotuses in the lotus pond of Paradise pay no heed at all to such things. Their jewel-like white blossoms sway their calyxes about the Buddha's feet, and from the golden stamens at their centers an indescribably sweet fragrance flows out endlessly all around. In Paradise, too, it must now be nearly noon.

The close, after the thread breaks and Kandata falls back into Hell. Depicting the calm of Paradise, wholly indifferent to the human tragedy, it returns in a circle to the lotus-pond scene of the opening.

Hōkyōnin no shi

All the worth of human life lies, in the end, in a moment of deep emotion that nothing can replace.

The narrator's comment after it is revealed that Lorenzo was in fact a woman. Written in an archaic style modeled on the Christian books printed in early Japan, it voices Akutagawa's view of art: that the value of a life lies in a single moment of emotion.

Mandarins

It was only then, for the first time, that I was able, if only for a moment, to forget my inexpressible weariness and ennui, and this incomprehensible, base, and tedious life.

The closing line, just after watching a girl toss mandarin oranges from the train window to her little brothers. A gray landscape takes on color for just one moment — the clearest ending among Akutagawa's short pieces.

The level crossing on the edge of town in the gathering dusk, the three children crying out like little birds, and over them the vivid color of the tangerines falling helter-skelter — all of it passed outside the train window in the blink of an eye.

The scene in which a young girl going into service throws tangerines from the train window to the little brothers who have come to see her off. The vivid color scattering over the crossing at dusk transforms, in an instant, the weary heart of the narrator.

The Ball

I was thinking of the fireworks — of fireworks that are like our life.

Words spoken by the French naval officer who dances with Akiko at the Rokumeikan ball, as he gazes up at fireworks in the night sky. In the midst of a glittering evening, this remark likening the brevity of life to fireworks is justly celebrated.

Toshishun

It was a certain spring evening. Beneath the western gate of Luoyang, the capital of Tang China, stood a young man, gazing vacantly up at the sky.

A children's story that opens in the voice of a folk tale. It moves quietly toward the question of what Toshishun, who has seen both glory and hell through an immortal's sorcery, will choose in the end.

Forgetting the old man's warning entirely, Toshishun stumbled forward and ran to her side, threw both arms around the neck of the half-dead horse, and, tears streaming down, cried out once: “Mother!”

The scene in which, on seeing his mother reduced to a beast in Hell, he finally breaks his vow of silence. Choosing human feeling over his hope of becoming an immortal, this is the very heart of the story.

“Whatever I become, I mean to live an honest life, as a human being should.”

Words Toshishun speaks to Tekkanshi near the end, having given up his wish to become an immortal. After passing through both wealth and magic, he shows the simple human way of life he has chosen.

In a Grove

Yes, that is so. It was indeed I who found the body.

It opens with the words of a woodcutter answering an imperial examiner's questions. Its structure, in which the testimonies never agree and the truth is never reached, left the phrase “in a grove” in the Japanese language as an idiom for the unknowable.

Someone — that someone, with an invisible hand, quietly drew the small sword from my breast. At the same moment, blood welled up once more in my mouth. And with that I sank forever into the darkness between death and rebirth.

The close of the tale told by the dead man, Kanazawa no Takehiro, through the mouth of a medium. Who drew the sword from his breast is never revealed; the testimony sinks into darkness, and the truth is left to the reader.

The Handcar

It was the year Ryōhei turned eight that construction began on the light railway between Odawara and Atami.

An opening that fixes time and place by way of the boy's age. The anxiety of the long walk home after riding a handcar too far becomes a memory he never forgets, for the whole of his life.

Before him, worn out by the toil of the world, there still runs—just as it did then—a single thin path through dim thickets and up slopes, breaking off and running on.

The close of the story. The grown-up Ryōhei recalls the forlorn road home after riding a handcar too far as a boy, and the story ends by laying life's anxieties over that path.

Shuju no kotoba

Life is like a box of matches. To treat it as a serious matter is absurd. Not to treat it as a serious matter is dangerous.

A line from this collection of aphorisms. Refusing to side with either the lightness or the weight of life, it captures a wary stance through the metaphor of a single box of matches — one of the clear-eyed epigrams of Akutagawa's final years.

Life is more hellish than Hell.

The opening of the fragment titled “Hell.” It goes on to say that the torments of Hell follow fixed laws, while those of life are not so simple—an ironic comment on the incomprehensibility of life.

Freedom is like the air on a mountain peak. Neither can be endured by the weak.

One of the fragments titled “Freedom.” Likening freedom to the thin air of a high mountain, this aphorism pointedly exposes the human weakness that seeks freedom yet cannot bear it.

Public opinion is always a lynching, and a lynching is always an entertainment—even if newspaper articles are used in place of pistols.

The fragment titled “Public Opinion.” It declares that when public opinion passes judgment on someone, it is a private punishment and an entertainment besides. It is still quoted for a sharpness that speaks to today's online pile-ons.

A genius is one who stands just a single step away from us. His contemporaries never understand that this one step is a thousand leagues. Posterity, in turn, is blind to the fact that these thousand leagues are a single step.

The fragment titled “Genius.” A paradox: the gap between a genius and ordinary people is a single step, yet contemporaries misjudge how vast it is, and posterity how close.

What saves us from love is not so much reason as busyness.

The fragment titled “Busyness.” It goes on to say that love needs leisure above all to be fulfilled, and that the great lovers of old were all idle people—a witty paradox.

Of all the attributes of God, the one that most moves me to pity Him is that God is unable to kill Himself.

An aphorism under the heading “God.” Turning upside down the one thing an all-powerful God cannot do, it lets Akutagawa's twisted feelings about life and death show through in the unexpected form of pity for God.

The benefit morality confers is a saving of time and labor. The harm morality inflicts is the complete paralysis of conscience.

From the section “Morals.” Holding morality at arm's length as a convenient convention and weighing its benefit against its harm, it pierces the numbing of conscience in those who lean on morality.

Weapons in themselves are nothing to fear. What is to be feared is the skill of the warrior. Justice in itself is nothing to fear. What is to be feared is the eloquence of the agitator.

An aphorism holding that what we should fear is not the tool itself but the skill and the words of those who wield it. It pinpoints the danger of rhetoric that moves people in the name of justice.

I have no conscience. All I have is nerves.

A self-portrait of a section, headed “I.” In the claim that he has only nerves in place of a conscience, one senses how Akutagawa saw himself — a man who lived with an overly sensitive temperament.

Life is like a book with many missing pages. It can hardly be called a complete volume. And yet, somehow, it is a volume.

One of the aphorisms on life, placed right after “Life is like a box of matches.” Likening life to a book with many pages missing, it describes a human life that forms a whole even while incomplete.

One side of genius is plainly the talent for causing scandal.

An aphorism that turns the usual praise of genius inside out, declaring that the power to set the world talking is also part of talent. It ironically reflects a literary world where scandal and talent live side by side.

It is harder to defend oneself than to defend others. Let anyone who doubts it look at lawyers.

The section “Defense,” from the latter part published posthumously. Its irony — that even those whose trade is defending others falter when it comes to themselves — pierces human weakness.

What is human, all too human, is for the most part surely animal.

An aphorism playing on the title of Nietzsche's Human, All Too Human. It coolly asserts that much of what we tend to affirm as humanity is in fact rooted in animal instinct.

Love is merely sexual desire given poetic expression. At the very least, sexual desire that has not been given poetic expression does not deserve to be called love.

From the section “Love.” What looks like a cold definition reducing love to desire also holds, in Akutagawa's two-sided way, that it is poetic expression that makes love love.

Haguruma

To live on in such a state of mind is an agony beyond words. Is there no one who will quietly strangle me in my sleep?

The closing of this manuscript, written in the year of his death and published posthumously. Placed at the end of a record of nerve-wracking hallucinations, it is the single most famous last line in modern Japanese literature.

Kappa

This is the tale that a patient at a certain mental hospital — Patient Number Twenty-Three — will tell to anyone who will listen.

The opening of this late satirical novel. By declaring in advance that it is the tale of a madman, it opens the way to a scathing critique of human society, entrusted to the land of the kappa.

A fool always believes that everyone other than himself is a fool.

A line from “The Words of a Fool,” written by Mag, a philosopher in the land of the kappa. Using the kappa world as an inverted mirror of human society, it mocks human folly.

Think it over carefully before you answer: will you be born into this world or not?

In the scene of a birth in the land of the kappa, the words the father calls loudly into the mother's womb to the unborn child. The fantastic idea of letting the child itself choose whether to be born becomes a sharp satire on human society.

Bungeiteki na amarini bungeiteki na

I do not consider the novel without a “story” worthy of the name to be the highest kind. Nor, therefore, do I say that one should write only novels without a “story.”

The opening of the essay Akutagawa wrote during his debate with Tanizaki Jun'ichirō over plot in the novel. The phrase “a novel without a story worthy of the name,” which values poetic spirit over the interest of plot, is known as a statement of Akutagawa's late view of literature.

Aru kyūyu e okuru shuki

In my own case, at least, it is only a vague unease. Some vague unease about my future, and nothing more.

A line from a memoir entrusted to a friend just before his suicide. Written as an answer to the question of why he chose to die, it has been read ever since as words that captured the mood of intellectuals at the end of the Taishō era, reaching beyond any one man's private reasons.

Only, to one such as I am now, nature is more beautiful than ever. You will laugh at my contradiction — loving the beauty of nature and yet intending to kill myself. But nature is beautiful because it is reflected in my dying eyes.

A passage near the end of the note in which he gave “a vague unease” as the motive for his suicide. The idea of “dying eyes,” to which nature appears all the more beautiful in the face of death, is also known from Kawabata Yasunari's essay of the same name.

A Fool's Life

“Life is not worth a single line of Baudelaire.”

A line from a scene atop a bookstore ladder, looking down on the people below. Placed at the head of these autobiographical fragments, left unpublished at his death, it reflects both Akutagawa's art-above-all stance and a bottomless despair.

He merely lived from day to day in the half-dark—leaning, as it were, on a slender sword whose blade was chipped away.

The close of “Defeat,” the final section of this autobiographical sequence of fragments. A piercing self-portrait of his own last years as a man leaning on a slender, chipped sword.
