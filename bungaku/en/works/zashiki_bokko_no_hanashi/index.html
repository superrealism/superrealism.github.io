About this work

A small piece in which Miyazawa Kenji set down the traditions of the zashiki-warashi handed down in Iwate as four short episodes. The sound of a broom in a room where no one is; ten children playing who somehow become eleven — each closes with the one line that this is what a zashiki-warashi is, and the offhandedness of adding no explanation is exactly what quietly chills the reader.

Summary

This is a tale of the zashiki-warashi from our part of the country — so it begins, and four short episodes follow. The first: on a bright afternoon, with all the grown-ups off working in the mountains, two children are playing in the garden when, from some room in the house, comes the sound of a broom sweeping, swish, swish. Creeping up arm in arm to look, they find no one in any of the rooms — only the sunlight pouring down. The second: ten children join hands and go round and round a room chanting "Round the great road, round the great road," and before they know it there are eleven of them. There is no unfamiliar face among them, and no two faces the same, and yet however they count it, eleven it remains. The grown-ups say that the extra one is the zashiki-warashi, but every child, determined it could not be them, sits up straight and keeps a sharp eye on all the others. The third: at the festival of the Buddha, a child laid up in bed with the measles kept saying, over and over, "I want to go." In the room where the festival was held, there was a child who looked exactly like that one — and yet the real child had been at home in bed the whole while. The fourth: a boatman tells his tale. On the evening of the seventeenth of the eighth month by the old calendar, called across from the far bank, he put out his boat, and a beautiful child in a crested robe and hakama climbed aboard. Asked where the child had come from and where it was bound, it answered, "I've been staying quite a long while now at the Sasada house over there, but I've grown tired of it, so I'm off elsewhere. I'm going to the Saitō house at Sarashina." After that, the Sasada house fell into ruin, while the Saitō house at Sarashina prospered by leaps and bounds. That is the kind of thing a zashiki-warashi is.

The opening (original text)

This is a tale of the zashiki-warashi from our part of the country. On a bright afternoon, with everyone off working in the mountains, two children were playing in the garden. No one else was in the great house, so all around lay hushed and still. And then, from somewhere in the house, from some room, came the sound of a broom sweeping — swish, swish.

Zashiki bokko no hanashi by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"When we reached the far bank the child was gone, and I found myself sitting at the door of my hut. I don't know whether it was a dream or what. But it must be true, all right — because after that Sasada fell into ruin, and at the Saitō house in Sarashina the sickness cleared right up, and the son finished university too, and the whole family prospered by leaps and bounds." That is the kind of thing a zashiki-warashi is.
