About this work

Yosano Akiko's first collection of tanka, published in 1901 by Tōkyō Shinshisha and Itō Bun'yūkan. Written against her forbidden love for Yosano Tekkan, it sings the passion and sensuality of a young woman without apology, and shocked a literary world in which feudal morality still lingered. Designed by Fujishima Takeji, it holds nearly four hundred poems in six sections. Its manner — an open affirmation of love and of the self, caught in the famous poem asking the man who preaches the Way whether he is not lonely, never having touched the hot blood beneath soft skin — broke the frame of older waka and shaped the tanka that followed.

Summary

The collection is in six sections — "Crimson and Purple," "The Lotus Flower Boat," "White Lilies," "A Wife of Twenty," "The Dancer," "Spring Thoughts" — and sings the love and the passion of a young woman's youth in bold language, with Kyoto and Sakai for its settings. It opens with a poem that proclaims her own youth and beauty aloud — that girl of twenty, her black hair flowing through the comb, and how beautiful is the pride of her spring — and goes on in a passionate measure with the exaltation and the anguish of a forbidden love for the man called in the poems "you" and "god" (Yosano Tekkan), with meetings at Sagano and by the Kamo River in Kyoto, and with the parting from a friend she had loved as a sister. Poems that sing the body and the passion of a woman without flinching — such as the one asking whether the man who expounds the Way is not lonely, never having touched the hot blood of soft skin — left a vivid impression on the readers of the day as a supple resistance to the old morality.

Famous passages

Never once have you touched this soft skin, this hot welling blood — do you not feel lonely, you who preach the Way?

A poem cast at a man who preaches morality without ever having known the warmth of young skin. Challenging old ethics head-on, it symbolizes the passion of the Myōjō school.

That girl of twenty—her black hair flowing through the comb, the pride of her springtime—how beautiful it is!

A poem that sees the proud beauty of youth in a twenty-year-old girl's black hair, flowing through the comb. Boldly affirming youth and one's own beauty, it represents Tangled Hair.

Crossing Gion on the way to Kiyomizu on a night of cherry blossoms and moon—everyone I meet tonight is beautiful.

A poem on the way to Kiyomizu through Gion in Kyoto on a moonlit night of cherry blossoms. To a heart full of the joy of love, everyone she passes looks beautiful.

Somehow feeling that you were waiting for me, I stepped out — into a field of autumn flowers under the evening moon.

A poem from Tangled Hair. Feeling somehow that her lover is waiting, she steps out into a field of flowers with the evening moon above. One of Akiko's representative poems, melting the premonition of love into a scene of the autumn fields.

In the third month of spring, the koto without its bridges gave out a sound — touched, by chance, by my tangled hair of the evening.

A poem that includes the words “tangled hair,” echoing the title of the collection. A koto without its bridges sounds when her loosened hair brushes against it by chance: the allure of a spring evening is drawn through the feel of a sound.
