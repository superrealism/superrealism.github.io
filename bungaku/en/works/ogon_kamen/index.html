About this work

A long novel Edogawa Ranpo serialized in King in 1930 and 1931, which he himself placed as his first step toward the great detective novel. A thief in a golden mask troubles the capital, and the great detective Akechi Kogorō faces him. The golden mask, who is after pearls and works of art of national importance and after the young lady Fujiko, turns out to be none other than Arsène Lupin, the great French thief — a bold stroke that startled readers. A central full-length entertainment by Ranpo, drawing a brilliant contest of wits between a detective and a thief across national borders.

Summary

In the imperial capital, a mysterious figure in a golden mask appears, targeting the great pearl of national-treasure standing kept by the Ōtori family, along with a string of other priceless works of art. This elusive Golden Mask, appearing and vanishing without a trace, strikes terror into the police and high society alike. The great detective Akechi Kogorō takes up the case. Disguise, pursuit, escape, and the kidnapping of the young lady Fujiko as a hostage — amid this breathless struggle, Akechi at last uncovers the true identity of this extraordinary thief: the Golden Mask is none other than Arsène Lupin, the world-famous master thief who has crossed the sea from France to invade Japan. This unprecedented meeting between Japan's great detective and Europe's king of thieves, a duel of wits with reversal after reversal, comes at last to a close with Akechi's victory. Every one of the gang is captured, the national treasure recovered, and Fujiko freed from Lupin's grasp. Lupin alone escapes by airplane, and in time the plane is spotted adrift somewhere over the Pacific — but he is not a man to be so easily accounted for, and whether he has truly vanished beneath the waves remains far from certain. A spectacular tale on a grand scale, pitting a great detective against a great thief across national borders.

The opening (original text)

In this world, once in fifty years, or once in a hundred, there comes to pass — on the same order as some natural cataclysm, some great war, some sweeping epidemic — an extraordinary and uncanny event: something more outlandish than any nightmare, more outlandish than any novelist's invention, which simply happens, out of nowhere. Perhaps human society, that one vast living creature, is seized for a moment by some strange, acute malady, and goes briefly out of its mind.

Ōgon kamen by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Lupin's airplane remained unaccounted for over the following days, until one day a newspaper wire startled the public: a steamship crossing the Pacific had spotted Monsieur Chaplan's airplane adrift on the sea. Had Lupin, then, vanished beneath the waves of the Pacific? No, no — this was no man to be accounted for so simply. As he had done before, he might well have only feigned death, and even now, in some corner of the world, be quietly plotting some other grand and wicked scheme.

Ōgon kamen by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)
