About this work

One of Niimi Nankichi's central children's stories, written in his lifetime and collected after his death in the 1943 volume The Camellia Where the Ox Was Tied. On a cold winter morning, a fox cub sees snow for the first time, and his mother sends him into the town to buy mittens. Afraid of human beings, she changes one of his paws into a human hand, presses a silver coin into it and tells him to hold out that hand — but the cub holds out the fox's paw by mistake. The hatter sells him the mittens all the same. A jewel of Nankichi's work, gentle in its handling of a parent's love and of how human beings look from outside.

Summary

The cold winter came down from the north to the wood where a mother fox and her cub live. Seeing snow for the first time, the cub finds his paws numb with cold, and his mother decides to buy him mittens in the town. But she carries a terrible memory of what human beings once did to her, and cannot find the courage to go all the way with him. So she changes just one of his forepaws into the hand of a human child, presses a silver coin into it, and tells him over and over that at the hatter's in the town he is to hold out that hand and ask for mittens to fit it. Bewildered by the lights of the town, the cub reaches the hatter's shop — and, too nervous to remember what he was told, holds out not the human hand but his own fox's paw. The shopkeeper is suspicious for a moment, but since the cub is holding real money he sells him the woollen mittens all the same. The cub gets safely home and tells his mother that human beings are not frightening at all. And hearing him, the mother fox murmurs, are human beings really good, I wonder?

Characters

  • The fox cub　A young fox who sees snow for the first time. On his mother's instructions he goes alone into the town to buy mittens
  • The mother fox　A mother who loves her child. Afraid of human beings, she changes one of the cub's paws into a human hand and sends him to the town

The opening (original text)

The cold winter came down from the north to the wood where the mother fox and her cub were living. One morning the fox cub was about to come out of the burrow when he cried "Oh!" and came tumbling back to his mother with his hands over his eyes. "Mother, something's got into my eye, take it out, quickly, quickly," he said. The mother fox, startled, drew away in alarm the hands that covered his eyes and looked; but there was nothing in them at all. Only when she came out of the mouth of the burrow did she understand. In the night a great deal of pure white snow had fallen. The sun was shining on it and glittering, and the snow was throwing back a dazzling light. The cub, who had never known snow, had taken so strong a reflection for something got into his eye.

The ending (original text)

The two foxes went off toward the wood. The moon had risen, and the foxes' coats shone silver, and cobalt shadows collected in their footprints. "Mother, human beings aren't a bit frightening." "Why do you say so?" "I made a mistake and held out my real hand. But the hatter didn't catch me. He gave me these lovely warm mittens all the same" — and he clapped his mitten-covered hands together to show her. The mother fox said, "Well!" in astonishment, and then murmured, "Are human beings really good, I wonder? Are human beings really good, I wonder?"

Famous passages

Cold winter came down from the north, even to the forest where the mother fox and her cub lived.

A children's story that opens with the arrival of winter. From this single line begins a night shared by a mother fox who fears humans and a cub who knows nothing of that fear.

Can it really be true that humans are good creatures? Can it really be true that humans are good creatures?

The closing, after the cub returns safely with his mittens. The murmured question of the mother fox, who has always feared humans, leaves its echo without ever being answered.

“Mama, my hands are cold. My hands are all tingly and stinging,” he said, holding out his hands, wet and turned peony-red, to his mother fox.

The words of the little fox, having touched snow for the first time, to his mother. The babyish expression conveys the cold as a sensation on the skin, and the story begins to move from the mother's worry for her child.
