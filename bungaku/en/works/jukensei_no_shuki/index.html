About this work

Kume Masao's central work, in the form of the notes of a young man making his second attempt at the entrance examination for the First Higher School. Collected in the 1918 volume Student Days, it was widely read by the examinees of the time. Jealousy of a younger brother who has caught him up, the idleness and self-mockery of his fellow lodgers, the blankness of the moment when he sees that his number is not on the noticeboard — the workings of a mind being crushed by the examination system are recorded exactly, laid over a failed love. It is a story of a hundred years ago, and the airlessness of it has not aged.

Summary

Three in the morning at Wakamatsu station. "I" — Kuno Kenkichi — setting out for Tokyo for the second time, say good-bye to the younger brother who has come to see me off. Last year's disgraceful failure at the examination for the First Higher School — because of a single English word I did not know, the friend whose marks were the same as mine got in and I did not. If I do not get in this year I cannot go home alive. And my younger brother, who finishes middle school, is coming up to Tokyo this April. Looking at Lake Inawashiro at dawn, I weep of myself at the thought of the dawn of a new fate. But the days in Tokyo do not go as I wish. Thinking there are six months yet to the examination breeds idleness; the crammer becomes a good place to kill time; and calling on my fellow candidates and learning how little they are working, I am secretly reassured. What fills my mind is Sumiko, my brother-in-law's niece, who comes to the house every Sunday. The memory of the day I was taken to the First Higher School's anniversary, and held her hand in the crowded corridor of the dormitory and felt it held in return, is my one light. In April my brother comes up, sets himself a daily task of twelve hours, and solves in five minutes a geometry problem that cost me three or four hours. Unable to bear being in the same room, I move to a temple at Suidōbata. On the day I take Sumiko to the Home Exhibition, I take my courage and hold her hand in the dark hall of the sideshows; and she turns as though reproaching me and drew her hand away. On the way home I had to watch, pretending indifference, while she chose a pen case as a present for my brother. In July, four days of examinations. In mathematics I handed in the last problem with only the formula written; in history I even conceived the desperate notion of stealing a look at the blank space of my neighbor's paper. To the young man who was put out of the hall laughing "hee, hee, hee" at that moment, I feel a heartfelt sympathy — was not that laugh a curse upon this examination system from the very bottom? When the examinations are over my heart grows light, and before my sister I confess at last my hope of marrying Sumiko. My sister answered, "Wait at least until you know the result and are properly in the higher school." The day of the announcement. Number 129 was not on the board. It was not in section three, division A or B, nor in section one, nor in section two. And my brother's number was there beyond mistaking. Some days later, at the house in Sendagi, opening the drawer of my brother's desk like a thief, I find a letter from Sumiko to him. "I am glad beyond anything.... Yours, Sumiko. To my dear Kenji." All was lost. That night, taken by a rakish friend, I pass the first night of my life; and next morning, finding myself on a bench in Ueno park, I take a train for home. Getting off at Yamagata and sitting down on the embankment of Lake Inawashiro under the moon, on my right a black, straight, beckoning jetty ran far out. — The memoir ends here, with a note by the author saying that it was entrusted to him by the hand of the younger brother.

Characters

  • I (Kuno Kenkichi)　The writer of the memoir, sitting the First Higher School examination for the second time
  • My brother (Kenji)　Who comes up to Tokyo three months later and passes into section two
  • Sumiko　My brother-in-law's niece, who visits Sendagi every Sunday
  • My sister　The wife of my brother-in-law at Sendagi, and my confidante
  • My brother-in-law　A bachelor of medicine, and a graduate of the First Higher School
  • Matsui　A fellow candidate in the next room at the temple at Suidōbata
  • Satō　A rakish candidate lodging at Shinhanachō

The opening (original text)

1 The whistle rang through the empty station. The train that carried me left Wakamatsu station, still wrapped in darkness, at three in the morning. "Well then, good-bye. You graduate this year too, so work hard. I shall work hard this year myself." So I said, full of feeling, to my brother outside the window, who had come to see me off.

The ending (original text)

I sat down on the embankment and gazed at the water. And noticing it, on my right, where the landing must once have been, a jetty ran far out into the lake. Black, straight, beckoning, that shape would not leave my eyes. Now I shall get up and walk out along that jetty. Straight on, as far as it goes, as far as it goes....
