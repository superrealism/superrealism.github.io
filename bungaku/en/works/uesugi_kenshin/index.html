About this work

A historical novel serialized in the magazine Shūkan Asahi in 1942. It follows the life of Uesugi Kenshin, the warlord called the dragon of Echigo, around the five battles of Kawanakajima against his great rival Takeda Shingen. Yoshikawa draws a commander who fought not for his own gain but for a cause and for good faith, in his own idealizing manner, and passes on the figure of a hero who kept honour even in an age of war — caught in the story of sending salt to an enemy.

Summary

In the first month of the fourth year of Eiroku, Uesugi Kenshin, now thirty-three, keeps the new year in the castle at Umayabashi in Jōshū, with Uesugi Norimasa, deputy shogun for the Kantō, and the court noble Konoe Sakitsugu as his guests. Guileless and fond of drink, and at the same time deeply given to the Buddhist way, Kenshin holds it as his creed to fight not for private gain but for a just cause; and over his campaigns in the Kantō and over Shinano he crosses arms again and again with his great rival Takeda Shingen. The struggles at Kawanakajima above all are told as one of the finest scenes of the age of war, where the capacity and the strategy of two great men meet. When Shingen was suffering under the salt embargo imposed by the Imagawa and Hōjō, Kenshin sent him salt, saying that war is a matter of bow and arrow and not of rice and salt — an episode that stands for the man's good faith even toward an enemy commander. Loyal to the shoguns Ashikaga Yoshiteru and Yoshiaki, Kenshin came into deepening opposition with Oda Nobunaga, who was hurrying to unify the realm; and five years after Shingen died of illness in camp, he too, still loving his wine, was suddenly gone from the world. That both great men should have met so abrupt an end leaves a sense of something fated as the story closes.

Characters

  • Uesugi Kenshin　The warlord of Echigo, the hero of the work, who fights free of private interest, for a just cause and for good faith
  • Takeda Shingen　The warlord of Kai. Kenshin's great enemy and a rival each of them acknowledges
  • Uesugi Norimasa　The deputy shogun for the Kantō, who takes refuge with Kenshin in Echigo
  • Konoe Sakitsugu　Heir of the regental house, a court noble who visits Kenshin
  • Ashikaga Yoshiteru and Yoshiaki　Shoguns of the Muromachi bakufu, the lords Kenshin serves faithfully all his life
  • Oda Nobunaga　The warlord hurrying to unify the realm, whose opposition to Kenshin deepens

The opening (original text)

There is a worth in living With the coming of this new year, Kenshin turned thirty-three. He might still be called a young man. And yet his colors and everything he wore were of a very sober taste. Even his long-sleeved haori was plain, a warbler-brown of wild silk.

Uesugi kenshin by Yoshikawa Eiji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Kenshin was in the ordinary way a strong and vigorous man, but he loved his wine. The saddle cup he is said to have used brought tears from his family and his retainers long afterward. The everyday cups of his that remain at the Uesugi Shrine to this day are of a fearful size. He had no particular taste in food to go with it, and is said to have drunk enormous quantities with nothing but a pickled plum, so that his cheerful drinking is not hard to imagine. Fixed since ancient days, it comes round again to the four quarters — ruling and flourishing, the white snow of a thousand ages. That is an early poem of his. It is said he composed it in his youth, when he went up to the capital and spent a night viewing the snow with the shogun Yoshiteru. Setting it against the beauty of a world all one color of snow, he gives out the spirit of restoring the old order that he already held in his breast; and he lets fall a view of the national polity purer than snow itself. Yoshiteru at that time was a shogun of only nineteen. Whether he was able to take in Kenshin's ideal is not known. But when Yoshiteru died a violent death and the next shogun, Yoshiaki, came in, Kenshin's good faith did not change. He lent great strength, without display, in propping up the falling Muromachi shogunate. Because of it he came into opposition with Nobunaga. Nobunaga was a destroyer of exactly the opposite kind. The clash that had to come was fought fiercely, in diplomacy and in war.

Uesugi kenshin by Yoshikawa Eiji (Aozora Bunko)
