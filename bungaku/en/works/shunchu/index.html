About this work

A fantastical novel set on a spring day at Zushi in Sagami, published in Shinshōsetsu in November 1906. What the strolling narrator is told by the abbot at a hermitage of a mountain temple is that a young man who lodged there the year before pined for a married woman he had merely passed on the road, consummated the union in a dream, and vanished into the sea. Its telling, in which the border of dream and waking dissolves, is called the high point of Kyōka's work, and it makes one piece with the sequel published the following month.

Summary

In the full brightness of a spring day, a man known only as "the Stroller" sets out on an aimless walk from Kamakura toward Zushi. Along the way he tells a farmer in the fields that he has seen a great serpent, then climbs the stone steps to Ganshō-ji, a temple nestled in the mountains. On a pillar of the Kannon hall hangs a sheet of paper inscribed with Ono no Komachi's poem of a fleeting nap-time dream. Invited into the temple's hermitage, the Stroller listens as the priest tells him of something that happened the year before. A young man from Tokyo who had once lodged in this hermitage had fallen desperately in love with Miwo, wife of the wealthy local assemblyman Tamawaki Sainosuke, though he had passed her on the road no more than two or three times. One night he dreamed that a stage had suddenly appeared in a cave called the Serpent's Turret, deep in a ravine behind the hill, and that he had performed a play there together with her. Seeking to dream the same dream once more, he went back to the ravine — and was later found dead, washed up from the sea.

Characters

  • The Stroller　A traveler who walks from Kamakura to Zushi. The narrator of this story
  • The Priest　The head of the hermitage at Ganshō-ji. Tells the Stroller of the events of the year before
  • The Hermitage's Lodger　A young man from Tokyo who had once lodged at the hermitage. Meets his beloved in a dream and dies in the sea
  • Tamawaki Miwo　Wife of the wealthy assemblyman Tamawaki Sainosuke. Known locally for her beauty

The opening (original text)

"Old man! Old man!" "Eh — me?" He answered at once with that single word, no doubt because all around was so quiet, with no one else near. Otherwise, with the mild spring sun full upon his wrinkled brow beneath its loosely bound headcloth, his face flushed as though with drink, working his hoe in such unhurried, drowsy fashion — the soft earth beneath seeming almost ready to bead with sweat, ready to go fluttering off into a scattering crimson sunset — while a bird perched among the warm peach blossoms, setting them trembling as if aflame, chattered on as though telling some tale of its own, he had the dazed look of a man who, even on hearing a human voice, would not at once realize it was calling to him.

Shunchū by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

He must have wanted to see that play again, the one from that night. His body was found in the sea. As for the Serpent's Turret — it's an old cave at the foot of the second rise of this hill behind us, with water pooled inside. Shout into it, and the echo comes back — oh — spreading endlessly into the depths, they say ten miles and more. It's told that the water runs all the way to the sea, though who can say if that's so." Rain came sweeping in from the direction of the upper story. Only its sound could be heard; it left the grass undampened, trailing a hem as though passing along the road. It must have been the spirit of the beautiful woman, drawn near. Cloud-black hair, a robe the color of peach blossom, a butterfly in tow above the rape flowers — she came into the garden, stood beside the heat-shimmer, and quietly peered in at the window.
