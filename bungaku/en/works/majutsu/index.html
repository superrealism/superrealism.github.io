About this work

A parable-like short story about magic, published in the children's magazine Akai Tori. The narrator is taught a strange magic by Mr Misra, an Indian living at Ōmori, fails to keep the one condition — to give up desire — and, beaten by greed for money, loses the art. A lesson about the weakness of desire in the human heart, enjoyed alongside a fantastical device, and loved with "The Spider's Thread" and "Toshishun" among Akutagawa's stories for children.

Summary

On an evening of drizzling rain, the narrator calls on Matiram Misra, an Indian patriot living at Ōmori. Misra is a master of magic who has learned the secret arts of Hassan Khan, and he shows one wonder after another: he makes the flame of a lamp dance in the air, and the books on the desk walk about by themselves. Full of admiration, the narrator asks to be taught; and Misra, warning him first that to use his magic one must throw away desire, teaches him. A month later, having mastered it, the narrator gathers his friends and shows what he can do. But when he has turned the coals in the grate into gold coins, his friends egg him on and he begins to gamble at cards with the gold. And at the moment when a great heap of money lies before him and he reaches for a card, wanting only to win — he finds himself in Misra's house, with the clock still showing the hour before the lesson. It had all been a moment's illusion. Unable to give up his desire, he had not been fit to use the magic; and Misra quietly tells him so.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, taught magic by Misra, who loses it because he cannot master his greed for money
  • Matiram Misra　An Indian patriot living at Ōmori, a master of magic who has learned the secret arts of Hassan Khan

It was one evening when a cold rain was falling. The rickshaw carrying me went up and down the steep slopes of the Ōmori district again and again, and at last set down its shafts in front of a small Western-style house surrounded by a bamboo grove.

The ending (original text)

"If you wish to use my magic, you must first throw away desire. You have not carried your discipline that far." Misra, with a look of pity in his eyes, rested his elbow on the tablecloth with the red flower pattern woven along its border, and admonished me quietly in these words.
