About this work

Mikami Otokichi's translation of the Sherlock Holmes story (1903) known in Japan today as "Odoru Ningyō." Holmes sees that the scribbles of little dancing men on walls and window frames are a cipher, and breaks it by fitting in the letters of the English alphabet in order of frequency; his solution is famous as a classic of the cipher story. With Poe's "The Gold-Bug" it made frequency analysis widely known, and it was translated in the early Shōwa years as a volume of Heibonsha's "Complete Detective Stories of the World."

Summary

The story opens with Holmes, absorbed in a chemical experiment, suddenly naming what Watson has been thinking about his investments. Then Hilton Cubitt, a landowner from Norfolk, calls. His American-born wife Elsie asked him at their marriage never to question her about her past, and he agreed. But lately, he says, his wife has begun to be frightened after reading a letter from America, and scribbles like little dancing men have appeared on the window frames and the sundial of the house. Holmes takes away the figures copied onto slips of paper and sets to work on them by fitting in the letters of the English alphabet in order of frequency. As the messages begin to make sense, they turn into threats aimed at the wife. Hearing that matters are urgent, he hurries to the house, but by the time he arrives Cubitt has been shot dead and his wife gravely wounded. The police take it for a murder and suicide; but Holmes works out from the number of bullets and the state of the window that a third person was there. And then, using the cipher he has broken in reverse, he lures the culprit, Abe Slaney, to the house with a letter in dancing men.

Characters

  • Sherlock Holmes　He breaks the cipher of the dancing men by frequency analysis
  • Watson　The narrator
  • Hilton Cubitt　A landowner of Norfolk, who comes to consult him about his wife's fear
  • Elsie　His wife, who will not speak of her past in America
  • Abe Slaney　The man who has followed her from Chicago, and the writer of the cipher

The opening (original text)

Holmes had been seated for some hours in silence, with his long, thin back curved over a chemical vessel in which he was brewing a particularly malodorous product. His head was sunk upon his breast, and he looked from my point of view like a strange, lank bird with dull gray plumage and a black top-knot. "So, Watson," said he suddenly, "you do not propose to invest in South African securities?" I gave a start of astonishment. Accustomed as I was to Holmes's curious faculties, this sudden intrusion into my most intimate thoughts was utterly inexplicable. "How on earth do you know that?" I asked.

The ending (original text)

"There is a train at three-forty. We shall be in time for dinner at Baker Street, I think." Only a word of epilogue. The American, Abe Slaney, was condemned to death at the winter assizes at Norwich; but his penalty was changed to penal servitude in consideration of mitigating circumstances, and of the certainty that Hilton Cubitt had fired the first shot. Of Mrs. Hilton Cubitt I have only heard that she recovered entirely, that she has remained a widow, and that she devotes her whole life to the care of the poor and to the administration of her husband's estate.
