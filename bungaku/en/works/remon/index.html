About this work

A short story published in the first issue of the coterie magazine Aozora in 1925. Wandering Kyoto in the grip of a nameless unease, the narrator finds a moment's fullness in a single lemon, and leaves it on a shelf in the Maruzen bookshop as an imagined bomb. It reflects the author's tuberculosis and poverty closely, yet its acutely observed colour and texture lift it into something universal. The best known of Kajii's short pieces.

Summary

"I," with an unaccountable ominous lump pressing on my mind and no longer able to take in the music and poetry I once loved, wander the back streets of Kyoto as the attraction of shabby but beautiful things leads me. One day, buying a single lemon at a fruiterer's, "I" find in its coldness and colour and smell a moment's ease of mind. Going into Maruzen, where "I" once went often and which now feels oppressive, "I" build a strange castle out of piled art books, set the lemon quietly on its summit, and, imagining it a bomb, leave the shop without buying anything. Following the odd fancy that in ten minutes Maruzen will blow up, "I" go down the street of Kyōgoku.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, tormented by an unaccountable unease and wandering the streets of Kyoto. He has tuberculosis and debts

The opening (original text)

An unaccountable ominous lump had been pressing down on my mind all the while. Call it impatience, call it disgust — as there is a hangover after drinking, so if you drink every day there comes a period that corresponds to the hangover. That was what had come.

Lemon by Kajii Motojirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

A strangely ticklish feeling made me smile there in the street. To think that the strange villain who had planted a fearful bomb glittering golden on the shelves of Maruzen was myself, and that in ten minutes that Maruzen would blow up with the art shelves at its centre — how amusing it would be. I pursued the fancy eagerly. "And then that oppressive Maruzen will be smashed to powder." And I went on down Kyōgoku, where the painted signboards of the picture houses gave the street their odd flavour.

Lemon by Kajii Motojirō (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

A nameless, ominous lump was forever pressing down upon my heart.

A vivid line that gives an unfathomable melancholy the name of an ‘ominous lump.’ It opens a story of a young man's oppressed heart and the momentary release brought by a single lemon.

How wonderful it would be if I were the strange villain who had planted a terrible bomb, gleaming golden, on the shelves of Maruzen, and in ten minutes' time the whole store, centered on the art section, would go up in a great explosion.

The scene in which he sets a lemon atop a stack of art books and walks away. A fantasy that imagines the fruit as a bomb releases, if only for a moment, his oppressed heart.

I like lemons, I really do. That simple color, like lemon-yellow paint squeezed from a tube and left to harden, and that compact, spindle-shaped form.

The narrator's musing on finding lemons at a greengrocer's. The color like squeezed-out paint hardened in place, the spindle shape: his fondness for a simple object loosens, for a moment, his oppressed heart.

Looking it over, I saw that the lemon's color had quietly drawn the jangling scale of colors into its spindle-shaped body and stood there ringing clear — kaan.

The moment he sets the lemon on top of a castle of art books piled up at Maruzen. A single fruit absorbs the clutter of colors and rings clear — a celebrated sentence that fixes the tension of the scene with one onomatopoeic word.
