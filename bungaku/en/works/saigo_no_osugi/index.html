About this work

Uchida Roan's recollections of the last month of the anarchist Ōsugi Sakae, whom he spent it near as a neighbour at the same address before he was murdered. The doting father pushing a pram, the days under the earthquake, and his violent death in the Amakasu affair are drawn quietly, together with the innocent words of his small daughter Mako, and at the end Roan calmly dismisses the rumour current at the time that Ōsugi had been a spy.

Summary

Uchida Roan recalls the last days of the anarchist Ōsugi Sakae, killed by the military police in the confusion of the great earthquake, as a neighbor who happened to live at the same address. It begins with their meeting again when Ōsugi, sent back from France, moved to Kashiwagi and appeared with his wife Itō Noe and the little Mako and Louise; and it draws his easy talk of drinking white wine in a Paris prison, and the figure of a father fond of his children, pushing the pram about the neighborhood. Then the earthquake of the first of September, Ōsugi standing night watch amid the wild rumors, and the man who laughed and said, "It is no use taking care; when they take you, they take you" — who went out with Noe on the sixteenth of September and was never seen again, until the killing was made public as the Amakasu affair; and all of it is set down crossing with the innocent air of Mako, who came to play. "They have killed Papa and Mama too," the child repeats, and it rings piercingly. In a postscript Roan examines calmly, from the man's character and his daily life, the popular story that Ōsugi was a police spy, and rejects it: a man so direct and impulsive could never have served as an informer.

Characters

  • Ōsugi Sakae　An anarchist, killed under the confusion of the earthquake by the hand of Captain Amakasu Masahiko of the military police. He was the author's neighbor
  • Itō Noe　Ōsugi's wife, from the Seitō society. She was killed with him
  • Mako　The daughter of Ōsugi and Noe, who came to the author's house to play every day
  • His nephew Tachibana Sōichi　The small nephew killed together with Ōsugi and Noe
  • Uchida Roan　The author, neighbor of the Ōsugi household

The opening (original text)

Ōsugi and I were not what one calls close friends. But that we lived our last month at the same address must have been some sort of tie. Ōsugi first came to me soon after he had come out of his cell life following the Red Flag affair. After I moved to Yodobashi our houses were near, and he came often. We talked of thought, and we talked of socialism; but since it was not that our hearts were laid open to each other, it was mostly gossip of the literary world and of the town. Ōsugi's interests were fairly wide and he had plenty to talk about. Of late he had been busily translating Fabre; but he had had that sort of literary and scholarly interest from early on, and was of the temper of a man of letters rather than of a man of a cause.

Saigo no ōsugi by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Whether it was a play or what, I do not know; but Ōsugi died as an anarchist. Against the background of a great destruction by nature such as is seen but once in a hundred years, and by the hand of a soldier with a great army behind him, the curtain was brought down on an end befitting an anarchist. The fate of Jaurès, who was the blood offering at the opening of the European war, was much like it, and at the time of the killing Ōsugi was often compared with Jaurès; and Jaurès's remains, they say, are now being talked of for the Panthéon. Ōsugi, who wandered in the void even when he was bones, will never come up in the world again; but that he became, whether in gray or in brown, a great figure of history may be at least some satisfaction.
