About this work

A short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the Yomiuri Shimbun in August 1895. A family of unknown origins moves into a quiet rented house in Koishikawa, and with them the beautiful Yukiko, whose mind is failing after a disappointment in love. As the title — the cast-off shell of a cicada — suggests, it draws with pathos a girl left an empty husk by lost love, through her gradual sinking into madness in the heat of summer.

Summary

Into a quiet house to let near the botanical garden at Koishikawa there moves a family whose circumstances no one knows. There are the master, obliging but never quite to the point, two people who seem to be his aged parents, and Yukiko, a beautiful and sickly girl of about eighteen, pale as if transparent. Yukiko had pledged herself to a man named Uemura, but the family's circumstances would not allow the love, and out of the heartbreak of the broken engagement she gradually loses her mind. As the heat of summer grows, her derangement grows more violent, and she no longer answers when her family speak to her, but only weeps and says, "I do not know." In the middle of August the madness rises further, and as Yukiko goes many nights without sleep, repeating as though in delirium, "I have met Uemura," her family have nothing left to do but stay beside her.

Characters

  • Yukiko　The beautiful, sickly girl who has moved into the house at Koishikawa. The heroine, who loses her mind when she is parted from Uemura, whom she loved
  • Uemura　The man to whom Yukiko pledged herself. Parted from her by the family's circumstances, he never appears in person, and lives on in her obsession
  • Takichi　The bustling manservant of Yukiko's house, who acts as the family's factotum

The opening (original text)

The house has five rooms, counting the three-mat entrance; small, but it takes the wind well from north and south, and the garden is wide, and the trees in the planting are thick, so that it looks made for living in through the summer; and the place too, near the botanical garden at Koishikawa, is quiet — a house to let with a little inconvenience for its only flaw and nothing else to be said against it.

Utsusemi by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Perhaps some day she will come to her senses, as one waking from a dream, and there will be a time when she says father, mother; and though it is doubtful, one waits a day and another day, and looks at the empty cicada shell and takes comfort from it. Ah, would that the sound of the autumn wind were not heard in the willow at the gate.

Utsusemi by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)
