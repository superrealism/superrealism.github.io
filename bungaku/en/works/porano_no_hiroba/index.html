About this work

With "Night on the Galactic Railroad" and "Matasaburō of the Wind," one of Miyazawa Kenji's central long children's stories. In the city of Morio in Ihatovo, Kyūsuto, a young official of the natural history bureau, and the boy Fazēro and his friends wander the fields in search of the fabled square of Polano. The story, running from a beautiful May to October, turns in time from finding the ideal square to building one with their own hands. Famous too for the "Song of the Square of Polano" at the end.

Summary

Leōno Kyūsto, an official of the eighteenth rank in the museum bureau of the city of Morīo, lives alone in the keeper's hut at an old racecourse and keeps goats. One Sunday in May, going out into the fields after a goat that has run away, he makes the acquaintance of Mīro the shepherd boy and a boy named Fazēro. Fazēro tells him that if you follow the numbers on the clover flowers you can reach the legendary "square of Polāno," where the young people are said to have gathered long ago and sung in contest. Kyūsto goes out with them into the fields by night to look for the square. But the place they reach at last is not the legendary square: it is a rowdy banqueting ground stinking of drink, where the landlord Destupāgo — the adviser to the industrial cooperative, called the Wildcat Doctor — is handing out liquor for the sake of an election. In anger at seeing his ideal fouled, Fazēro quarrels with the doctor and disappears. In time the doctor's corruption comes to light and he falls, and Fazēro and the others who had vanished come back. They no longer try to look for the legendary square. They will start work with their own hands and make the true square of Polāno themselves. Seven years later, after much hardship, their cooperative is running well, and sends out ham and leather and oatmeal to many places. One day a sheet of music arrives for Kyūsto, who is working in the city of Tokīo. It is "The Song of the Square of Polāno," arranged so that everyone can hold it in his hands and sing.

Characters

  • Leōno Kyūsto　An official of the eighteenth rank in the museum bureau of the city of Morīo. The narrator of the story
  • Fazēro　The boy who looks for the square of Polāno, and later starts the industrial cooperative
  • Rozāro　Fazēro's elder sister
  • Mīro　A shepherd boy
  • Destupāgo　A landlord and adviser to the cooperative, called the Wildcat Doctor

The opening (original text)

In those days I was working in the museum bureau of the city of Morīo. Since I was an official of the eighteenth rank I was a long way down within the office, and my salary was very small indeed; but as my charge was the collecting and arranging of specimens, which was work I liked by nature, I worked every day with a good deal of pleasure. That transparent wind of Ihatovo, the blue sky that keeps a coolness at the bottom of it even in summer, the city of Morīo adorned with beautiful woods, the glittering waves of grass in the suburbs.

Porāno no hiroba by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The Song of the Square of Polāno In the night square where the clover lights its lamps singing to one another the largo of long ago, shaking even the clouds, forgetting in the night wind, the harvest close at hand, the year is ripe. Though we should quarrel over a rightful wish, beyond the Milky Way we shall laugh together; burning all our troubles for our firewood, let us build together a world of glory. I thought that the music was certainly Fazēro's own making.

Porāno no hiroba by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

That clear, transparent wind of Ihatovo; the blue sky that keeps a coolness in its depths even in summer; the city of Morīo, adorned with beautiful woods; the glittering waves of grass on its outskirts.

A passage in which the narrator, Kyūsto, fondly recalls the days he spent in the city of Morīo. The light and wind of Ihatovo, the land Kenji imagined as an ideal place, are called up vividly in a chain of limpid phrases.
