About this work

A novel Futabatei Shimei published late in life, serialized in the Tokyo Asahi Shimbun from October to December 1907. Exactly as his own note on it says — an ordinary man describing an ordinary half-life with an ordinary pen — a dull minor official looks back with self-mockery, in first-person confession, on the literature he once meant to pursue and on his own mediocrity. Known as a light satire on the ponderous confessional literature of the naturalism then in fashion.

Summary

"I," a petty official of thirty-nine, begin to look back, with irony and self-mockery, on a commonplace life in which I once aspired to literature and never came to anything. Listening with half an ear while an old friend from the literary world calls and tells with an air of importance the gossip and the theories of that world, my thoughts go back to memories of childhood — my strict parents, and Pochi, the dog I loved. Following the course of my own life, "I" set literature down as after all no more than a product of the imagination, and hold at arm's length, sharply and yet somehow airily, the self that gave itself up to it and lived floating away from reality.

The opening (original text)

I am thirty-nine this year. If fifty is the going rate for a human life, I do not expect to go into a hole today or tomorrow; but the future, long as it looks, is short. Once past, it is over in no time at all. While one is still saying not yet, not yet, a gap opens in this world before one knows it, and the time comes to say farewell. When that time comes, no amount of struggling and floundering will avail. If one is to make up one's mind, now is the time. But then I have grown old. Some will say that thirty-nine is a little early to be growing old; but it is better to carry oneself as older than one's years. That way one is safe.

Heibon by Futabatei Shimei (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

What literature is, exactly, I do not know. From what people say, it seems to be a thing that has the imagination for its life. Even if the nature and the life that appear in a literary work are what the author has actually felt in touching life and nature directly, still, once they are made to appear again by the imagination, they are not the real thing. However closely it is copied, it is not the real thing. It is the shadow of the real thing, and holds an element of imagination. In the feeling one gets from touching it there is somewhere a play; that is, a literary work cannot but hold an element of play. That one cannot get from it the sharp feeling of touching real life and real nature is only natural. May it not have been the inevitable result that, because I was always soaking in this sort of feeling and never tightened my heart with real feeling, I went slack and soft, and a worthless fellow grew more and more worthless? If so, then even a lofty and pure literature will ruin a man if he drowns in nothing but this. How much more, when it is undisciplined men writing undisciplined things that make up the literary world of every age and........................ (The End) Futabatei speaks. This manuscript I picked up while idling at a night stall, but the rest is torn away and missing. It is rather as if the telephone had been cut off in the middle of the conversation; but there is no help for it.

Heibon by Futabatei Shimei (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

I turn thirty-nine this year. If fifty is the going rate for a human life, I do not expect to be in my grave tomorrow or the day after, but even so, the future, however long it looks, is short.

A colloquial opening that begins to tell the story of an ordinary half-life. It conveys the free, easy narrative voice that Shimei, who staked his career on genbun'itchi prose, arrived at in his later years.
