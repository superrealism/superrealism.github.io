About this work

Mikami Otokichi's long novel, serialized in the "Asahi Shimbun" from 1934 into the following year, which became a representative work of popular fiction in the Shōwa era. The son of a merchant house in Nagasaki that was brought to ruin goes up to Edo as Nakamura Yukinojō, a celebrated onnagata of the Kamigata Kabuki, and closes in on the three men who destroyed his family, using the fame of the stage as his weapon. It is a story of revenge, and yet it gains its depth from the twist by which the hero, an onnagata standing between the false and the true, is loved by the daughter of the man he hates. Filmed again and again with Hasegawa Kazuo and Misora Hibari among others, its very title became a byword for the period drama.

Summary

Late autumn in Edo. A young onnagata walking through Komagata in a woman's dress and a purple kerchief draws the eyes of every girl on the road. This is Nakamura Yukinojō, the popular actor of Nakamura Kikunojō's company come down from the Kamigata. But he was not born to the theater. Raised as the son of a great merchant of Nagasaki, he had a father who was talked into a scheme by three men — Dobe Sansai, Hiromiya and Nagasakiya — and who was left to carry the whole guilt and died mad in a back tenement. In the precincts of the Hachiman shrine the words of the eccentric Kōken-sensei push him on, and the thought of revenge he has been holding down flares up. With the arm he has trained under the swordsman Wakita Isshōsai and with the fame of the stage for his weapons, Yukinojō works his way into his enemies one by one. The chivalrous thief Yamitarō and the woman thief Ohatsu lend him their strength, and the rōnin Kadokura Heima stands in his way. Before long Namiji, the daughter of Dobe Sansai, comes to love Yukinojō without knowing that he is her father's enemy, and the road of revenge twists in a direction he had not foreseen. Bringing down the fortunes of Hiromiya and Nagasakiya and tightening his grip on Sansai's heart, Yukinojō moves to his last stroke. Having done what he set out to do, he takes leave of Edo and goes west; and five years later, the story tells us, he ended his young life, mourned by many.

Characters

  • Nakamura Yukinojō　A popular onnagata come down from the Kamigata, who is after the three men who drove his father to his death in Nagasaki
  • Dobe Sansai　Once governor of Nagasaki, and Yukinojō's chief enemy
  • Hiromiya and Nagasakiya　The merchants who joined with Sansai to ruin Yukinojō's father
  • Namiji　Dobe Sansai's daughter, who loves Yukinojō without knowing he is her father's enemy
  • Yamitarō　A chivalrous thief who lends Yukinojō his strength
  • Ohatsu　A woman thief, one of Yamitarō's companions
  • Nakamura Kikunojō　The master who took Yukinojō in and brought him up

The opening (original text)

The Onnagata 1 A clear day at the end of autumn had worn on to dusk, and the evening glow that had tinted the sky was fading little by little. From the direction of Imado in Asakusa, through the quiet streets of Komagata, a beautiful young man in the dress of an onnagata was hurrying toward Kuramae with quick little steps — a wig-cloth on his head, a purple kerchief over it, a glimpse of red at the collar and the cuffs, winter peonies under snow embroidered boldly on the deep black hem, a haori of wisteria purple with a folded-crane crest, and clogs clacking as he went. He had a manservant behind him carrying what looked like a gift cask of sake, and the figure walking with his face rather lowered had a grace rare even in a delicate woman. Quiet as the street was, at this hour before dark Komagata had people enough coming and going; and among them the young girls could hardly fail to notice this gentle figure, blossoming out of nowhere before their eyes.

The ending (original text)

Yamitarō, however, was another matter. When this run of performances was over he would be the one to go west with Yukinojō, who, sorry as he would be to leave it, was to return from this great city of Edo to the Kamigata. "I am an Edo man myself. It is hard to give up the place I come from, but I have come to feel that I will go anywhere you are" — so he had said, putting his hands to the floor meekly, when Yukinojō urged him to it on that night of danger. Here this story ends. But one sad postscript must be set down. Yukinojō, who had so fine a nature, was perhaps begrudged his beauty by heaven: five years later, bathed in the hot tears of the Kyoto and Osaka public who loved him, his fragrant spirit went back to the sky. It seems that when a man has finished a work he has longed for with all his heart, he does not live long afterward. (The End)
