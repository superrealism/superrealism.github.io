About this work

A novella Satō Haruo published in Chūgai in 1918. A young poet with damaged nerves flees the din of the city and moves with his wife and his dog and cat into a decayed country house at the edge of Musashino, only to find that the rural quiet he longed for sinks him instead into tedium, melancholy and hallucination. As the line about the sick rose suggests — he sees the sign of disease in the roses that have bloomed in the garden — he lays his own sick soul over the sick rose. A central work of Taishō literature, drawing the nerves and tedium of a modern person with acute sensibility and an aesthete's style, out of the author's own experience of living in the suburbs.

Summary

A young poet, "he," his nerves worn ragged, flees the suffocating life of the city and, together with his wife, two dogs, and a cat, moves into a decrepit thatched-roof house in the wild, overgrown outskirts to the south of Musashino. He had longed for a tranquil life amid quiet nature, for an ecstasy like a deep, dreamless sleep. But the rural seclusion he had wished for, once gained, does nothing to heal his heart — instead it drags him down into a nameless lassitude and melancholy, and into the hallucinations and phantom sounds bred by nerves grown too sensitive. A heart that will not meet his wife's, a clinging tedium, the oppressive weight of the damp green all around, the ceaseless crowing of chickens in the garden — his inner life is eaten away by degrees, and he sinks ever deeper into gloom. One day he notices, in the roses he has tended so carefully in the garden, the first faint signs of blight. In that instant a line of verse comes to him, pursuing him like a revelation, refusing to let him go: "O rose, thou art sick!" As though laying his own diseased soul over the sickened rose, the young man's melancholy reaches its extremity. Drawing on the author's own experience of life in the suburbs, this piece — rendered in delicate sensibility and an aesthetic prose style — crystallizes the nervous exhaustion and ennui of modern man, and stands as a representative work of Taishō-era "mind" fiction.

Characters

  • He　A young poet whose nerves are worn ragged. He flees the city for the countryside, only to sink into ennui, melancholy, and hallucination
  • His wife　The young wife who follows her husband to the country house. She cannot fathom the moods of her difficult, elusive husband, and is left troubled and uncertain
  • Frate and Leo　The two dogs he brings with him. They settle quickly into their new home

The opening (original text)

The house now appeared before his eyes. At first his two dogs had run about in high spirits, kicking up clouds of dust, darting ahead of their master and falling behind him and twining about his legs, but by now they had grown obedient, and walked quietly along behind him, side by side. Just as the path curved sharply beneath the tall trees, their guide — a plump, ruddy-haired woman — said, "Ah, here we are at last," wiping with one hand the sweat trickling down her sunburnt brow with a soiled cloth, and with the other pointing on ahead to where they were going.

The ending (original text)
