About this work

A novella Okamoto Kanoko published in Bungakukai in 1937. The mother Kanojo, who went to Paris with her husband, the painter Ippaku, to study art, left her only son Ichirō — modelled on Okamoto Tarō, and set on being a painter — alone in Paris when they returned. Around her inexhaustible longing for that son, her affection for Kasuga Kikuo, a young man she once cared for, twines and is sublimated into maternal feeling. Overflowing maternal love, and a romantic feeling that is enfolded into it, sung in a gorgeous, dense style: one of Kanoko's central works.

Summary

Kano-jo, a novelist steeped in Mahayana philosophy, had spent several years together with her painter husband Issaku in Paris, that capital of the arts. But when the time came to return to Japan, she was forced, with a heart-rending grief, to leave behind alone in Paris her only son Ichirō, who had immersed himself deeply in the new art movements and put down roots in that foreign soil. "Sad it is that I, mother to a child of this fleeting world, should have no child within my reach to touch" — back in Japan, Kano-jo, her breast burning with longing for the son she cannot reach, returns again and again in memory to the rich, dense days she once spent with him in Paris. Students glimpsed from a bus window, the ailing son of a social worker met at a café — everything that meets her eye comes to overlap with the bond of mother and child. And at the very edge of the strict, pure core of her motherhood there still lingers, faint as a fragrance, indelible as a stain, the image of a young man to whom she had once given her heart — Kasuga Kikuo. One day, Kikuo, having purchased a painting her son Ichirō exhibited at a solo show — calling it "a painting by a son as miraculously happy as this" — sends her a letter after a long silence. Kikuo, she learns, has become a researcher in physics in Sendai, and remains unmarried still. Kano-jo no longer means to write him back, but her vast motherhood — having seen her grown son off into the world, having exhausted every hardship and every parting — gently opens its arms even to the image of that young man as well. A representative work in which the very essence of Kanoko's literature — a love that folds even romantic longing within maternal love — crystallizes into a dense, lyrical style.

Characters

  • Kano-jo　A novelist steeped in Mahayana philosophy. Carries within her motherhood both her longing for the son she left in Paris and her tender affection for a young man from her past
  • Ichirō　Kano-jo's only son. Aspires to be a painter and remains alone in Paris to continue his artistic training (modeled on Okamoto Tarō)
  • Issaku　Kano-jo's husband. A painter. Concerned for his wife's melancholy, he often takes her out
  • Kasuga Kikuo　A young man to whom Kano-jo once gave her heart. Later becomes a researcher in physics. Purchases one of Ichirō's paintings

The opening (original text)

Kano-jo had gone out ahead of him into the garden before the entrance, and stood waiting for her husband Issaku to come out. The fierce wind, characteristic of spring, which had begun to die down around suppertime, had now settled completely, leaving behind traces of its violence only in the spots where it had blown scraps and leaves into drifts. The plants of the small front garden, some ten tsubo in size, stood out sharply stemmed, like specimens in a glass case, their forms cut clean in the especially bright light before dusk. "It's an evening just like a vacuum."

Boshi jojō by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Should she now, once more, draw the image of Kikuo out into the very core of herself? — Now that she had seen her son safely through to full maturity, her motherhood, having exhausted every hardship and every parting, would surely, rather, hold out its hand gently to welcome it in, and, just as her husband Issaku might say, "You too knew hardship while you were young. It would do no harm to chase after what remains of a dream once glimpsed and lost." Would she really speak to herself so understandingly? Should clouds hang over the road you walk, ask of that cloud; should snow pile there, ask the snow — ask it of me. Since you went, my heart too has darkened; the snow that they say falls white as mulberry cloth at night looks black to me.

Boshi jojō by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)
