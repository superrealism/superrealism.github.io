About this work

A very early piece by Hayama Yoshiki, written in June 1921 while he was working at a cement factory in Nagoya and taking part in the labour movement. Cast as a call of "Brothers!" to his fellows in the same factory, it protests about the four hundred yen paid to a workmate killed in an industrial accident and to his family, and about how they themselves are treated like beads on an abacus. Unlike his later stance as a proletarian writer, it is strongly coloured by a Christianity that preaches non-resistance and the coming of the kingdom of God. An interesting piece for understanding where he began.

Summary

'Brothers! We must wake up now. It's five in the morning' — so it begins, calling out to fellow workers heading into the factory. In just these past few days there have been many injuries and one death. A comrade who broke his leg was sent home to his lodgings with the leg not yet fully healed, and one foreman says of him, 'He'd been out drinking and lay down for a rest right under a pile of cement bags that was bound to collapse.' Saying so, of course, lets the company avoid treating it as an official on-the-job injury. On May nineteenth, a comrade who fell into hot ash and suffered severe burns left only the words, 'I beg you to see to the matter afterward,' and died cursing no one at all. Left to his wife and four children was the sum prescribed by the Factory Act — one hundred seventy days' wages, plus about a hundred yen — four hundred yen in total. The chapter that follows sets down the monologue of a single factory hand who supports a wife and three children on forty yen a month, and who has no recourse but to strike his child with his fist when the child begs for a moving picture. A child, too, who cannot wait to turn ten so he can become a factory hand, is made to shoulder that same dark fate. And the writer likens the workers to pickled daikon: the heavier the weight pressed down upon them, the more they crush against one another and give up their brine. But outside the barrel stand the abacus and the compass, gazing upon an altogether different world. Even so, he preaches not rebellion against the capitalists but the doctrine of non-resistance, declaring that the mission of bringing the 'union of the people through love' down to this earth has been given to the workers themselves, and lays down his pen with a line from the Lord's Prayer.

The opening (original text)

One Brothers! We must wake up now. It's five in the morning. We must rise and go to work at the factory. Otherwise mankind will suffer from a want of goods. Let us wait patiently. As we have always waited. For before long, the capitalists too will surely wake their own conscience.

Kōjō no mado yori by Hayama Yoshiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The more of us there are who cast off this delusion, the nearer the Kingdom of God draws. Brothers. Let us reject every temptation of the devil, and advance toward the Kingdom of God. For God and the devil dwell together within our own bodies, and so we must fix our eyes on God alone. Brothers. Let us come to know God, and bring the heaven of His holy name down upon this earth. Thy kingdom come, Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.

Kōjō no mado yori by Hayama Yoshiki (Aozora Bunko)
