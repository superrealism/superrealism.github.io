About this work

A short story for children and one of Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's most famous works, published in July 1918 in the first issue of Akai Tori, the children's literary magazine founded by Suzuki Miekichi. Taking its material from Buddhist legend, it follows a single spider's thread that the Buddha lowers from paradise to a great thief in hell, and turns it into a plain-spoken lesson in egoism and consequence. A fixture of Japanese school textbooks and the best known of Akutagawa's stories for children.

Summary

One day the Buddha, at the edge of the lotus pond in paradise, notices Kandata, a great thief, at the bottom of the pool of blood in hell. Kandata did much evil in his life, but once he spared a small spider he had been about to crush underfoot. Remembering that one good act, the Buddha lowers a single thread of the paradise lotus down into the pool of blood. Kandata sees it, rejoices, and begins to climb with all his strength — but partway up he looks down and sees countless sinners scrambling up the same thread behind him. Afraid it will break, he screams, "This thread is mine!" and tries to kick them back down. At that instant the thread snaps just above him, and he falls headlong into the pool of blood. The Buddha, who has watched it all, looks on Kandata's mercilessness with a sorrowful face and begins once more to walk at the edge of the lotus pond.

Characters

  • The Buddha　Looks down on hell from the lotus pond of paradise and lowers a thread of rescue to Kandata
  • Kandata　A great thief who did much evil in life and fell into the pool of blood in hell. One good act earns him a chance at salvation, which his own selfishness destroys

The opening (original text)

It happened one day. The Buddha was strolling alone at the edge of the lotus pond in paradise. The lotus flowers blooming in the pond were all white as jewels, and from the golden stamens at their centres an indescribably sweet fragrance welled out endlessly into the air. It must have been morning in paradise.

The ending (original text)

The Buddha stood at the edge of the lotus pond in paradise and watched it all; and when at last Kandata had sunk like a stone to the bottom of the pool of blood, he began to stroll on again with a sorrowful face. Kandata had tried to climb out of hell alone, and had fallen back into hell as that heart deserved: to the eyes of the Buddha it must have seemed a wretched thing. But the lotuses of the paradise pond took no notice at all. Their jewel-white flowers swayed on their calyxes about the Buddha's feet, and from the golden stamens at their centres an indescribably sweet fragrance welled out endlessly into the air. It must have been drawing on toward noon in paradise.

Famous passages

It was on a certain day. The Buddha was strolling alone along the edge of the lotus pond in Paradise.

An opening as gentle as a fairy tale, depicting a morning for the Buddha in Paradise. It begins a parable about a single thread lowered down into Hell.

Hey, you sinners! This spider's thread is mine. Who gave you leave to climb up on it? Get down. Get down!

The scene in which Kandata screams down at the sinners climbing the thread after him. At these very words the thread snaps, and he falls back down into Hell.

But the lotuses in the lotus pond of Paradise pay no heed at all to such things. Their jewel-like white blossoms sway their calyxes about the Buddha's feet, and from the golden stamens at their centers an indescribably sweet fragrance flows out endlessly all around. In Paradise, too, it must now be nearly noon.

The close, after the thread breaks and Kandata falls back into Hell. Depicting the calm of Paradise, wholly indifferent to the human tragedy, it returns in a circle to the lotus-pond scene of the opening.
