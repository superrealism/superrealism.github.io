About this work

The first collection of Tachihara Michizō, who died at twenty-four, designed and published at his own expense. Ten-line poems and sonnets are arranged like the movements of music, as Sonatine No. 1 and Sonatine No. 2, and the light and wind of the uplands at Shinano Oiwake, and the foretaste of meeting and parting, are sung in a whispering Japanese. "To One I Meet for the First Time" and "On Another Night" are among the most delicate crystals of lyricism in modern Japanese verse.

Summary

"A small convulsion of the earth, for its keepsake, / let ash fall on this village for a while" — the opening poem, "To One Met for the First Time," draws a night of talk in a village where the ash of Asama's eruption is falling. On the night when he leaned at the window and talked with someone, light and laughter overflowed into every corner of the room. Asking what it is to know a person's heart, what a person's heart is, the young man watches with wonder the movement of the other's hand chasing a moth. In the next poem, "And on Another Night," two people standing in the mist foresee that, without knowing or being known, they will part like clouds that have met. "We shall not meet a second time." In "On the Evening of a Late Day" he sings that a great turning is prepared and no one notices it; and in "At the Noon of Parting" young life is called upon: "Shake them, the green treetops; / pluck them, the green fruits of the tree." The book goes on to "SONATINE No. 2" and "Four Poems," where a summer's mourning, travel and forgetting return again and again as themes. "If only I remembered! I want to go back once more. Where? To that place." And in the last poem, "Having Forgotten," looking from the grass at the richly ripened grapes and figs and the flowing clouds, he murmurs, "I have been left behind, alone!" — and closes still believing in "only that gentleness which answers to quiet music."

The opening (original text)

SONATINE No. 1 To One Met for the First Time A small convulsion of the earth, for its keepsake, let ash fall on this village for a while the ash, like a sorrowful memory, made a sound as it came down and down on the treetops, on the roofs of the houses that night the moon was bright, but I leaned at the window and talked with someone (from that window the shape of the mountain could be seen)

The ending (original text)

Having Forgotten A deep autumn has come! (holding spring in it) The lake glitters and shines in the sun The birds, flying through the wide, wide sky, go toward the gorge along the beautifully colored flank of the mountain The grapes and the figs have ripened richly The harvest of the grain has already begun I have been left behind, alone!

Famous passages

My dreams always went back — to a lonely village at the foot of the mountains, where the wind rose over the knotweed flowers, where the meadow lark never ceased its song, along the forest path, hushed, in the afternoon.

The opening of “In Later Thought.” Four lines in which the poet, who died young at twenty-four, sets down a vanished time as the memory of a dream.
