About this work

One of Nakajima Atsushi's central works and a finalist for the fifteenth Akutagawa Prize, published in Bungakukai in May 1942 under the title "The Death of Tusitala" and later renamed. Partly in diary form, it follows the last years of Robert Louis Stevenson — author of Treasure Island and Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde — in Samoa, where he settled in search of health. Between civilization and what is called the primitive, and between an artist's calling and actual politics, it looks in dignified prose at the life and death of a writer whom the islanders loved as Tusitala, the teller of tales.

Summary

Robert Louis Stevenson, the English writer whose lungs are failing, moves from place to place in search of somewhere to recover, and settles at last on the South Pacific island of Samoa. Surrounded by the warm nature of the island and its simple people, he works at his writing in his house at Vailima and passes the days that are left to him. What his diary records is the struggle with a long novel that will not come, his grief for a South Sea culture that is passing away, and the ceaseless conflict between writing as an artist and living as a man. In time Stevenson is troubled by the politics of a Samoa played with by the great powers; he speaks up for islanders who are being exploited, comes into conflict with the colonial authorities, and involves himself deeply, as a writer, in the affairs of the place. The islanders called him Tusitala, the teller of tales, and loved him. Swaying between the ideal and the real, between civilization and what men call savagery, but faithful to the end both to writing and to living, he is carried off one day, suddenly, by a stroke. The islanders bury him in tears on the top of a mountain of the island, and say their farewell: "Tofa, Tusitala."

Characters

  • Robert Louis Stevenson　The English writer known for Treasure Island. Ill, he moves to Samoa in the South Seas and is loved by the islanders as Tusitala. The central figure of the work
  • Fanny　Stevenson's wife, who supports her sick husband and shares the life of the island

The opening (original text)

Late one night in May 1884, at a lodging in Hyères in the south of France, Robert Louis Stevenson, then thirty-five, was suddenly seized by a violent hemorrhage. To his wife, who came running, he wrote on a scrap of paper in pencil: "Do not be afraid. If this is death, it is an easy one." Blood filled his mouth and he could not speak.

Hikari to kaze to yume by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

One of the old chiefs, with the tears running down his wrinkled, copper-colored face — with the despairing grief that Southern peoples, drunk as they are on the joy of living, feel for that very reason toward death — murmured low: "Tofa — sleep! Tusitala."
