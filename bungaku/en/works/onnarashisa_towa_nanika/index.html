About this work

An essay in which Yosano Akiko answers head on those who would hold down the education and the work of women with the phrase losing one's womanliness. What is called womanliness, she argues, reduces in the end to humanity, and is not a supreme standard of value binding on women alone.

Summary

To those who argue that if women receive the same higher education and take up the same occupations as men, they will lose their "womanliness" and become some abnormal, intermediate species of human being, Yosano Akiko fires back a question: in a Japan that does not even grant ordinary women a middle-school education, let alone the qualification to sit as a town or village councillor, on what grounds can anyone deny that "equal results" would follow? And in the first place, what is the true nature of this "womanliness"? Walking with small, modest steps; being as docile as a doll — are these not simply a yardstick peculiar to Japan, one that carries no currency at all in Europe or America? Murasaki Shikibu once feigned ignorance of even the character for "one" while lecturing on Chinese poetry in secret, and no one has ever said that this made her any less a woman. There have been reigning empresses, women statesmen, and the two million Englishwomen who kept the munitions factories running in wartime. Love, grace, and modesty are not qualities to be demanded of women alone; they belong to human nature as a whole, shared equally by men and women both. Motherhood is no different: reproduction is a "human activity" belonging jointly to both father and mother, not a duty assigned to women alone. Dismantling each of these arguments in turn, Akiko shows just how thoroughly the single phrase "unwomanly" has suppressed the activity of women throughout history, and concludes that what truly terrifies people today is not that word at all, but another one: "inhuman."

The opening (original text)

We Japanese learned early on, through Buddhism, that this is a world of swift impermanence, and through Confucianism, that we ought to renew ourselves day by day and again day by day — and yet, because we have persisted in reading these teachings only in their pessimistic, Hinayana sense, we have never managed to stand upon the more optimistic, Mahayana ground from which the ceaseless flux of all things is itself seen as the abiding form of human life. Even now, in the present age, when contact with imported learning and art has brought us face to face with the idea of "flux and evolution," one still finds people who, at every turn, curse the new life of the modern world and cling instead to musty, conventional ways of thinking.

'Onnarashisa' towa nanika by Yosano Akiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

There is no telling just how much the single phrase "unwomanly" has, since ancient times, suppressed the activity of women. Habit is a stubborn thing, and even now, when told they are "unwomanly," some women still recoil, as startled and shrinking as if a snake had been flung at them. But the great majority of women today are no longer frightened by so small a word as "unwomanly." That is because they sense, instinctively, that there is a far more terrifying word than that — namely, that the ruin of one's humanity, expressed in the word "inhuman," is, above all else, the thing that truly terrifies people in this present age.

'Onnarashisa' towa nanika by Yosano Akiko (Aozora Bunko)
