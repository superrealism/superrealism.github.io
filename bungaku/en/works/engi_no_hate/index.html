About this work

Word reaches the narrator, who writes radio drama, that the actress Yasui Mariko, an old acquaintance, has swallowed two boxes of sleeping pills — a novella Yamakawa Masao published in Bungakukai. At the wake his friend Satō says flatly to her husband Yasui Susumu that in the end it was he who killed Mari. Yasui's rule, not to enter the locked-room parts of another person, was the narrator's own rule as well. The memory of having abandoned a woman in order to keep his own integrity drives him through a night of rain.

Summary

Opening the door of the reading room, "I" am told that Yasui Mariko, who was to have been in that evening's program, took two boxes of sleeping tablets this morning at her house in Nakano. Mariko had been in the drama club at the university with us, and married Yasui Susumu while still a student, and to anyone's eye they were a happy couple. In time her death is announced, a young understudy is put in, and the broadcast goes out as it always does. "I" noticed that I was not even trying to think about the cause. ... On the night of the wake the drunken Satō begins to press Yasui. He has never quarreled with her, he says, never even been angry; to throw raw feeling at another person is a kind of violence, and he has avoided it. To that Satō says: "Yours is a father's love, a guardian's love, the love of a man for his own toy. It is not a husband's." "What you loved was not Mari; it was that relation with Mari — it was only yourself in it." "I" listen without being able to leave my seat. For Yasui's creed, that a human being cannot get outside his own skin, was exactly the creed on which, two years ago, I had thrown over Oda Tomiko after four years, saying, "You stifle me." Two days later Hirayama of the theater company tells me alone: that Mariko died not in Nakano but at a hotel in Fujisawa; that a month ago the man had been himself; and that her husband, told everything, had said nothing but "And is that all?" — I am a murderer. Through the pouring rain "I" run to Kami-Meguro and wander the dark residential streets reading the name-plates "Oda" one by one by the light of matches, and end by being taken into a policeman's care. Some days later a letter comes from Tomiko: when she saw him at the funeral she thought, "Ah, Kubo — so he is still alive." At six, the hour she named, "I" leave my lodging; but my feet carry me as always past the bookshop and onto the bus for the broadcasting station.

Characters

  • I (Kubo)　A writer of radio plays
  • Yasui Mariko (Mari)　An actress, and a companion of mine since our student days
  • Yasui Susumu　Mariko's husband, the only son of the president of a provincial bank
  • Satō　A producer, and an old friend of mine and Mariko's
  • Hirayama　A member of the theater company
  • Oda Tomiko (Ton-ko)　The lover I parted from two years ago

The opening (original text)

The glare had been dazzling at midday, but before I knew it it had turned into a warm early-summer evening. A faint wind had come up as well, and walking along the street and looking up, there was still a pale blue clear sky above the buildings. "It was lovely today," said the woman with me, letting go of my arm as I stopped. "Particularly your appetite was lovely." "The sauce was well made, wasn't it — the veal cutlet."

The ending (original text)

Suddenly I was thinking: had I ever once sought a true love from Tomiko? I had always been desperately pursuing satisfactions of my own — the clearness of our positions, the outline of them, the distance and the balance of the relation — and in the end I could never become free of my concern for such things. ... At that moment an outrageous thought ran through my breast. Was it not rather Mariko that I had loved? — Either will do. Thinking so, I smiled bitterly. That too was probably nothing but nonsense, thought of because Mariko had died and because some outline was coming into being in my feeling for her. The bus was running along the wide asphalt road of early June, whitening toward dusk. It drew up to the sidewalk as if coming alongside a quay, and started again. The sun is about to set. Looking up at the clouded sky through the glass of the window, I thought: ah, the rainy season is about to begin.
