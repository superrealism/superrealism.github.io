About this work

Inoue Enryō's lectures on the study of monsters, serialized in the records of the Tetsugakukan. Dividing physical monsters such as fox-fire and mirages from psychological ones such as fox possession and prophecy, he explains one by one the reason behind the superstitions. The study of monsters as applied psychology, by the man they called the professor of monsters.

Summary

"The study of monsters," as one calls it, is not, in fact, any single, fully established discipline — Enryō begins his lectures with this qualification. His purpose is to gather up the facts about monsters, give them an explanation grounded in psychology, and so contribute to psychological research. First, monsters are broadly divided into two kinds: physical monsters arising from a change in matter, such as fox-fire, shooting stars, will-o'-the-wisps, and mirages; and psychological monsters arising from a change in the mind, such as strange dreams, divine sensations, fox possession, and prophecy. The latter are further classified into those that appear in the outer world, such as ghosts and tengu; those that come by way of another person's mediation, such as shamans, divination, and hypnosis; and those that arise from one's own body and mind, such as dreams, sleepwalking, and delusion. Following this framework, Enryō takes up, one after another, the strange happenings that people of his day actually spoke of, and unravels them from the standpoint of illusion, suggestion, coincidence, and the power of popular superstition. In the discussion of chance placed at the close, he argues that chance, probability, and necessity are, in their essence, one and the same, and that our distinguishing between them arises only from the still-insufficient development of our own intelligence. An attitude that does not flatly deny monsters but tries instead to explain how they came to be runs through the whole work, and it is known as a piece of work that anticipated the later establishment of folklore and psychology in Japan.

The opening (original text)

The study of monsters is set out here as a part of applied psychology, and even though I attach the character for "study" to it, I by no means intend it as a fully established discipline in its own right. It is nothing more than an attempt to gather the facts about monsters and give them an explanation grounded in psychology — that is, to apply the theories of psychology in practice to explain these facts, and thereby to contribute, in some small way, to psychological research.

Yōkaigaku by Inoue Enryō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

(A closing note.) The Lectures on the Study of Monsters comes to its end here. And, as I noted also in my preface, this course of lectures was not set out as "the study of monsters" in its own right; I have only wished to contribute to psychological research, and have given a psychological explanation for the facts about monsters that I have studied thus far. If the reader will take these lectures as a reference within a part of applied psychology, he will have grasped the lecturer's true intent.

Yōkaigaku by Inoue Enryō (Aozora Bunko)
