About this work

In the stands at the Jingū stadium, a student of nineteen is told by a woman four years older that she is to be married this autumn — the earliest work of Yamakawa Masao, published in Mita Bungaku when he was twenty-two. He lays the year that passed without so much as taking her hand over the fireworks he had watched at the river-opening at Ryōgoku the summer before. Is the love of youth not like a firework let off into a noon sky, that vanishes without a sound? Through a faint lyricism his later subjects already show.

Summary

On a fine early-summer afternoon he has come to a Six Universities baseball game with a woman four years older than himself. He is a student of nineteen; the woman does not know baseball, and applauds warmly even at the fielding practice. Whenever she met him she made herself up to look young. As she laid a white handkerchief over her fringe and looked up at the blue sky, he suddenly felt the line running from her chin down her throat to be beautiful. — Last summer the two of them had gone out alone for the first time, to the opening of the river at Ryōgoku. On the roof of an old building at Asakusabashi, every time a tangled chrysanthemum went up the woman laughed and said, "Fireworks are meaningless, aren't they," and taught him their names like an elder sister: the clove chrysanthemum, the silver brocade, the flower garden. He only went over and over in his mind that standing-stock-still feeling of his, which was bound to the gentle line of her throat. Without so much as holding a hand or laying lip on lip, the distance between them had not changed at all in a year. ... While they waited for the siren that would begin the game, the woman said suddenly, "Waiting is tiring, isn't it," and told him: "I am to be married this autumn. ... I meant to say it after the game." "...But what of it?" he laughed. And yet, the moment a term was shown him, he began to notice that he was feeling the happiness of being with her more deeply and more surely than ever before. "Congratulations," he said in a low voice. "When will you go home next?" "As soon as the summer holidays begin." "I see. ... Then you cannot come to the river opening." On the field the young nine were joined by the line of a white ball, and their healthiness dazzled him. "Why did you never try to lean on me a little more?" the woman whispered. And he thinks: what he had seen in her this last year, the one love he believed in a moment ago — is not that itself a firework? And the bed from which his firework rises is not the pitch-black night but the blue sky of high noon, brimming with light.

The opening (original text)

When he had climbed the dark stairs of the ballpark, a great bright field like another world opened before his eyes. The flood of white shirts hurt the eye. The spectators had already filled eight parts of the infield stands. On the field the university players in brand-new uniforms were running about briskly after the white balls of fielding practice, which echoed with a good clean sound. With the early-summer light falling straight down on their sunburned faces, the young men had the healthy look of wild animals. The dull sound of the leather taking the ball. The hard, sharp crack of the bat. The calls. All of it mixed with the young cheering and applause; and walking along the aisle, he too was drawn into a light excitement.

The ending (original text)

The woman did not stop applauding. It was an oddly slow, drawn-out applause. Following her, he too had begun to applaud the far green leaves of the wood. Far off, the softly swelling tips of the trees are moving as though in waves. A wind seems to be passing over. He called up before his eyes the trees of the outer garden, joined green branch to green branch, rustling, their young leaves glittering and brimming over everywhere. He was looking only at the light of the fresh green of a young summer. As though in a contest, the two of them went on applauding slowly, and long. He would go on until she stopped; so he was thinking.
