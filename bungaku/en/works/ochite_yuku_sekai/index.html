About this work

Written in November 1950. It shares almost the same opening as "The Dominoes' Message," published that spring, but is greatly extended from there: a picture of Kusaka Yōko's own family. It follows the autumn of an old house living by selling off its possessions through the eyes of Yukiko, the twenty-five-year-old elder sister, until the change a fortune-teller predicted arrives as the father's death. Neither lamenting the decline nor denouncing it, a sober eye that simply finds it absurd runs through the whole.

Summary

With the five hundred yen she got by selling fifty grams of her father's blood, Yukiko buys a Yi-dynasty Korean dish and comes home. Her father gasping with his chronic asthma; her mother, who has entrusted everything to the Shinrei sect; her elder brother Shin'ichi, still in hospital with tuberculosis; her younger brother Shinjirō, who has entered the university and walks about with an older married woman; her aunt and her cousin Haruhiko, living on piecework and lottery tickets — in a house of long standing, the bottom of what can be sold is already in sight. And still her father and mother cannot give up appearances, and will not let Yukiko go out to work or set up in trade.

The inkstones and hanging scrolls brought out of the storehouse, and the Sèvres that was to have gone with her as a bridal gift, are taken by a dealer for twenty-five thousand yen, and the silver is sold to a jeweler. The fortune-teller she visits on the way lays out wooden dominoes and tells her that the character for "movement" stands within this month; that there will be a change in the house or in her own condition; that she thinks only of carrying and does not measure her own strength.

One morning a few days later Yukiko opens the sliding door and sees her father's body. The bottle of the poison is empty. Grief does not come; she only goes over the days when she and her father alone could understand each other through porcelain and painting. The funeral is done, her brother coughs blood, and still Yukiko sits down at the bridge table with her brother and her aunt. She will live on with no aim and no repentance, finding beauty in porcelain and in pictures.

Characters

  • Yukiko　The elder sister, twenty-five. The narrator, who sells blood and household goods to hold the family together
  • Father　Who gasps with his chronic asthma. Only porcelain and painting join him to Yukiko
  • Mother　Who has taken refuge in the Shinrei sect and leaves everything to the god
  • Shin'ichi　The elder brother, still in hospital with tuberculosis
  • Shinjirō　The younger brother, a university student, who walks about with an older married woman and likes to gamble
  • The aunt　A widow living on piecework and lottery tickets in the tea room

The opening (original text)

One day — With my footsteps muffled I went in from the entrance to my own room, and changing quickly out of my Western clothes I opened the sliding door of the room where my father lay. With the dim light of the lamp drawn close to his pillow, my father is gasping there. His chronic asthma always comes on upon a damp day like today. Though it has turned autumn, this year there has not yet been one clear, dry day; every day has been somehow close and at the same time chilly.

Ochite yuku sekai by Kusaka Yōko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And again one day. Shinjirō and my aunt and Haruhiko and I were absorbed in bridge. My brother fighting his illness at the hospital, my mother offering everything up to the god — everyone goes on living. And I too shall go on living with no aim, without examining any single act of mine, only finding beauty in porcelain and in pictures, and holding a little longing.
