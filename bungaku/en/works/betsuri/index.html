About this work

A poem of parting in five sections by Nakahara Chūya, in which the call of goodbye is repeated. The greeting on the day of leaving a house where he was looked after turns before one notices into a song of irrecoverable loss. The muttered asides in parentheses — that it is stupid to think about such things; that it is the same as sleet — hold the sentiment at arm's length and make it the more piercing.

Summary

"Goodbye, goodbye! / Thank you for everything" — the first chapter opens as a farewell spoken on the day of leaving a house that has cared for the speaker so long. To think of parting on such a fine day is truly hard to bear. This time tomorrow, he will be looking at the soil of the home town he knew for so many long years. "You wave your parasol so — it dazzles me too much." In the second chapter, he returns again and again to the memory of that day when he woke from an afternoon nap to find everyone gone from the house. Sunning himself and trimming his nails, the little lawn, eating chestnuts, the sun-dried rice tub, a crow crying idly on the hill behind the house — "I remember everything." But the thing he remembers most of all, he falls silent over: "No, I can't tell you that, not anymore." In the third chapter, an unforgettable rainbow and flower are sung as things that will one day vanish along with that hand, those lips, and he mocks himself with the aside, "(It's the same as sleet, you know.)" The fourth chapter is a sudden cry of hunger — "Please, give me something to eat" — but there is no food to be found on the country road. In the fifth chapter, beside a moss-grown hut with a watermill visible in the distance, he bids farewell — "Well then, you may turn back now" — and says, "I can go on alone, I can go on." Though, civilized as he is, he could have found other things to talk of, he says again and again that, after all, he simply cannot — and with that repetition, the poem comes to its close.

The ending (original text)

Over there, I can see a watermill, beside a moss-grown hut, Well then, you may turn back now, turn back now, I can go on alone, I can go on, Whatever am I saying? No — even I, civilized as I am, could have found other things to talk of, if I'd tried, No — after all, I can't, I just can't.
