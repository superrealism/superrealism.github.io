About this work

The last of Murō Saisei's three autobiographical novels, published in Chūō Kōron in 1919. It draws the young manhood of the narrator, who has come up to Tokyo from Kanazawa on the strength of his gift for poetry and lives poor but for poetry. In the loneliness and want of the city, he gives his heart to the sweetness of the small girls near him and has a brief warm friendship with them. But that innocent friendship is cut off by the death of one of them. Pure affection for small, fragile things and the grief of loss, sung in Saisei's own lyricism.

Summary

Recognized for his gift in poetry, "I" leave Kanazawa and move to Tokyo. With no settled work and hardly enough for the day, still he goes on living without letting go of writing poems. What comforted "my" heart in the lonely life of the city was the careless loveliness of the little girls around the lodging house. Out of a pure fondness for what is lovable, "I" pass a brief and warm companionship with two of them, and live through the days as though saved by their innocence. But so small a happiness does not last. One of the girls he has cherished falls ill and her young life goes out. A pure feeling given to a lovable thing arrives at the irrevocable parting of a child's death. The clean affection that grew up in poverty and solitude, and the grief of the loss that takes it away, are drawn quietly by Saisei with a poet's eye.

The opening (original text)

Because a voice was calling me from far away, I woke suddenly, and the mistress of the lodging was standing at my pillow. Looking at her, I was already sinking back drowsily into deep sleep.

The ending (original text)

On the third of October in the forty-fourth year of Meiji I made my first retreat from the capital. In December, going beyond that, I received a letter from her father, Mr. Yamamoto Chinsō. Opening it, it was set down that Fujiko had taken a disease of the bowels and died. He wrote to thank me for the many kindnesses shown her in her life, and added that Fujiko had always been talking of "the uncle with the long hair." Reading that letter I felt violent tears. And I put my palms together toward the soul of her who had been a small savior to me, and was now empty.
