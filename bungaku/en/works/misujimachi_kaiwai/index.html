About this work

A recollection of the days about 1896 that the boy Saitō Mokichi passed in the Misujimachi quarter of Asakusa, where the family that adopted him lived, after coming up from Yamagata. The Kuramae road with its horse-drawn tram, the old woman drawing sand pictures in the precincts of the Asakusa Kannon, festival stalls and public baths — the lost sights of Meiji Tokyo are drawn vividly, with a fine eye for detail.

Summary

In 1896, the boy Mokichi, having come up to Tokyo from the countryside of Yamagata, begins living in the Misujimachi quarter of Asakusa, where the medical practice of the Saitō family, distant relatives who adopted him, was located. In that era, just after the end of the First Sino-Japanese War, when the great tsunami along the Sanriku coast and the Ashio Copper Mine pollution incident were shaking the world, and Ōgai and Kōyō were wielding their pens, an old woman drawing sand pictures worked freely with coloured powders in the precincts of the Asakusa Kannon, and along Kuramae street a horse-drawn tram ran, its horse fitted with blinders. The bustle of festival days, a girl he glimpsed on his way to school, the memory of wandering by mistake into the women's bath while being led by a maid, and the story of a copy of the Kokinshū, annotated in Kamo no Mabuchi's own hand, that his father had once received as a fee for treating a patient, only for it to burn up in a fire — a reminiscent essay in which Mokichi, now become a tanka poet, sets down with fine, loving care the sights of Meiji Tokyo he saw as a boy, and the faint awakening of his own feelings.

The opening (original text)

This reminiscent essay covers the four or five years beginning in 1896, and so ought to correspond to the period just after the First Sino-Japanese War, when the question of the return of the Liaodong Peninsula was making a great clamor, when the great tsunami struck the Sanriku coast of the Tōhoku region, and the Ashio Copper Mine pollution incident occurred, and in the literary world, around the time Mori Ōgai's Mezamashigusa and Yosano Tekkan's Tōzainanboku appeared, and Rohan's "Kumo no Sode," Kōyō's "Konjiki Yasha," and Ryūrō's "Imado Shinjū" were being written. It was the era when the horse-drawn tram first came to Tokyo, and an old woman drawing sand pictures still worked in the precincts of the Asakusa Kannon.

Misujimachi kaiwai by Saitō Mokichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And so it turned out that there was nothing in that bathhouse but women. There were old women too, but a great many pretty young ones as well. I have some memory of feeling my heart leap for no reason I could name, though in truth it may not yet have been quite that. What I had taken for a place full only of women turned out to be the women's bath. It was found out afterward, the maid was scolded by my mother, and I was never again able to enter the women's bath. There was a time when I looked back fondly, with real affection, on that single visit to the women's bath. This time too I wavered over whether to leave it out of this essay or not, but there is still a sweetness left in it too precious to throw away.

Misujimachi kaiwai by Saitō Mokichi (Aozora Bunko)
