About this work

Awashima Chingaku, an eccentric painter who boasted that his pictures would fetch a price once he was dead, and who threw off mud pictures in the Ōtsu-e manner. A biographical essay tracing his strange life — from a dealer in the light rice cakes that were a specialty of Edo, to a man of parts at an oil exchange, to a showman at Asakusa, to caretaker of the Awashima hall — together with the history of the family of this father of the painter Kangetsu, and drawing vividly a monstrous hybrid produced by the culture of a transitional age.

Summary

A critical memoir in which Uchida Roan draws the life of Awashima Chingaku — the father of the painter Awashima Kangetsu, and himself famous as an eccentric painter. Beginning with his regret for the Bon'un hermitage at Mukōjima, burned in the great earthquake, he speaks of the charm of Chingaku's bold, unrestrained mud-color paintings, which ignored all tradition. He goes back first to the genealogy of the Awashima family, and sets out the second Kihei, who built up the fortune of a light-cake shop at Bakurochō, and Chingaku's relation with his elder brother Itō Hachibei, born into the wealthy farming house of Uchida at Kawagoe and one of the greatest fortunes of the end of the shogunate. After a first half of life spent as "Kobayashi Jōzō," a clever man of the oil exchange who gave himself the air of a samurai, he made a hit after the Restoration with a peep-show of Western views at Asakusa, and became the keeper of the Awashima hall and sold his eccentric name. He banged on a piano and scraped a violin and struck a biwa to mystify people; he is said to have had more than a hundred and sixty mistresses; and his works — a manner of his own, descended from Ōkyo and yet bound to no school, flung down in mud colors, and his Asakusa pictures, and the coffered ceiling of the Enpukuji — are introduced one by one. The essay follows him to his deathbed, where he laughed aloud, "I have tried all sorts of things until now, but dying is the first time," and on to the sudden rise in the fever for collecting Chingaku after his death, when foreigners began to buy him — all set down with affection for a strange talent produced by the culture of an age of transition.

Characters

  • Awashima Chingaku　Surnamed Hattori, also called Kobayashi Jōzō. From a light-cake dealer and a clever man of the oil exchange he turned eccentric painter, and flung down mud-color pictures in the manner of the Ōtsu prints
  • Awashima Kangetsu　Chingaku's son. The discoverer of Saikaku and the reviver of Genroku literature, and a painter. He lived at the Bon'un hermitage
  • Itō Hachibei　Chingaku's elder brother. A great merchant reckoned the richest man in Edo at the end of the shogunate
  • Uchida Roan　The author

The opening (original text)

Among the memorials turned to ash in the fire of the earthquake, to speak of historic sites would be too grand; but there are small remains and buildings whose burning is to be regretted. The old hermitage of Awashima Kangetsu at Mukōjima is one such. He is living now in a shack on that ground and means to raise the old place again; but even if it is raised again, it will not be the Bon'un hermitage on which the hand of the elder Chingaku was laid. Kangetsu's name has long been known as the discoverer of Saikaku and the reviver of Genroku literature; but of late his fame as a painter has risen steadily, so that at the burned main house of the Chikuba in Shintomichō there was even a room called the Kangetsu room, all Kangetsu from the sliding doors to the cupboard panels and the tablets. Kangetsu's bold and unhindered manner took over the mantle of his father Chingaku, and whoever would appreciate Kangetsu's paintings must go back further, to Chingaku.

Awashima chingaku by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But though Chingaku's finest collection has come to nothing, the works he left are still scattered about. Since most of Chingaku's masterpieces were held in the low town, one imagines that they perished for the most part in the fire of the earthquake; but since the country peculiar to Chingaku is displayed rather in small pieces of a foot or two than in large works, if an exhibition were held again unlooked-for fine pieces might come out from somewhere. And since a great many seem to have gone abroad for high prices, it is not impossible that before long the fellows of Kurth and Succo may bring out a life of Chingaku with plates reproducing masterpieces hardly known to the Japanese, and shake the picture-reading world of Europe. Chingaku, who boasted, "My paintings will fetch a price when I am dead," must be smiling with satisfaction beneath the moss and saying, "There, you see; you make a fuss about impressionism and expressionism, but Gauguin and Cézanne were doing long ago what I have been doing all along."
