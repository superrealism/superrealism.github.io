About this work

Tobacco was brought to Japan by a devil who came over with the southern-barbarian missionaries — a story of Japan's Christians that Akutagawa wrote from that legend. A wager between the devil and an ox dealer — that if the man cannot name the crop in the field the devil takes his soul — comes to an unlooked-for end. The closing line, that a devil never falls without picking something up, tells.

Summary

Tobacco, properly speaking, was not a plant native to Japan. As for who brought it over by ship, any historian would answer: a Portuguese, or a Spaniard. But there is another answer besides — a legendary one: that tobacco was brought from somewhere by the devil, and that this devil was brought to Japan by a padre of the Catholic faith (Father Francis, most likely). That a Southern Barbarian devil should arrive at the very same time as a Southern Barbarian god, that Western evil should be imported at the very same time as Western good, is, Akutagawa writes, only to be expected. The devil who came to Japan in the padre's company grew bored, and, disguising himself as a lay brother (an iruman), took to growing an unfamiliar plant in the church's field. A cattle merchant happened to pass by. Asked by the merchant what the plant was, the devil proposed a wager: if the merchant could name it within three days, he could have the entire crop; if he failed, the devil would take his body and soul. At a loss, the merchant hit on a scheme — he drove his cattle into the field to run wild. Startled, the devil cried out without thinking, "Don't trample the tobacco!" and the merchant, learning the name, won the wager. But Akutagawa closes with a twist: though the cattle merchant was saved, it was thanks to this very episode that tobacco spread throughout all of Japan. Might not the devil's failure, in one sense, carry a kind of success within it after all? "The devil never falls for nothing. Might it not be that even when we believe we have overcome temptation, we have, more often than we know, in fact been defeated?"

The opening (original text)

Tobacco, properly speaking, was not a plant native to Japan. As for just when it was brought over by ship, the records do not agree on the date — some give it as the Keichō era, others as the Tenbun era... As for who, then, brought this tobacco over, any historian would answer: a Portuguese, or a Spaniard. But that is not necessarily the only answer there is.

Tabako to akuma by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

So it would seem: just as the cattle merchant's salvation carried within it, on one side, a kind of fall, might not the devil's failure, too, carry within it, on one side, a kind of success? The devil never falls for nothing. Might it not be that even when we believe we have overcome temptation, we have, more often than we know, in fact been defeated?

Tabako to akuma by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)
