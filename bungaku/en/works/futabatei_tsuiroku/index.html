About this work

Uchida Roan's essay on Futabatei Shimei, starting from the question of what would have happened had he lived. Taking as his thread the wish that Futabatei could have witnessed the Russian Revolution, he argues in four sections that Futabatei was an old man who understood modern thought and yet could not throw off the temper of the old patriot-scholar, that his character was such that he would have gone on writhing however long he lived, and that his life was that of a hero of failure.

Summary

A critical recollection in which Uchida Roan discusses Futabatei Shimei in four short chapters. The first, "If Futabatei Were Alive," imagines what he would have done had he been present at the Russian revolution, and regrets that a Futabatei who could not give up his faith in power and who made light of the revolutionary party could not have been there, taken aback, to witness that great upheaval. The second, "Futabatei Was in Fact an Old Man," analyzes how, pioneer as he was in introducing Russian literature, a head trained from childhood on Wei Shuzi and Lu Xuangong could not take in modern liberal thought, and how he could never become a modern man at the bottom of his heart, but was an "old man." The third, "Even Had He Lived Long, Futabatei Would Have Been a Man of Anguish to the End," holds that Futabatei, who preached that doubt itself is philosophy, was a being who could never be satisfied with things as they stood and who struggled on from one torment to another, and that even had he lived long he would have found no "satisfaction" and no "solution." The fourth, "Futabatei Was a Hero of Failure," explains the misfortune of a man born in a country unlike the West, where poets and novelists may become ministers of state, and, judging frankly that Ukigumo and Sono omokage and Heibon are all unfinished and ill-made, places him as a hero of failure born too early for Japan.

Characters

  • Futabatei Shimei　Properly Hasegawa Tatsunosuke. The pioneer who introduced Russian literature, who understood modern thought and yet could not give up the old temper of a patriot-scholar
  • Uchida Roan　The author, his close friend of twenty years
  • Tsubouchi Shōyō　Cited as the exceptional man who set out from the first resolved to make drama and fiction his life's work
  • Tokutomi Roka　Mentioned as an example of a man of letters who declared, "I am not ashamed to be a novelist"

The opening (original text)

1. If Futabatei were alive What would Futabatei be doing now, if he were alive? — that question once became the subject of talk at a gathering of those who had known him. I think he could hardly have endured in the Russian capital until the time when the revolution broke out; but supposing he had been there, would he have taken the side of the Romanovs or felt with the revolutionary party? Which would he have supported? With his rancorous poet's temper he would have offered a dirge over the last days of a fallen dynasty; but he knew too well and too deeply the history of the crimes of the Romanovs to weep over the wretched fate of the Tsar.

Futabatei tsuiroku by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

To speak honestly, Ukigumo and Sono omokage and Heibon are all of them unfinished and ill-made. That his name as a man of letters should rest on those three works must have been a regret to him, even if he thought it beneath him to be a man of letters at all. In the end Futabatei was born too early for Japan. Had it been Europe, he would have been treated with some regard as a novelist, and Futabatei too might have been content to be a man of letters.

Futabatei tsuiroku by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
