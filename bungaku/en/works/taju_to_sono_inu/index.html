About this work

A short story drawing the life of Tajū, an old farmer shunned by the village as an ugly man, who has lived with a single dog for his one support. The fine affection between a lonely old man and his dog, and his abrupt death, are set down within the author's own close description of nature. A central short story by Nagatsuka Takashi, a master of sketch prose, looking at the pitifulness of human beings and the life of the village.

Summary

The story opens with a single line: "Tajū is dead." His face disfigured by smallpox scars, his nose half-crushed, Tajū was known in the village as "Old Man North," shunned by his peers as a man uglier than any other. In his youth no woman would so much as look at him, and he had done nothing but work in silence — the only warmth he ever came to share, in his later years, was with O-Ishi, a blind itinerant singer (goze) who came to the village at the autumn festival, and with a single dog. Once O-Ishi stopped appearing in the village, Tajū poured his affection all the more into the dog, living out his days in the watchman's hut in the fields. And then, one day, as he passed his time there, a violent evening thunderstorm struck the village. The life and death of one solitary, stubborn man are set down, against the backdrop of the untamed force of nature along the Kinu riverside, in prose that admits not a trace of sentimentality.

The opening (original text)

Tajū is dead. He was known as "Old Man North," because his house stood at the northern edge of the village. The gateway to it ran fairly long, overhung on both sides by a bamboo thicket. The thicket had grown quite ragged from reckless cutting, but it still cast a gloom over that corner of the yard even so. "Oitsuan" — the word was neither quite "uncle" nor quite "father" — carried within it both a note of respect and a note of mockery. No one could say when it had begun, but it had been so for a long time now. Even past sixty, he kept company only with men in their thirties and forties, and especially with those in their twenties; men of his own age were, if anything, not the sort he cared to be around. He was, in the whole village, a man uglier than any other.

The ending (original text)

When his family came running and, in the light of the setting sun, dug through the ashes, they found Tajū lying on his back, his body burned and disfigured. His hands still covered his ears, as though he had, indeed, been afraid of the thunder to the very last. The sickle hanging on the wall beneath the roof, its white blade bared, may well have drawn the lightning — the hut had burned in a bolt from the sky. By the time the fire caught the hut, Tajū must already have been dead, free of all pain. And so it was that the stubborn, solitary Tajū, alone out there in the fields, had his last strength spent in so short a span of time. And nature's own overwhelming power, driving the sudden violence of the weather, pressed down with crushing force upon his small, helpless fear, and in that same instant buried his simple life away for good. The board with the dog's hide stretched over it lay fallen face-down. And the underside of the board was faintly scorched.
