About this work

A historical comedy Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Kōdan Zasshi in 1947. Goza Heinosuke, third son of the chief retainer of a daimyo house, moves into the haunted house called the Pomegranate Mansion in the castle town of Hiroshima. It draws the comic exchanges at the well between this utterly innocent young samurai and Tomi, the girl next door who is a keen observer of the haunting. The riddle of what the ghost really is, and the budding of a fresh love, set down in a dapper and light hand: a piece full of laughter.

Summary

On the edge of the samurai quarter in the castle town of Hiroshima in Aki province stood a house that had lain empty for seven or eight years. Would-be tenants came and went, but the faint-hearted never lasted through a single night, fleeing white-faced before midnight — a notorious haunted house known as the "Pomegranate Mansion," after the great pomegranate tree towering in its garden. Tomi, the daughter of the house next door, had loved being startled by strange things since she was a small child, and had become an avid observer of the mansion's hauntings, recording them in a memorandum with almost scientific detail. Then a young samurai comes to live there: Heinosuke, third son of Ueda Mondo, the Edo house-elder of the Asano house, who has taken on the Goza surname. Though raised in a household on a par with a daimyo's twelve-thousand-koku estate, he is "utterly innocent, straightforward, and open," a man of such boundless, sunny good nature as belongs only to gods or simpletons. Every morning Heinosuke and Tomi meet at the well. When Tomi asks whether the ghost has appeared, Heinosuke answers placidly that "the one-eyed goblin brought tea" or some such thing. In truth, the ghost's true nature is nothing but the doings of gamblers using the mansion as a gambling den — but Tomi, plucking at the hedge and blushing scarlet, finds that before she knows it, their talk of ghosts has drifted to laundry, to meals, and at last to marriage. A period comedy by Yamamoto, told through uproariously funny dialogue, of a haunted house's mystery and the budding of an innocent love.

The opening (original text)

Hiroshima, in the province of Aki, was the castle town of the Asano house, lords of four hundred and twenty-six thousand koku — a city of over ten thousand households, thriving with merchants who came and went freely from other provinces to trade their wares, a prosperity that did not belie its name as the foremost town of the Chūgoku region. ...And in this bustling castle town of Hiroshima, in the very midst of the samurai quarter, there stood a single haunted house. It lay near Shukkei-en, the domain lord's garden villa east of the castle — on the edge of the samurai quarter, as such things go — and had already stood empty for seven or eight years.

Fūryū bakemono yashiki by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

At that same moment, in the Miura household, young Miss Tomi was writing in her memorandum: "He is certain to propose, and when he does, I shall refuse him flatly. Yes, quite flatly — and then I shall enter a nunnery and live out my life in purity, weeping and thinking of him always. ...But no, I shan't have any patterned robes made — after all, one only marries once, so it would be better to have plenty of practical things made instead, Chichibu weave, or pongee, or the like. But then, whatever shall I wear for the ceremony itself? Oh, how troubling. How very troubling..."
