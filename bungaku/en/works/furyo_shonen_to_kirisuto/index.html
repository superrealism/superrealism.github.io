About this work

Sakaguchi Ango's memorial piece for Dazai Osamu, written immediately after his death, and an anti-memorial as much as a memorial. It begins with ten days of toothache — if an atomic bomb crushes a million people in an instant but cannot stop one man's toothache, what is civilization worth? — and goes on to abuse his dead friend roundly, and to defend him more deeply than anyone. The piece in which Ango's thought, that one should live and fall, breaks out most violently.

Summary

My tooth has ached for ten days now — lying down with an ice bag on his cheek, Ango has been rereading nearly all of Dazai's books. The doctor will say nothing but "I expect it will get better presently." "What is civilization worth if you can smash a million people in an instant with an atomic bomb and still cannot stop the toothache of one single man? Idiots." Beginning from such private matters, the essay turns before long to the dead Dazai. Dazai was a master of school-composition writing for the little misses and masters, a man who suffered from a hangover kind of self-consciousness, who kept saying he would die and could not die — so Ango writes, without mercy. But at the same time, as one who knew Dazai's weakness and loneliness better than anyone, he snaps at a world that dismisses his death as "sentimentality." For a man, living is the whole of it; die, and you go back to nothing, Ango says. Religions and philosophies that discourse on life and death are only toys. It is by knowing living, and only living, that justice and truth are born. And while letting slip the honest words "but living does tire one out," he says flatly: come what may, I shall live the time I have to live, and I shall fight. Man can never win; he only avoids being beaten. Learning lies in the discovery of limits; the atomic bomb and suicide are not learning but a child's game. "That is what I fight for."

The opening (original text)

My tooth has ached for ten days now. I lie down with ice on my right cheek and take sulfa drugs. I do not want to lie down, but once you put ice on it there is nothing to do but lie down. I lie down and read. I have reread nearly all of Dazai's books. I have emptied three boxes of the sulfa drug, and the pain does not stop. There was nothing for it, so I went to the doctor.

Furyō shōnen to kirisuto by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Thanks to this war I have been taught that the atomic bomb is not learning, that a child's game is not learning, that war is not learning. I had overrated grand things. Learning is the discovery of limits. That is what I fight for.

Furyō shōnen to kirisuto by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)
