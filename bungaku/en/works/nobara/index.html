About this work

One of Ogawa Mimei's central short stories for children. An old soldier of a great country and a young soldier of a small one guard the stone marker at their border. The only two people there, they come to play shōgi together and to share the scent of the wild roses — and are torn apart, willing or not, into enemies by a war between their countries. In a plain and quiet hand it sets the meeting of two people who never really knew each other against the unreason of war. A clear parable against it.

Summary

A great country and a somewhat smaller country lie side by side, and on the border in the mountains, far from either capital, each has posted a single soldier to guard the stone that marks the frontier. The soldier from the great country is an old man, and the soldier from the small one is a young man. Distant with each other at first, wondering whether they are friends or enemies, the two grow thoroughly fond of each other in the boredom of days with no one else to talk to, and on fine spring days they play chess every day beside the boundary stone where the wild roses bloom. Winter comes, and while the old man is longing for the children and grandchildren who live in the south, the two countries go to war over some trifling question of advantage. From that day the two friends are enemies. When the old man tells him to take his head and win credit by it, the young man says, "How could you and I be enemies?" — and goes away to the far northern front. Left alone, the old man passes his days worrying about him, until a traveler tells him that the small country has lost and its soldiers have been killed to the last man. One day, dozing, the old man dreams that the young man passes at the head of an army, bows to him in silence and smells the wild roses. He wakes and it was a dream; before long the roses wither, and in the autumn of that year the old man is given leave and goes home to the south.

Characters

  • The old man　An old soldier, a major, posted from the great country to guard the frontier. He has children and grandchildren in the south and longs to go home
  • The young man　The young soldier posted from the small country, who plays chess with the old man and grows close to him, and goes off to the war

The opening (original text)

A great country and a country somewhat smaller than it lay side by side. For the present nothing had happened between the two, and there was peace. This is the frontier, far from the capitals. There a single soldier from each country had been posted, to guard the stone that marked the border. The soldier of the great country was an old man. And the soldier of the small country was a young man. The two of them kept watch on the right and on the left of the stone. It was a very lonely mountain. And only rarely was the figure of a traveler to be seen thereabouts.

Nobara by Ogawa Mimei (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The old man was about to say something when he woke. It had been entirely a dream. About a month later the wild roses withered. In the autumn of that year the old man was given leave and went home to the south.
