About this work

Sakaguchi Ango's best-known essay, and one whose title became a byword for postwar Japan. Published in the magazine Shinchō in April 1946, it looks hard at the official pieties of the war years and at how quickly they were dropped afterwards, and argues that it is precisely by falling that a person discovers himself and can begin to live truly. It is the central statement of Ango's thought — he was grouped with Dazai Osamu and Ishikawa Jun as one of the "decadents" — and a starting point for postwar literature.

Summary

Sakaguchi Ango begins from the fact that in the half-year since the war's end the temper of the times has changed utterly. The kamikaze pilot who was to have given his life becomes a black marketeer; the war widow who swore fidelity begins to keep a new face in her heart. Human beings have not changed, Ango argues: what has changed is only the outer skin of the age. The seppuku of the forty-seven loyal retainers and the aesthetics of bushidō, he declares, were likewise no more than a facade to hide human weakness and human fallenness. Institutions such as the emperor system and bushidō are contrivances made by people who cannot bear to fall, in order to keep themselves tethered, and no merely surface reform by politics can save anyone. So long as a person is alive, that person cannot help but fall. And it is precisely by falling all the way down that road, Ango concludes with force, that one may at last discover one's own true self and be saved.

The opening (original text)

In half a year the temper of the times has changed. "We who set out as the shield of the sovereign." "For our great lord we shall die and never look back." The young men scattered like blossom — and those same young men survive and become black marketeers. "I will not wish for a hundred years of life; one day I shall go as your shield, and so we pledged." The women who sent their men off with such brave hearts will find, within half a year, that bowing before a husband's memorial tablet has become a mere formality, and it will not be long before they keep a new face in their hearts. It is not that human beings have changed. Human beings were always like this, and what has changed is only the outer skin of the age.

The ending (original text)

After the war we were granted every freedom; but when a person is granted every freedom, that person becomes aware of the incomprehensible limits set upon them and of their own unfreedom. A human being can never be free. For a human being is alive, and must die, and thinks. Political reform can be carried out in a day; the change of human beings cannot. How much change has human nature shown since it was discovered far off in Greece and took its first step toward being established? Human beings. However fearful the destruction and the fate that war brings against them, it can do nothing to human beings themselves. The war is over. Has the brave kamikaze pilot not already become a black marketeer, the widow's breast not already swelled with a new face? Human beings do not change. They have merely come back to being human. Human beings fall. The loyal retainer falls and the saint falls. It cannot be prevented, and no one is saved by preventing it. Human beings live, and human beings fall. There is no convenient shortcut to saving them outside of that. It is not because we lost the war that we fall. We fall because we are human, and we fall simply because we are alive. Yet human beings will not be able to go on falling forever. For the human heart cannot be as steel against suffering. Human beings are pitiable and frail and therefore foolish, but they are too weak to fall all the way down. In the end a person cannot help stabbing the virgin to death, cannot help devising bushidō, cannot help hoisting up an emperor. But in order to stab to death one's own virgin rather than another's, and to devise one's own bushidō and one's own emperor, a person must fall all the way down the road of falling, and fall rightly. And Japan too, like a person, must fall. By falling all the way down the road of falling, we must discover our own selves and save them. Salvation by politics is a thing of the surface and worth nothing at all.

Famous passages

We must fall all the way down the road of falling, and in doing so discover and save ourselves.

The core of this essay, written for the Japanese people just after their defeat in the war. Rather than returning to the borrowed morality of bushidō or the emperor, it argues, one must find oneself only after falling all the way down.

Human beings do not change. They have only returned to being human. Human beings fall into decadence. Even loyal retainers, even saints, fall into decadence.

A passage that looks steadily at the state of things after the defeat. It is the core argument of the essay: accept decadence as human nature itself.

The war is over. Have the heroes of the Special Attack Corps not already become black marketeers, and do the widows' hearts not already swell with new faces?

A passage confronting the state of society less than a year after the defeat. It looks squarely at the collapse of beautifully told wartime ideals and at how easily people change in order to live.

Human beings live, and human beings fall. There is no convenient shortcut to saving humanity other than that.

A sentence placed after the argument that people cannot be saved by preventing their fall. The pivot of the essay: accept living and falling as one and the same.

It is not because we lost the war that we fall. We fall because we are human; we fall simply because we are alive.

A passage rejecting the view that blames decadence on the defeat. Declaring that decadence is not a product of the times but of human nature, it urges postwar people to look at themselves anew.
