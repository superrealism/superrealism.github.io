About this work

"I," told by a doctor that his heart is weak, is terrified by the violence of the epidemic influenza and goes to extreme lengths to guard against it. He avoids going out, never neglects his gargle, and will not take off his black mask even in the season when no one wears one any longer. Having justified this to himself as "the courage of a civilized man," he is seized by an unexpected disgust and hatred when, on his way to a baseball ground in early summer, he passes a young man in a black mask. A short story that captures with the utmost precision the workings of the mind of a person living through an epidemic.

Summary

"I" am stout to look at, but every one of my internal organs is weak, and when I see a doctor about my stomach I am told that the valve of my heart is imperfect, and that if I caught the epidemic influenza and ran a fever of forty degrees for three or four days I should almost certainly not survive. Just then the influenza began to rage, and I was wholly possessed by a dread of it. I kept from going out, would not let my wife or the maid out either, gargled with hydrogen peroxide morning and evening, and when I had to go out put on a mask packed with gauze. I was raised and cast down by the number of deaths in the newspaper, and felt relief when, after a peak of three thousand three hundred and thirty-seven, the figure began to fall. When the epidemic fell off in March and no one wore a mask any more, I still would not take mine off, explaining to my friends that to fear the disease and absolutely avoid infection is the courage of a civilized man. On the rare occasions when I saw at a tram stop someone with nose and mouth covered in black cloth, I even felt the encouragement of having found a comrade. In April and May, when at last I too had left off the mask, on my way in mid-May to watch the Waseda baseball I am passed by a young man of twenty-three or four wearing a black mask. At that moment I felt him monstrously ugly and felt a clear hatred. Turning over why he should have been so disagreeable to me, I come to see that I had been oppressed by the strength of a young man who could do calmly what I could not do for fear of the season.

Characters

  • I　A stout man with weak internal organs, who fears the epidemic influenza to an extreme degree
  • The doctor　He tells him that the valve of his heart is imperfect, and offers not a word of comfort
  • The young man　A man of twenty-three or four who still wears a mask in early summer

The opening (original text)

Stout as I look, so that people take me for exceedingly robust, I knew better than anyone that every one of my internal organs was weaker than the common run. Going up the slightest slope left me out of breath. Going up a flight of stairs left me out of breath. When I was a newspaper reporter and ran up the stairs of some big government building, I would sometimes be shown into the room of the man I had come to see and, still panting, be unable to begin at once on what I had to say. My lungs were not very strong either. When I meant to take a deep breath and began to draw the air in, once I had drawn in a certain amount my chest would at once feel tight, and beyond that I could not draw breath at all.

The ending (original text)

The first cause of my dislike of this man was without doubt that on so fine a day he had made me remember the threat of the influenza. And mixed in with it was the self-centered feeling that while I was wearing a mask I had been glad to meet the rare person who wore one, and now that I had left it off a person wearing one looked disagreeable to me. But beyond such feelings I felt something further. Was not my finding this man disagreeable the resentment of the weak toward the strong? At a time when even I, who had been so earnest about wearing a mask, had come to feel too self-conscious about the season to wear one, to put on a mask boldly and even haughtily and push out into a place where thousands of people were gathered — was that not a fairly thoroughgoing attitude of the strong? At any rate, I thought, this young man was bravely doing what I could not bring myself to do for fear of the world and of the season. My feeling this man disagreeable was, I thought, the feeling of one oppressed by such courage as his.
