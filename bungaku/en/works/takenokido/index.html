About this work

A short story Kunikida Doppo published in Chūō Kōron in 1908, late in his life and just before his death: a central work of naturalism. The Ōba family, office people in the Tokyo suburbs, and the poor gardener and his wife who live on the other side of the hedge. It draws how the wife, Ogen, secretly steals the Ōba family's charcoal out of cold and want, and how, driven into a corner by it, she takes her own life. It presses on us quietly, in a hand with sentiment refused, the reality of those at the bottom of society, whom not even a kind master's warmth and goodwill can save.

Summary

Next door to the household of Ōba Shinzō, a mild-mannered company employee living on the outskirts of Tokyo, separated only by a hedge, a poor gardener and his wife, Isokichi and Osen, move into a shack scarcely better than a storage shed. To reach the well, the couple open a gap in the Ōba family's hedge and rig up a crude bamboo gate — but the sharp-witted maid Otoku frowns on it, saying it is "as good as making an entrance for thieves." That winter, with the price of charcoal high, Osen, hard-pressed in her daily life, takes to secretly stealing charcoal from the pile beneath the Ōba family's eaves each time she passes through the gate to the well. One day the master, Shinzō, happens to see Osen with charcoal in her hands, but being a kindly man, he does not press her on it, and resolves to say nothing to anyone. But the maid Otoku sets a trap by marking the charcoal, catches the theft in the act, and the whole household learns of it. Not wishing to make a scene, the Ōba family moves the charcoal indoors and quietly carries on as though nothing had happened. Terrified now of being exposed, Osen can no longer bring herself to go out to the well, and that night rails at her husband for his want of provider's pride. Unable to bear it, Isokichi rushes out and, as if in spite, steals an entire sack of charcoal from a charcoal dealer and brings it home. The next morning, Osen happens to be present when the charcoal is discovered to be stolen goods, and, unable to endure the shame and the terror, hangs herself from a beam of the earthen-floored room with a narrow sash. Two days later the bamboo gate is torn down and the hedge restored as it was, and two months after that, Isokichi has taken another woman as his wife, and still lives in a house no better than a pigsty. A piece written near the very end of Doppo's life, in which he quietly depicts the pitilessness at the bottom of poverty — a place that neither goodwill nor human kindness can ever quite reach.

Characters

  • Osen　The wife of the gardener Isokichi. Driven by cold and poverty, she steals charcoal from the Ōba family, and, terrified of being exposed, takes her own life
  • Isokichi　Osen's husband. A taciturn "man no one can figure out." He works only when he feels like it, and steals charcoal from a charcoal dealer when his wife falls on hard times
  • Ōba Shinzō　A mild-mannered company employee living on the outskirts of Tokyo. He notices Osen's theft but says nothing out of kindness
  • Otoku　The Ōba family's sharp-witted maid. She sets a trap to catch the charcoal thief and exposes Osen's theft

The opening (original text)

Ōba Shinzō, a company employee, lived on the outskirts of Tokyo and commuted to an office somewhere around Kyōbashi Ward. The distance to the streetcar stop was more than half a league, but he walked it, plodding along, every single morning without fail, saying it made just the right amount of exercise. Being of a mild and gentle nature, he was well liked at the office too. His household consisted of his old mother, sixty-seven or sixty-eight and remarkably sturdy for her age; his wife, twenty-nine; his wife's younger sister, Okiyo; his seven-year-old daughter, little Rei; and a maid named Otoku who had been with them for five or six years — five in all, plus the master Shinzō himself, six altogether.

The ending (original text)

Otoku was thoroughly scolded, both by the old mother and by the wife. When evening fell and Osen was nowhere to be seen, Okiyo, growing worried, went to call on her — meaning both to smooth things over and to look in on her as though for a cold. Finding the house strangely still, she called out, "Osen-san, Osen-san," but there came no answer, and, fearfully, she slid open the paper door — to find Osen, who had apparently used a sack of charcoal as a stool, hanging dead from a narrow sash tied to the beam in the middle of the earthen-floored room. Two days later the bamboo gate was torn down. And the hedge was restored as it had been before. Two months after that, Isokichi had taken a woman about the same age as Osen for his wife, and was living in Shibuya village — in a house no better, as before, than a pigsty.
