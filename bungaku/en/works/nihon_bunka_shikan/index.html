About this work

An essay published in the magazine Gendai Bungaku in March 1942. Announcing flatly that he has seen neither the Katsura villa nor the Hōryūji that Bruno Taut so admired, Sakaguchi Ango argues that real beauty lives not in tradition or style but in the necessity of living. From the paradox of calling three structures built purely to necessity beautiful — Kosuge prison, a dry-ice plant, a destroyer — he arrives at the famous line that if it is necessary, one should pull down the Hōryūji and put up a railway station. The essay at the core of Ango's thought.

Summary

I have almost no knowledge of the ancient culture of Japan, and I have never seen the Katsura Detached Palace that Taut praises so highly — having opened with that admission, Ango goes on to reject the habit of deferring to whatever "Japanese" quality foreigners discover for us, and to show how far the things called tradition and national character stand from actual living. If the old temples of Kyoto and Nara burned down, the tradition of Japan would not perish, because tradition lives not in buildings but in the way the Japanese live. It is in the neon signs and the vulgar pleasure quarters that people despise as coarse that the desires of real life appear without pretense. What strikes Ango as beautiful are Kosuge prison, a dry-ice factory and a destroyer — things built with no aim at beauty, obeying necessity alone. Smartness of appearance by itself will never produce true beauty; everything is a question of substance. "If it is necessary, pull down the Hōryūji and build a station." So long as our actual life has set its soul down where the evening sun falls on the roofs of the barracks and the neon signs are shining, he ends, what could that be if not beautiful?

The opening (original text)

I have almost no knowledge of the ancient culture of Japan. I have never seen the Katsura Detached Palace that Bruno Taut praises so highly, and I know nothing of Gyokusen or Taigadō or Chikuden or Tessai. Still less have I so much as heard the names of Hata Zōroku or Master Chikugensai; and, to begin with, since I hardly ever travel, I do not know this town or that village of my own country, nor its manners, nor its mountains and rivers.

Nihon bunka shikan by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

It would not trouble me in the least if the Hōryūji and the Byōdōin burned down. If it is necessary, pull down the Hōryūji and build a station. The glorious culture and tradition of our people will certainly not perish by any such thing. The quiet sunsets of Musashino are gone; but the evening sun falls on the roofs of the barracks heaped up together, the dust clouds even a clear day, and neon signs shine in place of the view on a moonlit night. So long as our actual life has set its soul down here, what could this be if not beautiful?

Famous passages

It wouldn't trouble me in the least if Hōryūji and Byōdōin burned down. If necessary, tear down Hōryūji and build a railway station. The glorious culture and traditions of our people would never perish because of it.

The most provocative passage of this essay on Japanese culture, written during the war. Rejecting a view of tradition that venerates old buildings, it holds that the essence of culture lies in what arises from the necessities of life.
