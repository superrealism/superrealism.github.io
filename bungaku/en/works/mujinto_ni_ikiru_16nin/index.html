About this work

The true story of the sixteen crew of the sailing ship Ryūsuimaru, cast away on an uninhabited Pacific island in 1898, who kept discipline, ingenuity and cheerfulness on a hopeless island until they were saved. Sugawa Kunihiko, a pupil of Captain Nakagawa Kurakichi, wrote his teacher's account down as a book for boys. Setting a daily routine, surveying the island, talking to the sea turtles, building a lookout and waiting for a ship — a classic of Japanese survival writing that never fails to warm the reader at the end.

Summary

"I," a cadet aboard a training sailing ship, am told by my instructor Nakagawa Kurakichi of an experience I shall never forget as long as I live — the wreck of the Ryūsui-maru. Sailing the southern seas in search of pearl oysters, the ship ran one night onto a coral reef that was not on the chart, and sank. What they landed on with their lives was a desert island of sand, small in circumference, with no water and no trees. The captain gathered his downcast crew and told them: let us fix a daily routine for our life on this island; let us make life on the island cheerful; and let no one fall ill. Four vows were made, and an orderly life together began. They distilled fresh water from the sea, kept themselves alive on sea birds and fish and turtles, and burned seal fat for their lamps. Surveying the island, building a pen for the turtles, putting up a watchtower and keeping a lookout by turns, and a study meeting every evening — the sixteen men piled up their days without one of them letting his spirit go to seed. They gave the sea turtles names and made pets of them, tamed the birds, and treated every living thing on the island as a companion. Nearly despairing at the sails that passed again and again, they kept the fire on the watchtower alight and waited more than twelve months. At last they were found by the Matoya-maru, and all sixteen came home alive, not one of them missing. A sailor who could not even write a letter sent home a fine one, and four of the young men took their navigating officers' certificates. When the long story was over and I looked up at Captain Nakagawa, the Milky Way could be seen between the yards, and there were tears running down my cheeks.

The opening (original text)

Captain Nakagawa's story This is an account of his own experience, told to me forty-six years ago by Nakagawa Kurakichi, our instructor, when I was aboard the training sailing ship Kotonoo-maru as a cadet of the Tokyo Higher Nautical School — a story that moved me from the bottom of my heart and that I shall never forget as long as I live.

Mujintō ni ikiru jūroku nin by Sugawa Kunihiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Captain Nakagawa's long story was over. I, Sugawa, looked about me like one waking from a dream. I had been so completely absorbed into the story that I had thought I was sitting cross-legged at the foot of a great cedar with its branches crossing overhead, in the guardian grove of Mera in the gathering dusk; but this great tree was the mast of the training ship Kotonoo-maru, and the great branches crossing above my head were the great yards. Between the yards I looked up at, the Milky Way could be seen. The night had grown late and everything was wet with dew, and together with the drops that fell from my cap, tears were running down my cheeks.

Mujintō ni ikiru jūroku nin by Sugawa Kunihiko (Aozora Bunko)
