About this work

Dazai's last critical work, serialized in Shinchō in the year he killed himself, and an open declaration of war on the literary establishment. He declares that the confidence of a certain group of aged masters rests on the egotism of the household, and attacks Shiga Naoya thoroughly, in all but name. Less criticism of works than words aimed at the way the writers themselves live, strung together at a possessed speed. An extraordinarily forceful piece in which Dazai's despair and his pride are both laid bare.

Summary

"There is no point in attacking other people. What should be attacked is their god. It is the enemy's god that ought to be struck" — quoting Valéry, Dazai declares that he will write out every month the protest he has held back and held back for ten years. His first target is "a certain set of grand old men." Where does their unshakable confidence come from? He has learned it at last, he says. It is the household. It is the egoism of the household. Is it not simply that their wives and children are dear to them? And he cuts at them together with a world that reveres as "admirable," "fastidious," "aristocratic" novels that merely arrange an expression to suit the taste of the patrons around them. Round by round the point of his pen grows fiercer, and turns at last into a direct address to someone plainly meant for Shiga Naoya: that he understands nothing whatever of Akutagawa's anguish; that not reading books is proof that a man is not alone; "a man who has written children's reading matter at a good age with great self-importance, and got carried away by it" — words that are half abuse and half a cry of pain. "At a newspaper round-table a member of the imperial family said, 'I read The Setting Sun eagerly, because it comes home to me.' Well, that is enough, is it not?" And with the line "Will you have Dazai killed? Word for word, I mean to write as much as it takes," the serial broke off. In June of that year Dazai threw himself into the Tamagawa Canal.

The opening (original text)

There is no point in attacking other people. What should be attacked is their god. It is the enemy's god that ought to be struck. But to strike it, one must first find the enemy's god. Men hide their true god well. This appears to be a murmur of the Frenchman Valéry's; and it seems that the "time" has come at last, not by my own will, when I must write out in this magazine (Shinchō), month after month, however unpleasant it may make people feel toward me, the things I have held back and held back for these ten years, angry as I have been.

Nyozegamon by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

You have been talking about aristocrats and I do not know what — (it is beyond me why everyone gets so worked up at the word "aristocrat") — but at a newspaper round-table a member of the imperial family said, "I read The Setting Sun eagerly, because it comes home to me." Well, that is enough, is it not? It is not a thing for you upstarts to know about. Jealousy. At your age, it is shameful. Will you have Dazai killed? Word for word, I mean to write as much as it takes.

Nyozegamon by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Let me say it in a word: you are wholly lacking in the capacity to love, to the same degree that you lack the capacity to suffer. You may caress, but you do not love.

A passage from the fierce rebuttal aimed at Shiga Naoya and other established masters, written right up until Dazai's death. Linking the capacity to suffer with the capacity to love, it states plainly what Dazai demanded of literature.
