About this work

The notes Akutagawa set down just after the Great Kantō Earthquake. It begins with his going about Kamakura in August saying that some great disturbance of nature must be coming, having seen wisteria, kerria and iris all in flower at once, and goes on to the notices posted in the burnt ruins, the friendliness of people who had fled, and his bitter anger over the library's books lost to the fire. The eye for observation and the humour working in the middle of a disaster become, for that very reason, a record of the time.

Summary

In August of Taishō 12 (1923), Akutagawa was staying with the painter Oana Ryūichi (Ittōtei) at the Hiranoya villa in Kamakura. Beneath the eaves' wisteria trellis, purple blossoms showed even though it was August; in the back garden a double-flowered kerria was blooming, and in the pond at Komachi-en, irises vied in bloom with lotus. "Counting off wisteria, kerria, iris — this, surely, is no ordinary thing. That Nature herself shows some sign of having gone mad is a fact hard to doubt." From then on, Akutagawa took to telling anyone whose face he happened to see that "some great calamity seems about to occur." No one took him seriously; Kume Masao, for one, mocked him, saying "Kikuchi Kan's gone soft in the nerves, has he." Akutagawa returned to Tokyo on August 25th, and the great earthquake struck eight days later. What follows are fragments written amid the ruins. On a single notice pasted up amid the burned ruins of Yoshiwara — "I am aboard a boat at the Hamachō riverbank. Sakuragawa Sankō" — he conjures the image of a professional entertainer who has come to rely on a boat as his home in the autumn wind, and is moved by its wit. He records how people who had taken refuge outdoors suddenly grew warm and neighborly, chatting freely with whoever happened to be nearby. Conversations with Kikuchi Kan, rumors and vigilante groups, how Tokyo had changed since the disaster — short fragments follow one after another. Near the end, Akutagawa's pen suddenly grows heated. That the university library's books burned was the university's own negligence — the storehouse itself poorly built, its holiday oversight slipshod. Above all, the crime of scholars who did nothing but bundle old books away on high shelves, never troubling to have them reprinted, begrudging others so much as a glimpse of their materials until at last those very materials went up in smoke, "deserves to be denounced to the beat of a drum."

The opening (original text)

One: Miscellaneous Notes on the Great Earthquake In August of Taishō 12, I went to Kamakura with Ittōtei and became a guest at the Hiranoya villa. The eaves of our room ran the whole length beneath a wisteria trellis. And here and there, among the leaves of that very trellis, purple flowers could be seen. A wisteria blooming in August is the stuff of chronicles.

Taishō jūninen kugatsu tsuitachi no daishin ni saishite by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

To press the matter still further: it was also wrong of the university to have done nothing but bundle its old books away on high shelves, never troubling to see them actively reprinted. The scholars' crime — hoarding their materials, begrudging others so much as a glimpse of them, and in the end letting those very materials go up in smoke — deserves to be denounced to the beat of a drum.
