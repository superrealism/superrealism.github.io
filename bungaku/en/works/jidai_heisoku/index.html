About this work

An essay Ishikawa Takuboku wrote in August 1910, subtitled "State Power, the End of Pure Naturalism, and a Consideration of Tomorrow." Written for the Tokyo Asahi Shimbun in answer to an essay by Uozumi Setsuro, it was not published in his lifetime and appeared in 1913, after his death. It analyses sharply the closing-off of an age in which the state, as a power, stands in the way of the individual, and argues that after naturalism has finished its work, young people should give up fancy, study today rigorously, and find in it what tomorrow requires. An essay important in the history of modern Japanese literature, showing where Takuboku's thought arrived after the shock of the High Treason Incident.

Summary

Taking as his starting point Uozumi Fūryo's essay "Naturalism as a Philosophy of Self-Assertion," Takuboku begins by pointing out that under the single label "naturalism," two mutually contradictory currents have been allowed to mingle without ever being properly sorted out: a self-assertive tendency, and a scientific, fatalistic, self-negating tendency ("pure naturalism"). This pure naturalism, he argues, has already arrived at a mere contemplative stance of reproducing reality exactly as it is, and has thereby exhausted its theoretical mission. And yet young people, having lost any center to their thinking, now find themselves in the very midst of the tragedy of a self split against itself. At the root of this impasse, Takuboku identifies a "blockage of the age": the state in which the "strong power" of the state stands blocking every possibility open to the individual. The way out of this, he insists, does not lie in fantasies of "the good" or "the beautiful." Rejecting every kind of fantasy outright, one must instead discover the one truth that remains — "necessity" — through the most rigorous, most daring, and freest possible study of "today." "Necessity is the most certain of all ideals." And literature, too, buried as it has been in its own age, will at last awaken as the fittest possible criticism of "today," once the hearts of young people have taken hold of "tomorrow." A landmark essay in literary history, showing the intellectual culmination Takuboku reached after being shaken by the High Treason Incident and drawn ever deeper toward socialism.

The opening (original text)

Mr. Uozumi's essay, titled "Naturalism as a Philosophy of Self-Assertion," which appeared a few days ago in this column, deserves attention for having pointed out, with relative clarity, one half — the neglected half — of the intellectual life led today by us young Japanese.

Jidai heisoku no genjō by Ishikawa Takuboku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Literature — which, ever since the age passed when the discovery and acknowledgment of "truth" in the first half of that naturalist movement carried the force of "criticism," has gradually inclined toward mere description, mere narrative — literature too, I believe, will thus have its sleeping spirit awakened once more. For it is only once the hearts of all of us young people have taken possession of "tomorrow" that everything of "today" will, for the first time, receive its truest and most fitting criticism. One cannot criticize one's own age while wholly submerged in it. What I ask of literature is criticism.

Jidai heisoku no genjō by Ishikawa Takuboku (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

The air that surrounds us young people no longer moves at all.

A passage from an essay written in 1910, as the arrests of the High Treason Incident went on, and never published in Ishikawa's lifetime. Expressing the suffocation of an age shut in by state power as stagnant air, it urged the young to awaken to their times.
