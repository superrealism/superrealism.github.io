About this work

Yamada Bimyō, who became a great figure of the literary world at a stroke with his colloquial style and then lost his popularity like a hand-held firework and came to a miserable end. Uchida Roan analyses with a contemporary's eye the glory and fall of a premature birth produced by the fever for things Western, taking in his impression at their first meeting and a comparison with Ozaki Kōyō.

Summary

A recollection in which Uchida Roan draws the rise and fall of Yamada Bimyō, the forerunner of the unified spoken and written style in Japan. Calling Bimyō, who created a strongly Western colloquial style at the very height of the fever for Europeanization and became at a bound a great figure of the literary world, "a premature child hatched in the incubator of the Europeanizing fever," he follows his appearance with "Musashino" and Natsukodachi, his heyday spanning the Ken'yūsha's Garakuta Bunko and the Miyako no Hana which he himself edited, and the great sensation caused by the nude drawing that accompanied "Butterfly" in the supplement to Kokumin no Tomo. He conveys Bimyō's wide reading and his youth through what he saw himself — their first meeting at the editorial office of the Kinkōdō, a visit to the gloomy tenement at Surugadai. He arranges the causes of the fall of his popularity under six heads — early ripening in boyhood, the wearing off of the novelty of his prose, meddling with criticism he did badly, the diffuseness that came of too many gifts, a narrow and insincere way with people, and the newspaper scandal over his relations with the woman writer Inabune — setting them against his sworn friend Kōyō, who spread his power by a gift for company. He gives Bimyō's self-abasing answer to the memorial volume for Futabatei, and the wretched end in which he died with cream puffs left at his pillow; and he closes by reproaching a society that would not forgive one lapse in a man of genius.

Characters

  • Yamada Bimyō　Bimyōsai Bimyō. He became at a bound a great figure of the literary world with the unified spoken and written style, and lost his popularity as quickly, and came to a wretched end
  • Ozaki Kōyō　Brought up in the same quarter as Bimyō, and one of the organizers of the Ken'yūsha with him, until they parted. By his gift for company he took Bimyō's place
  • Tazawa Inabune　The woman writer who became Bimyō's wife, and fell victim to tragedy soon after the marriage
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who exchanged letters with Bimyō and met him several times from his student days

The opening (original text)

Just when this Europeanism had reached its very height, when everything was the West, first and second the West, and high and low were beside themselves in praise of Western culture, the man who rode that tide and created an extremely Western colloquial style, and whose popularity boiled up at once so that he became at a bound a great figure of the literary world, was Yamada Bimyōsai. Bimyōsai was, as it were, a premature child hatched in the incubator of the Europeanizing fever. Before this, Bimyōsai had published as a separate volume a new-style poem singing an old tale of a beautiful youth of Satsuma. Unlike the student ballads in Doctor Toyama's manner, of the "six hundred horsemen riding into the place of death" sort, it was a real poem, stringing together elegant and rich lines of seven and five; but it did not draw enough notice to move the world.

Bimyōsai bimyō by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I heard the story some months later: at the last, it seems, Ishibashi Shian and Maruoka Kyūka looked after him entirely. When he had grown really bad, Kyūka heard that cream puffs were Bimyō's great favorite and took a box of them as a present. Bimyō was so delighted that his household too thanked him warmly and treasured them, and would let no one but the invalid — not even the children — touch them; and the moldy cream puffs were still at his pillow when he died. What a wretched piece of history for the end of the clever man of an age, the forerunner (or the founder) of the unified spoken and written style in Japan, who once raised such a storm in the literary world. That he brought so wretched a fate on himself was after all his own fault; but even if it was rust from his own body, the society that would not forgive one lapse in the founder of Japan's new prose was truly cruel.

Bimyōsai bimyō by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
