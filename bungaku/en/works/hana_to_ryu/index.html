About this work

An autobiographical novel drawing the world of the coal stevedores — the "gonzo" — of the port of Wakamatsu, modeled on the author's own parents, Tamai Kingorō and Man. It was serialized in the "Yomiuri Shimbun" from 1952 into the following year. Kingorō builds up a stevedores' company by the strength of his arm alone, and Man supports the house and the company with a spirit that gives nothing to her husband's. The title comes from the tattoo on Kingorō's arm: a rising dragon gripping a chrysanthemum flower. Drawing a port town from the end of Meiji into the early Shōwa years, where quarrels and obligation and human feeling swirl together, and folding his own upbringing into it, the author produced a representative work that has been filmed and dramatized again and again.

Summary

Man, living in a mountain village, leaves home to go to her elder brother, who works as a stevedore at Moji. The girl who gives up a valley the sun never reaches and goes down to the port meets there a man named Tamai Kingorō. At Wakamatsu, boiling with the shipping of coal, the stevedores called gonzo gather, and the bosses' territories and quarrels and obligations swirl together. Kingorō, who makes his name by his arm and his nerve, has a tattooist named Okyō put a rising dragon on his left arm, and makes the dragon's claw grip not a jewel but a chrysanthemum flower. Once he has set up house with Man and hung out the sign of the Tamai company, one hardship follows another: the scramble for cargo, the feud with Tomoda Kizō in the same trade, great affrays and the truces that end them, labor disputes and the police, fire and debt. Man keeps the business going with a strong heart, and more than once makes up her mind before her husband does and settles the matter. Their children, Katsunori among them, grow up, and the two of them carry in their hearts dreams of striking out for China or Brazil, but in the end put down their roots in the small world of Wakamatsu. Kingorō is fifty-eight, Man fifty-four. News of the February 26th Incident arrives, the China Incident follows, and the age opens out into a wholly new history.

Characters

  • Tamai Kingorō　A coal stevedore who built up the Tamai company by the strength of his arm. The author's father
  • Man　Kingorō's wife, who supports the house and the company with a strong heart. The author's mother
  • Okyō　The tattooist who puts the rising dragon on Kingorō's arm
  • Tomoda Kizō　A rival in the trade who contends with Kingorō at the port of Wakamatsu
  • Mori Shinnosuke　The friend with whom Kingorō shared his dreams
  • Tamai Katsunori　The son of Kingorō and Man — the author himself

The opening (original text)

Prologue A Woman Sets Out "It's very dark. Kinu, what time would it be now?" "It won't be three yet, surely." "Dear, dear. In this valley a day is only half as long as anywhere else. You can only get half a day's work done." "And in exchange the night is twice as long." "Twice as long, and no electric light, and oil dear, so there's nothing to do but sleep. It's easy to see why the people of this village keep going off to the cities. Some go as far as Hawaii or Brazil. Plenty of them do well, too. And the ones who do well never come back to a backwoods place like this. And they're right not to." "Man, it looks as though you have got it into your head to go as well. Your brother Rinsuke is over Kanmon way, they say. Is he keeping well?"

The ending (original text)

— and so they had ended by being buried in the small world of Wakamatsu, which they had kept at arm's length; but now they had no regrets about that either. Mori Shinnosuke, who had held the same dreams, had settled down as the proprietor of the Asuka and the Hiwakaza. A week after the celebration, on the twenty-sixth of February, a mutiny of the army broke out in Tokyo. Many leading men of the government were attacked. There was heavy snow. Martial law was declared in the capital, and the sign of something dreadful in this affair left the people uneasy. On the seventh of July, 1937, the China Incident broke out. From this time on, the age was to unroll a wholly new history. What dreams would be born in it, and what dreams would collapse, there was of course no telling. Tamai Kingorō, fifty-eight years old. Tamai Man, fifty-four years old.
