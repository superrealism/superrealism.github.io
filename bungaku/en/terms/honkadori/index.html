What is Honkadori (Allusive Variation)?

Category
Style & Technique

A technique of consciously incorporating phrases from a well-known older poem (the honka, or "source poem") to compose a new poem, layering the earlier poem's scene and mood behind it. This is not plagiarism; the point is to let the reader, who is presumed to know the source poem, savor the depth that arises between the two poems. Fujiwara no Shunzei prized this for the beauty of lingering suggestion it created, and his son Teika, in works such as "Kindai Shūka" and "Eiga no Taigai," set out detailed rules of practice — that one should borrow no more than about two phrases from the source poem, and shift the season or theme so as not to overlap with it. Source poems were drawn from poems everyone who studied waka would know, such as those in the "Kokin Wakashū" or "The Tales of Ise." The poems of the "Shin Kokin Wakashū" (1205), which made rich use of this technique, create a world of mysterious depth built from many layers of folded memory. When a work other than waka — a monogatari or Chinese poem — is drawn on, it is instead called "honsetsudori." The same technique was carried over into renga and haikai. This idea of creating a new work on the foundation of an earlier one runs through to the adaptations of the modern era and to Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's court tales drawn from classical setsuwa, and can be called one of the methods flowing beneath the surface of Japanese literature.
