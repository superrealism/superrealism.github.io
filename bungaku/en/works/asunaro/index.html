About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Shōsetsu Shinchō in 1958. Set on the wharf at Shibakanasugi in Edo, it draws the people who live in the shadows. It entrusts the joys and sorrows of those who are never rewarded to the name of the asunarō tree — which wishes to be a cypress tomorrow and never becomes one. A piece with a great deal of flavour.

Summary

A man in the manner of a farmer, his face wrapped in a grimy hand towel, was pacing back and forth at dusk along the Kappa riverbank at Shibakanasugi. The row of shops on one side, facing the Kanasugi river, was busy with people out enjoying the cool of evening, but no one paid the man any attention. To a craftsman-looking man coming out of a tavern called Nadakyū, the farmer-looking man swiftly draws near, and the two exchange a whisper. Set in the back streets of Edo, the story draws the precarious, poignant human pattern of people living in the shadows. "Asunarō" — the name of a tree that wishes tomorrow to become a cypress, and never does — is entrusted with the sorrows and joys of those who are never rewarded. At the story's close, in the fog, a man's dead body lies fallen. "Gang trouble, I'd guess — happened somewhere around here, and he made it this far before he died," a passing fisherman murmurs, walking carefully so as not to step in the blood, "let's hope it doesn't turn into trouble at the guardhouse." The two men walk off into the fog. A piece of Yamamoto's, drawing with restrained prose the sorrows and joys of those living at the bottom of the ordinary streets.

The opening (original text)

A man in the manner of a farmer, his face wrapped in a grimy hand towel, had been pacing back and forth for some time now along the Kappa riverbank at Shibakanasugi. Day had fully given way to night, and the row of shops on one side, facing the Kanasugi river, was busy with people out enjoying the cool of evening, but no one paid the man any attention.

Asunarō by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Gang trouble, I'd guess," one of them said. "Happened somewhere around here, and he made it this far before he died." "Hey, watch it," his companion said, pointing at the ground. "There's blood there, look, and here too — mind you don't step in it." The first fisherman stepped aside, and, walking carefully, said, "Let's hope it doesn't turn into trouble at the guardhouse." The two of them walked off into the fog.
