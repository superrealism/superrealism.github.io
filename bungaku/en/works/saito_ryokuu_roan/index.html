About this work

"I have the honour to announce that on this day of this month I died in good order" — a recollection of the ironist Saitō Ryokuu, who left his own death notice behind when he went. It draws with Uchida Roan's particular affection the bearing and temper of a strange talent who took sharp aphorism and cold laughter for his weapons as the last light of Edo gesaku, his friendships, and the late years in which, unfortunate, he grew more given to complaint.

Summary

The author reminisces about the writer Saitō Ryokū, who left this world after placing, in the newspaper, a notice of his own death: "I have the honor to announce my own happy demise, effective this very day." Roan places Ryokū as the last spark of the gesaku literature of the Kasei era, holding that with Ryokū's death, true literature of the Edo-born townsman came to an end. He paints, vividly, the figure and temperament of Ryokū — the cold, faint sneer that played across his thin face, worth a thousand ryō in charm; taciturn, yet given to epigrams that, in a single word, struck at truths that duller men could not have expressed in a thousand. He traces their friendship from its start in Meiji 23, with a letter Ryokū sent from a hot-spring resort, its haiku written in the fashionable Danrin style, through the years in which Ryokū, allied with men like Shōyō and Ōgai and keenly alert to new literary currents, nonetheless kept his roots firmly in the lineage of the Edo gesaku writers, dismissing questions of life and society as vulgar and unrefined — down to his final years, in which, as the world came to praise him and his own self-regard swelled, the edge of his irony dulled, and, sunk in disappointment, he grew ever more given to complaint. After a lonely end, with no friend at his bedside, Roan describes the rain-soaked memorial service at which Rohan read a mournful elegy, and, naming Aburajigoku and Amagaeru as Ryokū's masterpieces, mourns this singular talent of his generation, a man who gave his life to the aesthetic of old Edo.

Characters

  • Saitō Ryokū　A writer and critic who wielded irony and epigram as his weapons. The last of the line descended from Edo gesaku literature. Died leaving behind a notice of his own death. Also known by the pen name Shōjiki Shōdayū
  • Uchida Roan　The author. Associated with Ryokū from Meiji 23 onward
  • Kōda Rohan　The writer to whom Ryokū was closest of heart. Read the elegy at his memorial service
  • Ozaki Kōyō　A writer who likewise drew on the lineage of Edo-born writers. Discussed here in contrast to Ryokū

The opening (original text)

That Saitō Ryokū should have placed, in the newspaper, a notice of his own death — "I have the honor to announce my own happy demise, effective this very day" — was less an ironic farewell to life than the very last flourish of wit an Edo-born writer could give, the last spark of the gesaku literature of the Kasei era. With Ryokū's death, true literature of the Edo-born townsman came to an end. Kōyō, too, drew on the lineage of Edo-born writers, but though the son of a commoner, he was raised in the samurai quarters of the hilly Yamanote district and breathed the air of the university. Ryokū, though born into a samurai family, was raised in the downtown district and steeped himself, far more deeply, in the true spirit of Edo. That final self-composed death notice was, in the same vein as the mocking death-poems of writers like Sanba and Ikku and other Edo authors, a jest peculiar to Edo-born writers, and Ryokū, right up to the very moment of his death, was still, in evident good spirits, singing out the old Edo gesaku writers' philosophy that a life in this floating world is worth next to nothing.

Saitō ryokū by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And yet Ryokū's true masterpieces are, after all, surely Aburajigoku and Amagaeru. Both of these were works in a style Ryokū himself was not particularly skilled in, and which he took up, almost out of spite, because he was irritated at being praised for other things — and yet they possess, unmistakably, a more lasting vitality than works like Kakurenbo or Kadojamisen, in which he himself took the greater pride. He had a keen and discerning eye, but, drawing still on the lineage of the Edo writers, he took his pride in a literary "wit" honed and refined from the polish of clever turns of phrase and popular ditties, and while he resented that his own "self-satisfied" style found no favor with the wider world, there was, too, something in him that took a certain pride precisely in being understood by no one. And yet, now that even Kōyō and Rohan have grown tiresome to readers, Ryokū alone continues, even now, to be read, and the fact that a reduced-format edition of his complete works, published last year, sold in considerable numbers shows that his devoted admirers have not yet died out. No doubt Ryokū lies beneath the moss even now, grinning to himself in satisfaction. As the last of a line descending from the Edo writers' tradition, his work has its reason for existing, as the anguished struggle of a spoiled child of a transitional age, thwarted in his fortunes. And yet, as the world grows ever more calculating and hard-hearted, I cannot help feeling a certain desolation at the thought that a man like Ryokū will surely never appear again.

Saitō ryokū by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
