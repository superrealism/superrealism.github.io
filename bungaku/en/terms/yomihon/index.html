What is Yomihon?

Category
Form / Genre

Fiction of the mid-to-late Edo period that, as against the kusazōshi, in which pictures were primary, placed its weight on text to be read. Written in a mixed classical-colloquial style learned from Chinese vernacular fiction, it is distinguished by its meticulously constructed romances of the fantastic. It begins with Tsuga Teishō's "Hanabusa Zōshi" (1749); the first phase centered on the Kamigata region, and Ueda Akinari's "Ugetsu Monogatari" ("Tales of Moonlight and Rain," 1776), depicting the uncanny and human passion in dignified prose, reached its peak. The second phase shifted to Edo, and, once Santō Kyōden's "Chūshin Suikoden" (1799) succeeded and publishers began to invest more heavily, Kyokutei Bakin brought it to its culmination with "Nansō Satomi Hakkenden" ("The Eight Dog Chronicles," 1814–42), the tale of the eight dog warriors, which he kept writing for twenty-eight years. Built on a framework of karmic retribution and the reward of good and the punishment of evil, this conception ceded its place to the modern novel once Tsubouchi Shōyō criticized it in the Meiji era for "not depicting human feeling," but its grand imaginative power was carried forward into the historical fiction and romances of the fantastic of later ages. Uchida Roan's "Hakkenden Dan'yo" and Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's "Gesaku Zanmai" are works in which modern writers looked back on this world.
