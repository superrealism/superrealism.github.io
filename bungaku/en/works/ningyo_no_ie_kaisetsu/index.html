About this work

The commentary Shimamura Hōgetsu attached in 1913 to his own translation of Ibsen's A Doll's House. Beginning from a complete list of Ibsen's works, he traces carefully the reaction to it as a play of the woman question, the happy ending Ibsen was forced to write for popular taste and the letter in which he cursed it, a comparison of the published draft with the finished text, and the history of its performance in various countries. At the end he records that in 1911 the Literary Society staged all three acts at the Imperial Theatre with Matsui Sumako as Nora — a record, left by one of the participants, of the starting point of modern theatre in Japan.

Summary

Hōgetsu first sets out Ibsen's life and a full list of his works, and confirms that in his plays of contemporary society he became the father and the king of the modern drama. Everyone thinks at once of the woman question when A Doll's House is named; but the argument whether it is good or bad to make a problem into art is useless, says Hōgetsu. The aim of a play as art lies in striking at our life, and whether its means and its material are a social problem is not the question. Citing Gosse's Life of Ibsen, he reports that the scene in the last act in which Nora comes out of the bedroom and confounds Helmer and the audience gave the sense that "something new has been born on the stage," and that Nora's declaration of independence rang through all Scandinavia, so that people came out of the theater every night pale with excitement and arguing. And yet, unless something radiates from behind the problem and the thought, there is no difference between it and a piece of platform agitation. What radiates from behind art is the light and the heat of life, Hōgetsu explains. He quotes Ibsen's own speech at the banquet of the Norwegian Women's Rights League, in which he declined the honor of having worked for the cause of women, saying, "My task has been the description of humanity." Hōgetsu then argues that the germ of the play appears already ten years earlier in Selma's speech in The League of Youth, and that the resemblance to Villiers de l'Isle-Adam's La Révolte is best taken for coincidence; and further he gives in full a translation of the alternative ending Ibsen himself wrote under pressure from popular taste — Nora sinking down at the door of the children's room and Helmer crying "Nora!" in joy. Ibsen cursed it as "a barbaric outrage upon the play," and wrote letters to theater managers and newspapers explaining how it came about. Hōgetsu further compares the published draft with the finished text. The note of the idea that "there are two kinds of spiritual law, two kinds of conscience, one for man and another quite different for woman." In the draft there is as yet no sign of the tarantella, nor the symbolic line as Nora goes into the other room — "I am taking off my doll's dress" — nor the cry of women's public anger, "and millions of women have sacrificed it." At the end the history of its performance is traced like a map of the world. From the first performance at the Royal Theatre in Copenhagen in 1879 to Germany, Vienna, Russia, Paris, England, America. And in Japan, in the forty-fourth year of Meiji, the Literary Society staged all three acts at the Imperial Theatre with Matsui Sumako as Nora. "This was the first performance of A Doll's House in our country, and at the same time the first serious play, in the wider field of the modern drama, to succeed with actresses in the leading parts instead of the male actors called onnagata" — and with that Hōgetsu closes his long commentary.

The opening (original text)

Henrik Ibsen, the author of A Doll's House, was born on the twentieth of March 1828 in the small town of Skien in Norway, and died on the twenty-third of May 1906 at the age of seventy-nine in Christiania, the capital of the same country. From his thirty-seventh to his sixty-third year, the twenty-seven years of the prime of his life, he was out of favor at home and passed a wandering life in Germany and Italy and elsewhere, and in that time he made more than ten plays, from Brand down to Hedda Gabler.

Ningyō no ie kaisetsu by Shimamura Hōgetsu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This was the first performance of A Doll's House in our country, and at the same time the first serious play, in the wider field of the modern drama, to succeed with actresses in the leading parts instead of the male actors hitherto called onnagata. The same company opened with A Doll's House for a week from the fourteenth of March in the forty-fifth year of Meiji at the Naka-za in Osaka, and there too it did well. Such, in outline, is the history of A Doll's House. March, the second year of Taishō Hōgetsu
