About this work

A collection of short songs Takehisa Yumeji compiled with his own pictures. As its dedication says — that these are the dear little songs of his boyhood — it takes as its core the lullabies and play songs he heard as a child, and sets down small scenes such as a singing clock, a linked-finger promise, a spinning wheel, a child-buyer, in plain measures rich in hiragana. It conveys well the poet in the Yumeji known for "Evening Primrose," and is a representative book of Taishō romanticism, in which picture and poem cannot be separated.

Summary

"Now I would send this one volume to the little companions of days gone by. O-Hana, O-Chō, O-Koma, Koharu; Tarō, Jirō, Sōnosuke" — beginning with this dedication to the childhood friends who once listened to his grass whistle, the collection picks up one memory of boyhood after another. "The Singing Clock," heard from a white bed at morning, on the border of dream and waking, when he notices that his mother is not beside him. "Pinky Promise," the childhood day when they linked fingers and swore, "Holy Mary, never, never shall I tell a lie." "The Spinning Wheel," turning quietly through a white, drowsy spring noon, drawing out a thread of white, sorrowful dream. "The Man-Buyer" in his blue hood, peeping from behind a wall in the half-light of an autumn sunset. The innocence of a nursery song and the helplessness just behind it stand back to back within the soft run of the kana. And at length the time of parting comes. "Joy," they wrote, and then, "Good-bye."

The opening (original text)

These are the dear little songs of my boyhood. Now I would send this one volume to the little companions of days gone by. O-Hana, O-Chō, O-Koma, Koharu; Tarō, Jirō, Sōnosuke. Truly you were the first listeners to my poor grass whistle. The Singing Clock On the border of dream and waking, the faintly white bed of morning. Because my mother is not beside me, that song the singing clock sings — why should it be so sorrowful?

Dontaku by Takehisa Yumeji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The two of them wrote, "Things unknown." The two of them wrote, "Joy." The two of them wrote, "Good-bye."

Dontaku by Takehisa Yumeji (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Waiting and waiting for someone who does not come — the forlornness of the evening primrose; tonight, it seems, even the moon will not come out.

“Evening Primrose,” from the collection Dontaku. This three-line poem, likening a person who waits in vain to the evening primrose, was later set to music and widely sung in the Taishō era.
