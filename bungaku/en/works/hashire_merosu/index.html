About this work

A short story published in the magazine Shinchō in 1940. Drawing on an ancient Greek legend and Schiller's poem "The Hostage," it tells of friendship and good faith winning out against suspicion. A staple of Japanese school textbooks, it is among the best-loved works of Dazai's middle period.

Summary

Melos, a shepherd, is enraged by King Dionys, a tyrant who can trust no one and goes on putting his subjects to death; he storms the castle and is seized. Asking three days' grace to see his sister married, Melos leaves his friend Selinuntius, a stonemason, as hostage and runs for his village. On the way back from the wedding a flooded river, brigands, and finally exhaustion and despair fall upon him, and he comes close to throwing everything away. Water from a spring restores him, and he runs on — because he is believed in — reaching the execution ground at the last moment before sunset. The two confess to each other that each had wavered once, strike each other, and then embrace. The king, moved by what he has seen, repents and asks to be admitted among their number.

Characters

  • Melos　A shepherd with a fierce sense of justice, devoted to his sister and incapable of enduring wickedness
  • Selinuntius　A stonemason and Melos's friend since childhood, who takes his place as hostage without a word
  • King Dionys　The tyrant of Syracuse, whose mistrust of humankind keeps him executing his subjects
  • Melos's sister　A shy girl of sixteen, the bride whose wedding sets the story in motion
  • Philostratus　Selinuntius's apprentice, who runs alongside Melos toward the execution ground

The opening (original text)

Melos was enraged. He resolved that he must rid the land of that wicked and violent king. Melos knew nothing of politics. He was a shepherd of the village, who had lived playing his flute among his sheep. But to evil he was more sensitive than other men.

The ending (original text)

"Melos — you are naked. Put this cloak on, quickly. This dear girl cannot bear to have everyone looking at you as you are." The hero blushed a deep red.

Famous passages

Melos was enraged. He resolved that the wicked, tyrannical king must be done away with, without fail.

The opening line that sets the story in motion. In a single breath it establishes Melos as a man who acts before he reasons, a character who runs before he thinks.

The brave man blushed deep red.

The last line, in which, having run the whole way, he is handed a cloak by a girl and realizes he is naked. It brings the heroic tale down to earth with human comedy.

That is why I run. I run because I am trusted. Whether I make it in time or not is not the question. A man's life is not the question either. I am running, somehow, for something far more terrible and great.

Melos's answer to Philostratus, who urges him to stop because he can no longer make it in time. The heart of the story is spoken here: running not merely to keep a promise, but for trust itself.

Faith, truly, was never an empty delusion.

The king's words on seeing Melos, who returned as he promised, and Selinuntius, who waited trusting him. The story's culmination, in which a tyrant who could trust no one has a change of heart.

I must repay their trust. Now there is only that one thing. Run! Melos.

The scene in which Melos, having nearly given up, drinks from a spring and rises again. This single word of self-exhortation became the very title of the work.

With the mouth, a man can say anything, however pure. But I cannot help seeing straight through to the very bottom of a man's bowels.

What the tyrant King Dionys flings at the captured Melos. The suspicion of a king who can no longer believe in people stands directly against the faithfulness that the story puts to the test.

“Selinuntius,” said Melos, tears in his eyes. “Strike me. Strike my cheek with all your strength. Once, on the way, I had a bad dream. If you do not strike me, I am not worthy even to embrace you. Strike me.” Selinuntius nodded as though he understood everything, and struck Melos on the right cheek so loudly that it rang through the whole place of execution. Then, smiling gently, he said, “Melos, strike me. Strike my cheek just as loudly. Once in these three days — only once — I doubted you for a moment. For the first time in my life, I doubted you. Unless you strike me, I cannot embrace you.”

The scene in which the two friends, reunited at the place of execution, have each other strike them on the cheek. Their integrity in refusing to forgive themselves for doubting the other even once forms the climax of this story about faithfulness.
