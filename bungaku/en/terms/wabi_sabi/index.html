What is Wabi and Sabi?

Category
Other

An aesthetic sensibility, cultivated from the medieval into the early modern period, that finds deep beauty in things simple and lacking, or aged and faded. "Wabi" derives from the verb "wabu," meaning to feel discouraged when things do not go as one wishes, and "sabi" from "sabu," meaning to grow old and fade in color; both were originally words lamenting a state of deficiency. In the medieval period, through a reversal of values — that beauty lies precisely in what is lacking — they turned into aesthetic principles. In the way of tea, Murata Jukō, then Takeno Jōō, and finally Sen no Rikyū rejected extravagant Chinese wares and spacious rooms and sought the richness of the spirit in a plain thatched hut, perfecting wabi-cha ("tea of wabi"). In haikai, Matsuo Bashō placed "sabi" — the sense of clear, quiet charm found in stillness — at the center of the Bashō-school principles. Okakura Tenshin's English-language "The Book of Tea" (1906) is a work that explained this heart that reveres the imperfect as the core of Japanese culture to the West. Today the two words are often run together as a single term, "wabi-sabi," but they were originally separate principles cultivated in the separate settings of the tea ceremony and haikai, respectively. Alongside yūgen and mono no aware, it stands as one of the basic terms for speaking of Japanese beauty.
