About this work

Dickens's famous story (1843) in which Scrooge, a mean and pitiless old merchant, is led by three spirits on Christmas Eve to see the past, the present and the future, and is born again. This is the version translated by Morita Sōhei, a writer of Natsume Sōseki's circle, which came out in 1929 as an Iwanami Bunko volume. It is said to have shaped the very way Christmas is kept in Europe and America today, and, borrowing the frame of a ghost story, it treats poverty, charity and repentance. It is known for Tiny Tim's words: "God bless us, every one!"

Summary

Seven years since his partner Marley died. Scrooge, a moneychanger in London, has become an old man so mean and cold that neither the weather nor human kindness can get at him. On Christmas Eve he refuses his nephew's invitation, drives away the gentlemen asking for a subscription, and grudgingly allows his clerk Bob Cratchit the day off. That night the ghost of Marley appears dragging chains and cash boxes, and tells him that if he would avoid the same end he must receive the visits of three spirits. The first spirit takes him to Christmases past: himself as a boy left behind at the boarding school, his gentle sister, old Fezziwig's ball, and the day his betrothed left him for his grasping after money. The second shows him the present: the Cratchits' table kept on a few shillings, the lame Tiny Tim, the laughter ringing through his nephew's house. And the third, silent and robed in black, shows him a deathbed no one mourns, people stripping even the bedclothes to sell, and Tiny Tim's empty stool. Reading his own name on a gravestone, Scrooge swears to change, and wakes. Outside the window it is Christmas morning. He sends a turkey, calls at his nephew's house, raises his clerk's wages, and becomes a second father to Tiny Tim.

Characters

  • Scrooge　A mean and pitiless old merchant of London
  • Marley　His partner, dead these seven years, who appears as a ghost dragging chains
  • Bob Cratchit　Scrooge's clerk, who works for a meager wage
  • Tiny Tim　Cratchit's youngest child, lame and on a crutch
  • The three spirits　They show Scrooge the Christmases of the past, the present and the future

The opening (original text)

Stave One: Marley's Ghost Marley was dead, to begin with. There is no doubt whatever about that. The register of his burial was signed by the clergyman, the clerk, the undertaker, and the chief mourner. Scrooge signed it; and Scrooge's name was good upon 'Change for anything he chose to put his hand to. Old Marley was as dead as a doornail. Mind! I don't mean to say that I know of my own knowledge what there is particularly dead about a doornail. I might have been inclined, myself, to regard a coffin-nail as the deadest piece of ironmongery in the trade. But the wisdom of our ancestors is in the simile; and my unhallowed hands shall not disturb it.

A Christmas Carol by Morita Sōhei (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

His own heart laughed, and that was quite enough for him. He had no further intercourse with Spirits, but lived upon the Total Abstinence Principle ever afterwards; and it was always said of him that he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed the knowledge. May that be truly said of us, and all of us! And so, as Tiny Tim observed, God bless us, every one!

A Christmas Carol by Morita Sōhei (Aozora Bunko)
