About this work

An early short story drawing a night in which the nerves are worked on by a ghost story. Published in the coterie magazine Shichinin, run by Osanai Kaoru and others, in May 1905, and collected in the early volume Yōkyoshū. A story he hears from a psychologist friend, about a wife whose soul visited her husband at the moment of her death, joins itself to his anxiety about a fiancée laid up with influenza, and the darkness on the way home and the words of a superstitious old servant swell his delusions one after another. A light-voiced piece on how the mind distorts reality.

Summary

"I," who lives with an old woman servant in a rented house in Koishikawa, pays a visit, after a long absence, to the lodgings of my friend Tsuda. Tsuda, who studies ghosts, tells me a story: that a young wife, who had died of pneumonia after influenza, appeared at the very moment of her death in the pocket mirror of her husband, then away at the front. Hearing that my own betrothed, Tsuyuko, is likewise laid up with the flu, Tsuda presses upon me, "Do watch carefully." As ill luck would have it, the way home lies through a dark, rainy night. A white kimono passing by, a coffin going past, a dog's distant howl, the sound of a koto drifting from the house next door — and, joined by the superstitious old woman's own fearful talk, I become possessed by the delusion that Tsuyuko is already dead, and pass the night without a wink of sleep. The next morning, unable to bear it any longer, I rush off to Yotsuya to see for myself.

Characters

  • I　A bachelor of law employed in Shiba. Keeps a house in Koishikawa with an old woman servant. The narrator
  • Tsuda　A friend of mine, a bachelor of letters. Studies ghosts from the standpoint of psychology
  • Tsuyuko　My betrothed. Daughter of the Uno family of Yotsuya, laid up with the flu
  • The old woman　My household's maidservant. A superstitious old woman who says a dog's distant howl portends misfortune

The opening (original text)

"How rare of you — it's been a good while since you last came," Tsuda said, trimming down the wick of a lamp that had been turned up too high. As Tsuda said this, I sat turning the foot of a Sōma-ware teacup round and round between three fingers, resting on trousers so tight at the knee they looked ready to split, and fell to thinking. Rare indeed, no doubt of it — I had not once called at Tsuda's lodgings since we last met face to face at New Year's, clear up to this very day when the blossoms are at their height.

Koto no sorane by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Tsuyuko's silvery laugh, the old woman's brassy laugh, and my own bronze-toned laugh blended together so merrily that all the spring of this whole wide world seemed gathered into our seven-yen-fifty rented house. Even the badger of Genbei Village, I daresay, could not have raised so loud a racket as that. Perhaps it was only my imagination, but after that Tsuyuko seemed, more than ever before, to show every sign of loving me all the more. When I met Tsuda and told him the whole course of that night's events, leaving nothing out, he said it was fine material and begged me to let him put it into his own book. It is I who appear, under the name of "Mr. K," on page seventy-two of the Bachelor of Letters Tsuda Masakata's published treatise, "A Theory of Ghosts."
