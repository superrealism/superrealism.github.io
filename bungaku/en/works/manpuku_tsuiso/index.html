About this work

A late short story written early in 1938. Its setting is the construction site in the gorge of the middle Tenryū river, where Hayama himself worked as a navvy and timekeeper. Before the eyes of the narrator, who serves as the man who gives the signal for blasting, Manpuku — a child of six who came over from Korea less than a year ago — takes a flying stone on the forehead. The wound is slight and he is told it will heal in a week, and yet the child stops being able to eat. A writer who kept to the workers' side sets down, with sentiment refused, the bottom of a shanty quarter where five to seven thousand labourers and their families lived.

Summary

In a gorge along the middle reaches of the Tenryū River. For the construction of a power plant and electric railway line, a barrack settlement had sprung up at the bottom of the gorge, home to some five to seven thousand laborers and their families. In the mountain stream, the children gathered firewood, walnuts, and chestnuts, and caught river crabs. To lay the tramway tracks, bedrock and great boulders had to be cleared by blasting, and 'I' was the signalman who went about ringing a bell and shouting, 'Blasting now!' On a mild day in mid-November, just after a charge Ōta had set gave off a shallow report, a fragment struck the nose of a child running up from downstream. It was Manpuku, Ōta's little cousin, six years old, who had come from Korea to the mainland less than a year before. The wound was at the vital point between his brows, but the bleeding stopped quickly, and the doctor said he would fully recover within a week. Yet a week later, when I visited the workers' quarters, his father said, 'His injury has healed already, sir, but ever since then, he hasn't been able to eat.' The father himself, his back struck by a falling rock half a year before, could no longer bend his own leg, and lived on sixty sen a day in injury relief. The construction company would have nothing to do with it. In a dark corner of the workers' quarters, Manpuku's once round cheeks had grown hollow, like those of a prisoner in a sickroom. All 'I' could do was gently stroke his head with my own calloused, blistered palm. Before long, Manpuku died. On the road to fetch boards for his coffin, the children on the riverbank were playing with piglets and kid goats. In the doctor's sitting room, an argument dragged on between the father and the construction company's side, over the diagnosis: one doctor said the cause of death was a stomach ailment, unrelated to the injury, while his adopted son, a physician himself, had plainly stated that the injury had damaged the nervous system and caused the death.

Characters

  • I　A laborer and bookkeeper at the construction site. Serves as the signalman for the blasting
  • Manpuku　A six-year-old child who came from Korea less than a year before. Struck on the forehead by a fragment from the blasting
  • Manpuku's father　His leg left unable to bend after a rockfall half a year earlier, he lives on sixty sen a day in injury relief and depends on his younger brother's support
  • Ōta　A young laborer who sets the blasting charges. Manpuku's older cousin

The opening (original text)

The mountain stream carried walnuts, chestnuts, and the like down with its floodwaters. Because the water receded quickly, it left them behind among the rocks and at the roots of driftwood. The children of the construction site would gather up the sun-bleached driftwood, worn bone-white, to use as firewood, and would gather walnuts and chestnuts for their own snacks.

Manpuku tsuisō by Hayama Yoshiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The matter would not be settled, no matter how long they talked. The reason was this: the doctor's own diagnosis held that the cause of death lay in a stomach ailment, and not in the injury — but when the doctor had been away and his adopted son, a physician himself, had examined the boy, he had plainly stated that the injury had been the cause, having damaged the nervous system. The former diagnosis was inconvenient for the patient's side, and convenient for the company and the construction crew. The latter was exactly the reverse. I was taught, through one sad death, that there could be two such opposing positions.
