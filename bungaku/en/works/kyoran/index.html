About this work

The second of Chikamatsu Shūkō's so-called Black Hair trilogy: an I-novel drawing the wretched, almost comic obsession of a man fixed on a woman in Kyoto. It begins with his jealousy over two photographs found in a household shrine, and his suspicion and lingering attachment whirl until he falls into a frenzy he cannot govern. The thoroughness with which he writes out his own ugliness, without a shred of shame, has been called the far north of the Japanese I-novel, and settled Shūkō's name as a master of the fiction of infatuation.

Summary

From inside the woman's household altar there came photographs of two men. Though he knows they are no longer in this world, "I" feel an ungraspable jealousy at their remaining in her breast as fond memories. If he says show me, she hides them in a moment in a small drawer of the chest. A man of about thirty in haori and hakama, a man of about forty in Western dress — probably, he guesses, the Japanese-style painter who died last summer; and his imagining swells without limit. The joy that the woman is now his alone turns at once dreary, and a treasure not to be exchanged for life itself begins to seem a worthless thing. Doubt calls up doubt and prying calls up prying, and "I" cannot help laying bare the whole of the woman's past. In time she disappears, and "I" go about asking for her without caring how it looks, from the streets of Kyoto to the road over the mountains. Unable, after all, to accept a young packhorse man's kindness in offering him a ride on his cart, and seeing range upon range of mountains ahead, he gives it up at last and turns back. As he crossed the plank bridge over the Kizu river, a river wind with snow in it cut at his cheek.

Characters

  • I　A man who clings to a woman in Kyoto and is driven mad by jealousy and obsession
  • The woman　The one he broods over, who once had dealings with the men in the photographs
  • The mother　The woman's mother, who seems to be hiding her daughter's whereabouts

The opening (original text)

I The photographs of the two men were found inside the household altar. That they were people no longer in this world I understood of itself; but thinking that even after their deaths they remained forever in her breast as the images of fond memory, I could not help feeling, after all, a strange jealousy that I could not get hold of.

Kyōran by Chikamatsu Shūkō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Come, get on," he said. In my heart I thanked the young packhorse man for his kindness, but I could not, after all, bring myself to get onto his cart. What fate is it, I thought, pressed by my feelings, that makes me unable to give that woman up; and looking ahead, there was the air of deep mountain upon mountain ranged under a gray sky. "No, I shall give it up," I said to the young packhorse man, and at last turned back. And when I had crossed the long plank bridge over the Kizu river again, a cold river wind with snow in it came blowing off the water and cut at my cheek.
