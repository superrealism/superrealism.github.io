Famous passages by Masaoka Shiki

Well-known lines from the works of Masaoka Shiki.

7 passages

Letters to a Tanka Poet

Tsurayuki was a poor poet, and the Kokinshū is a worthless collection.

A line that cuts head-on into a tanka establishment that had long held the Kokinshū supreme. Shiki's reform of tanka poetry began with this declaration.

Bokujū itteki

The sprays of wisteria set in the vase are short, and so they do not reach down to the tatami.

The first of a sequence of poems on the wisteria that the bedridden Shiki had arranged on his desk. Recording only the plain fact, just as seen, that the short sprays do not reach the tatami, it conveys the gaze of a sick man unable to rise. A representative poem of the “sketch from life” he championed.

In my view, the tanka of the two of us rest on entirely different standards: if Tekkan is right, then Shiki is wrong; if Shiki is right, then Tekkan is wrong. Tekkan and Shiki are not to be spoken of in the same breath.

His rebuttal to being discussed in the same breath as Yosano Tekkan. Declaring that their tanka rest on wholly different standards, so that if one is right the other is wrong, it makes plain Shiki's position as the champion of sketching from life.

The iris has come into bloom — and before my eyes, this spring, which for me will be the last, is about to pass.

A poem from a sequence written in the spring of the year before his death, under the note “forcing myself to take up the brush.” Before the newly blooming iris, he quietly watches this spring go by as the last he will have.

A Sickbed Six Feet Long

This sickbed, six feet long, is my whole world.

The opening of the essays he kept writing for the newspaper from his deathbed. Shiki's fierce resolve is fixed on a sickbed a mere six feet long, which he calls his whole world.

Until now I had misunderstood what Zen calls enlightenment. I had thought enlightenment meant being able to die calmly, whatever the circumstance — but I was wrong. Enlightenment means being able to go on living calmly, whatever the circumstance.

A passage from the essays kept written from his deathbed. Restating enlightenment not as dying calmly but as living calmly, it is celebrated as the point Shiki finally reached.

Ideals are the sort of thing that try to leap onto the roof in a single breath and often fall into the pond instead. Sketching from life is plain, but in return it makes no such blunders.

A passage on the meaning of sketching from life. Likening idealism to someone who tries to leap onto the roof in one breath and falls into the pond, it argues for the reliability of plain description. Shiki's view of literature is set out here in an easily grasped form.
