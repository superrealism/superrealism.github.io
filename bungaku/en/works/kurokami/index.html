About this work

The central work of Chikamatsu Shūkō's fiction of infatuation, and the first of the trilogy that continues with "Frenzy" and "A Night of Freezing Frost." The narrator, deeply fixed on a woman of the Gion quarter in Kyoto, is played with by her, suspects her, and still cannot keep away — set down in an unconcealed first person. As an I-novel so thorough that it lays bare even his own obsession and disgrace, it marked one limit of naturalist literature.

Summary

"I" have been captivated, more deeply than by any of the countless women I have known, by a woman of the pleasure quarters of Gion in Kyoto. Quiet and without affectation, she seems, in every particular, a woman of Kyoto through and through — and above all I cannot forget the moist, dark-irised eyes that give her away. In the fourth summer since I came to know her, I spend the season in the mountains of the Kinai region, then descend into a rain-soaked Kyoto, only to find, with a pang of disappointment, that the upstairs room she had rented has been given up; still I take a room at the inn I know so well. The woman has a man in Osaka whom she loves deeply, and I once endured a searing agony at merely glimpsing, from a distance, another woman who lived with that man. Though circumstances leave her unfree to do as she likes, and though she tells me not to come, I cannot bring myself to stay away, and am drawn back to her side again and again, tormented by suspicion and by a love I cannot let go. Watching the fallen fortunes of mother and daughter living together, my pity and my attachment to the woman only deepen — until, one day, I discover, hidden behind her household Buddhist altar, two photographs of a man. "Who is this?" I ask, holding them out — and, flushing with irritation, the woman snatches the photographs from my hand.

Characters

  • I　A man who becomes obsessed with a woman of the Gion pleasure quarters in Kyoto. He travels from Tokyo to Kyoto again and again, tormented by suspicion and lingering attachment
  • The woman　A woman of Kyoto living in the Gion quarters. Quiet and fair-skinned, she is also loved by a man deeply devoted to her in Osaka
  • The mother　The woman's mother. Amid a life of fallen fortunes, she comes to live together with her daughter in Gion

The opening (original text)

...That woman was the one I liked best of all the countless women I had ever seen. Asked just what it was about her that I liked so much, it would be hard to put into words point by point. But what I liked best of all, more than anything, was the way she spoke, the way she carried herself — quiet, without a trace of affectation. And she was, by birth, in every particular, a woman of Kyoto. Though it must be said that women of Kyoto often have a certain slackness about the face, this woman's brow and eyes were drawn tight, and her mouth, too, did not have that loose shape so often seen in women of that region; her skin was fair, and in summer it grew fairer still, the color of her skin, damp with sweat, taking on of its own accord a faint rose tinge — unlike those women over there who make a show of thick powder and paint, she never made much of a boast of going always without powder, though she did go without it.

The ending (original text)

"Say — who's this?" I said in a low voice from behind her, as she was folding her kimono. At once the woman turned to look back at me and came over, and, flushing with irritation, said, "You mustn't look at things like that," and snatched the photographs from my hand.
