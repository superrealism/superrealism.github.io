About this work

Dried bonito badly shaved gives a dead stock — a practical piece in which the gourmet Kitaōji Rosanjin explains how to choose and shave dried bonito, and how to draw stock from kelp. His first instruction is to get hold of a plane that cuts well.

Summary

Good dried bonito, struck together, rings out crisply like clappers — katsun, katsun. One that gives off a damp, muffled patter, like worm-eaten wood, is bad dried bonito. Rosanjin begins by teaching how to tell the two apart. Kamebushi (round-cut) is better than honbushi (standard-cut). And what matters most of all is how it's shaved. Unless the shavings come off as thin as gampi paper, with a glassy sheen, no good stock will result. For that, a sharp plane is essential. Ninety-nine households out of a hundred, Rosanjin writes without mercy, probably don't own a good one — not even people who lecture on cooking own one — and he recommends simply buying a carpenter's plane. When making stock, the moment the shavings are dropped into vigorously boiling water is already enough; the stock is done. Simmering it on and on only ruins the flavor, and one should never make a "second stock" from the same shavings. The latter half turns to kelp stock. For fish dishes, kelp stock is the only way to go — doubling up flavors only makes things heavy, he explains, and describes the method concretely: dampen the kelp with water, gently wipe its surface, and simply pass it briefly through boiling water. Simmering it for a long time is the height of folly — right down to the story of the "pull-out kelp" technique passed down in Kyoto — and in this way the fundamental techniques underlying Japanese cuisine are laid out with concision.

The opening (original text)

How should one select dried bonito, and how should one shave it? First, let me offer a simple method for telling good dried bonito from bad. Good dried bonito, when two pieces are struck together, rings out crisply — katsun, katsun — almost like clappers, or like the sound of a certain kind of stone. One that gives off a damp, muffled patter, like worm-eaten wood, with a moist odor, is bad dried bonito.

Dashi no torikata by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

To pass the kelp briefly through hot water and lift it straight out feels, somehow, like a waste — but simmering it on and on is the height of folly: it only draws out the cloying sweetness at the base of the kelp, and never yields a properly refined stock. In the Kyoto region there is also a method called "pull-out kelp," in which a long strip of kelp is fed in from one side of the pot, passed along the bottom, and drawn out the other side — and with a method this exacting, even the most demanding connoisseurs of food have nothing left to complain about.

Dashi no torikata by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)
