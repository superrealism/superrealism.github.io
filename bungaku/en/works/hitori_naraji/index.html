About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Fuji in 1944. Tochigi Daisuke, a captain of twenty in the service of the Takeda house, is a patient man who will never say that anything hurts or that he is beaten, whatever happens to him. It draws his single-minded endurance and his feeling for his betrothed, Hatsuho. A piece conveying a quiet encouragement through a young man who does not complain in adversity and keeps facing forward.

Summary

Tochigi Daisuke never once says the word "it hurts." Whatever the situation, he deliberately avoids every word of a passive, complaining sort — "I'm done for," "I give up," "I'm in trouble" — as though casting them aside on purpose. A retainer of Takeda Harunobu of Kai, serving under Baba Nobukatsu as a captain of twenty foot soldiers — an ordinary enough rank, and a man of quite ordinary appearance besides — it is this single trait alone that draws people's interest. Ever since his fifteenth year, when his father proudly announced that he had "been patient since earliest childhood, and never once said it hurts," his comrades have watched for any opening to make him cry out, but however they try, he never lets slip a word of weakness. Even when the boastful Takaori Matashichirō straddles him and chokes his throat with all his strength, Daisuke endures it to the end. Beneath this unbending patience lay a single-minded devotion to his betrothed, Hatsuho. At the story's close, with his engagement placed in jeopardy for want of any merit yet won on the battlefield, Daisuke makes Hatsuho a vow: "Whether it takes two years or three, I will surely see to it that I go out to the battlefield — and then our engagement can be restored." Hatsuho's tear-streaked face seemed, in that moment, to shine. "I will come back to you..." — her wide eyes glowed, vivid with new hope. A piece by Yamamoto depicting a young man who never gives voice to complaint, however hard his circumstances.

The opening (original text)

Tochigi Daisuke never says the word "it hurts." Nor, whatever difficulty may arise, does he ever say "I'm done for," or "I give up," or "I'm in trouble." In every other case too, he seems to deliberately avoid any word of a passive, complaining sort, casting it aside before it can be spoken.

Hitori naraji by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Whether it takes two years or three, I will surely see to it that I go out to the battlefield... Hatsuho, and then our engagement can be restored — don't you think so?" Hatsuho's tear-streaked face seemed, in that moment, to shine. Her eyes, opened wide, brimmed with a vivid light of new hope, and a bright flush of color rose to both her cheeks; and then, in a firm, strong voice, she said, "I will come back to you..."

Hitori naraji by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
