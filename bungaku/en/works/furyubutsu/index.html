About this work

The work that made Kōda Rohan's name, published in 1889 in the series Shincho Hyakushu. Shuun, a young master carver wholly given to Buddhist sculpture, meets Otatsu, an ill-fated beauty, at an inn on the Kiso road, and falls in love. But the two are pitilessly parted, and in his despair Shuun carves Otatsu's figure into an image — which rises, in a fantastical close, into life. A romance about art, love and faith, and the book that lifted Rohan's reputation at a stroke.

Summary

Shuun, a young master carver wholly given to the making of Buddhist images, stops at the inn at Suhara on the Kiso road while traveling from one famous temple to another to study their images. There he meets O-Tatsu, a beautiful girl who goes about selling pickled flowers, and the two come to understand each other deeply and fall in love. O-Tatsu was born to an unhappy lot: her father, a man of high rank, long ago abandoned her mother. The two promise themselves to each other; but her real father sends messengers to fetch her, meaning to take her back as a daughter of rank, and O-Tatsu is carried off from Shuun against her will. Losing his love, Shuun sinks into despair; but in time the soul of the sculptor rouses in him, and he begins to carve into an image, with all his heart, the unforgettable face of O-Tatsu. Forgetting to eat or sleep, he finishes a "Buddha of elegance" so beautiful that it might be taken for O-Tatsu herself. And as he goes on pouring his whole single-minded devotion into the finished figure, the image at last begins to move as though it had received life. The one-pointedness of his faith and his love calls down a response, and wrapped in the scent of white roses, with haloes about them, he and O-Tatsu rise together above the clouds — a brilliant ending in which a hopeless love in this world turns into a fantastic salvation.

Characters

  • Shuun　A young master carver wholly given to Buddhist sculpture, who meets O-Tatsu on his journey of training and falls in love with her
  • O-Tatsu　A beautiful and ill-fated girl who sells pickled flowers at the inn at Suhara on the Kiso road, taken back by her highborn father and torn away from Shuun
  • O-Tatsu's father　A man of high rank who long ago abandoned mother and child, and who sends messengers to fetch his daughter

The opening (original text)

He carried in his breast the triad, the Four Heavenly Kings, the twelve attendants, the sixteen arhats, and the five hundred arhats besides, and could raise them all with hatchet and knife; and those who did not know Unkei praised his skill, while he, knowing the sculptor Tori, was ashamed in his heart. The deeper his devotion to the art, the more he hated the shortcomings of his own work: born in Japan, this country of art, must he let it be said that in these days there is no craftsman of Hida? So long as life was in him, Shuun would spend such strength as he had, however far it fell short, if only to satisfy his own liking, and to work off some part of his resentment at being looked down upon by high-nosed foreigners with their plaster casts. Poor fellow, he swore his vow of single devotion before the Shaka of Saga; and how old was he? One-and-twenty that spring. Thereafter, while the wind beat the mist off Arashiyama and haiku poets with breaking hearts prayed the falling blossoms to turn into butterflies, he never so much as looked at the beauty of it: what flower of an India he had never seen was he carving now, until he grieved at the evening bell that ended the long day? He never joined the pleasant coolness of the riverbed at evening, where poets make their jokes about the fragrance of melons cooled in the clear water that holds a serenely floating moon, with the stones still wet after a shower has washed the dust from Third and Fourth Streets. He watched idly as the moonflowers on the fence lingered into the dusk, and burned his sandalwood chips to drive off the mosquitoes — grateful for that much profit from his trade, which is comical enough. He never entered the elegant company that reddens its faces against the autumn leaves and warms itself with wine, nor joined the set who think it stylish to view the snow through glass over boiled tofu on a bed of kelp; still less did he cast so much as a sidelong glance at the beauties of Shimabara and Gion. He was besotted, wholly and without a thought elsewhere, with a Benten of his own carving; he never listened to the sweet little songs of koto and samisen, and yet in his dreams his ears caught the voices of the Kinnara — such was his ardor that the very soul of Vishvakarman must surely have entered into him. Going straight on through the floating world in this way for some three years, no devil could keep pace with such application. He had gifts from childhood besides: even when he rolled snow into a Daruma or cut a radish into the shape of a bullfinch he often astonished people; and now, with the discipline of study on top of it and the courage of his indignation added, the keen hand grew keener and the sharp blade sharper. Having set his heart on the way at seven, he came into his mastery at twenty-four, and his teacher confessed that he could take him no further. Whereupon Shuun made up his mind on the instant, sold off, with a single man's freedom, the goods his father had left him, and shouldering a few tools set out to seek the traces of the old craftsmen at Nara, Kamakura and Nikkō — laughed at because the ties of his straw sandals, not being used to it, kept coming undone; and it was from that journey that he first learned the pathos of things.

The ending (original text)

Afterward there were those who saw her here and there, riding on white clouds with a beautiful halo about her. A certain gentleman worshipped her in a long velvet gown with ostrich plumes bright in her crown; a certain nobleman saw her wearing a white collar and a brocade sash blazing with gold. Farmers, on the other hand, worshipped a figure in a tattered padded coat and straw sandals with a sickle at the waist; small tradesmen saw her in a summer robe of Awa crepe, a cotton sash and a hairpin of nickel silver. In the cold winds of Hokkaidō she appeared to the eyes of fishermen in a padded jacket on which herring scales glinted strangely; on Sado, where the waves are loud, in a rag-woven garment of no definite color. And Marquis Narihira, some time later, is said to have encountered her in shoes with small heels and a dress dazzling with gay ribbons. All of these are one and the same Buddha of elegance, unrecorded in any of the sutras, the very image Shuun carved, appearing in a thousand different forms without the slightest difference between them. Everyone who has worshipped her has taken her for the guardian Buddha of his life; where faith is deep, descendants prosper and the household is at peace, and her blessings are beyond doubt. Even if one should now and then feel some resentment against her, to a man who, like Shuun, turns that resentment into ice upon a fire, she shows the Buddha-nature that is hers by right, and her vow of protection is not made in vain. But if by any error one should go near the wooden dolls and clay idols of the sect of Mahomet or the Mormons or the like, her punishment in this world and the next is swift and sure, and she turns the halo into a wheel of fire and burns up household and soul together. Be reverent; be very reverent.
