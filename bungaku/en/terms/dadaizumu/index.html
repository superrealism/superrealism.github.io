What is Dadaizumu (Dadaism)?

Category
Literary Movement

An avant-garde art movement begun in 1916, in the midst of the First World War, by Tristan Tzara, Hugo Ball, and others at the Cabaret Voltaire in Zurich, Switzerland. Under the banner of the meaningless word "Dada," it rejected wholesale the modern reason that had produced the war and all established art and values, seeking to set expression free through chance, nonsense, and destructive experiments with language. The movement spread to Berlin and Paris and prepared the way for Surrealism. In Japan it was introduced in 1920 through articles such as one in the newspaper "Yorozu Chōhō," and Takahashi Shinkichi, shaken by what he read, began writing poetry as a self-proclaimed Dadaist. Inspired by Shinkichi, Tsuji Jun also called himself a Dadaist, and "Dadaisuto Shinkichi no Shi" ("Poems of Shinkichi the Dadaist," 1923), published under Tsuji's editorship, became the monument of Japanese Dada. Hagiwara Kyōjirō, who joined the poetry magazine "Aka to Kuro" ("Red and Black"), founded the same year, published the collection "Shikei Senkoku" ("Death Sentence," 1925), which used even the arrangement of type destructively. Nakahara Chūya, then a student at Ritsumeikan Middle School in Kyoto, came upon "Poems of Shinkichi the Dadaist" in a secondhand bookshop in the autumn of 1923 and began writing Dada-style poems, and from the following year he came to know French Symbolist poetry through Tominaga Tarō. Traces of this baptism in Dada can be seen in his first collection, "Yagi no Uta" ("Poems of the Goat," 1934), as well. Japanese Dada faded within a few years, but its spirit of revolt was carried on in anarchist poetry and the avant-garde poetry of the early Shōwa era.
