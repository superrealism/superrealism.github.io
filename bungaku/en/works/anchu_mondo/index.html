About this work

A posthumous piece from Akutagawa's very last days, moving entirely as a dialogue between a certain voice and the narrator. The voice exposes without mercy his hypocrisy, vanity and cowardice; he answers back at times and at times falls silent. The ending, in which, driven into a corner and left alone, he calls his own name and tells himself to put his roots down firmly, since he is a reed blown by the wind, has been read ever since as a record of self-interrogation written just before his suicide.

Summary

"You were a man altogether different from what I had supposed" — so a certain voice in the darkness begins. That is not my responsibility, I answer. But you yourself have helped in that misunderstanding, the voice presses. From there the dialogue goes on without mercy. The voice takes up one by one what I have written, my life, my attitude to my family, my hankering after fame, and lays bare the vanity and the cowardice and the hypocrisy at the bottom of them. I argue, I excuse myself, I turn defiant, and sometimes I am silent. ... What is at issue in the exchange is whether I choose art or life, whether I acknowledge the limit of my talent, and whether I can rest content with being "one of the minor writers." At last I say that I am one of the minor writers, and that I mean to be one of the minor writers, and that peace is not to be had anywhere else — though when I have a pen in my hand I may perhaps become your captive. The voice says, "Then be careful always. For one thing, I may carry out every word of yours in act," and telling me that it will come and see me again some day, it goes. Left alone, I call out my own name and tell myself to plant my feet firmly, for my own sake and at the same time for the sake of my children.

Characters

  • I　The author himself, put to the question
  • A certain voice　The voice that examines me out of the darkness

The ending (original text)

I: (Left alone.) Akutagawa Ryūnosuke! Akutagawa Ryūnosuke, put your roots down firmly. You are a reed blown by the wind. There is no telling when the weather may change. Only plant your feet and hold on. It is for your own sake. And at the same time for the sake of your children. Do not be conceited. And do not be servile either. From now on you are to begin again. (The second year of Shōwa. Posthumous manuscript.)
