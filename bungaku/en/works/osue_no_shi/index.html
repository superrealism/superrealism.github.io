About this work

Osue, the youngest daughter of a family who keep a barbershop on the outskirts of Sapporo, sees four of her own people buried within half a year. Carrying the burden of a fear that her younger brother and her sister's baby died of the cucumbers she gave them, and yet unable to hold down the life that keeps welling up in her, the girl of fourteen is treated coldly by her mother and her sister. Told to die, and rebelling against it in her own heart, Osue at last takes poison. It is said to be modeled on a girl Arishima taught at the Enyū night school in Sapporo, and it is known too for having been translated into Chinese by Lu Xun.

Summary

Osue, the youngest daughter of the Tsurudoko barbershop on the outskirts of Sapporo, is fourteen. That year she lost in quick succession her long-ailing father and her second brother, and had taken to saying "bad times" to everyone as a kind of catchword. On the Emperor's birthday, out at the river in a break from the great cleaning, Osue eats the unripe cucumbers her younger brother Rikizō has brought, and shares them with her sister's baby. Dysentery was going about the neighborhood just then, and before long the baby and Rikizō died one after the other. Her mother, having lost a grandchild and a son, falls into hysterics, glares at Osue, and says, "I shall not forget this." Burdened as she is, Osue cannot hold down the life that wells up in her, and her mother and sister treat her coldly. On the forty-ninth day after Rikizō's death she is taken by a friend to watch a trial of caterpillar tracks, and when she comes home at dusk her mother pushes her away, calling her a good-for-nothing. Rebelling inwardly — told to die, who would obey? — Osue flees to her sister's house, and there too is reproached at length for the straits the family is in and for her own want of feeling; and after she has cried herself out she quietly makes up her mind to die. The next morning she takes the corrosive sublimate that was on the shelf at home and swallows it at her sister's house where no one can see. Carried to the doctor and asked, she answers that it was from the smaller bottle; and after hours of sickness and pain she dies at half past three that afternoon, her last words being, "There is no need to call Tetsu if he is at school." The next day, in newly fallen snow, the Tsurudoko sent out its fifth funeral.

Characters

  • Osue　The youngest daughter of the Tsurudoko, fourteen years old
  • Tsurukichi　Osue's eldest brother, who runs the barbershop after their father's death
  • Mother　Having lost her husband and her children one after another, she is harsh with Osue
  • Sister　Osue's elder sister, married in the neighborhood, who loses her baby
  • Rikizō　Osue's younger brother, who dies of dysentery
  • Tetsu　The youngest brother, who is lame

The opening (original text)

About that time Osue had picked up from somewhere, without knowing from whom, the words "bad times," and said them over and over. "Times are so bad, you see, that my brother is in difficulties too. And on top of it we have had four funerals between April and September." That was the way Osue talked to her friends. It was a precocious way of talking for a girl of fourteen; but when one looked at the flat, mask-like face with its slightly receding middle, those who heard her could not help being made to smile.

The ending (original text)

"There, there. That's right. Well done, well done. Well done indeed. Mother is here; don't cry. There, there." So her mother said, stroking about her all the while. And just so, at about half past three in the afternoon, Osue parted from her short life of fourteen years. The next afternoon the Tsurudoko sent out its fifth funeral. In the pure white newly fallen snow, a small coffin and a group of mourners to match it made a dirty stain. Tsurukichi and the sister stood at the door of the shop and watched the little procession go. Behind the coffin, lame Tetsu with the mortuary tablet in his hands could be seen clearly, wearing the worn clogs that had been Rikizō's and Osue's, bobbing up and down as he walked. The sister worked her beads and was lost in thought. On the joined palms of the sister and Tsurukichi, who had suffered the grief of the young dying before the old, the snow went on falling and falling.
