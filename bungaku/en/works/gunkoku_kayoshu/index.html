About this work

At the lodging in Shimokitazawa where the narrator, a university student, had installed himself, a young woman's singing passed along the narrow road under the window at about ten every night — a novella by Yamakawa Masao. The songs are always the martial ones of the war years. His housemate, a film extra called Dai-chan, finds salvation in that voice, later says he heard the same songs at the studio, and begins to explain that this is what love is. The bitter, unforgettable memory of a year in which a young man tried to keep another person's love alive on a lie.

Summary

"I," twenty-one, had moved into the lodging of Isojima Daihachirō — "Dai-chan" — a film extra whom I met at a drinking place on the edge of town. Days of drinking, of lecturing at random on the history of art, of eating without paying a penny. Along the narrow road below that window a young woman's singing passed every night about ten. The songs were always of the military kind: "The Katō Falcon Fighter Squadron," "Cherry Blossoms of the Same Class"; and "I" wrote out the words as I half remembered them and made him a notebook called Songs of the Military Nation. Dai-chan carried it always about him and said with tears, "That voice pierces me at the deepest place." ... One night, out of a spiteful plan, "I" open the window and light up the singer with the lamp. It was a girl with a face still like a child's. From the next day Dai-chan began to foretell by "inspiration" what would be sung tomorrow, and "I" went on lying and saying that every one of them had been right. To keep his "love" alive on lies, and at the moment when he was in the highest spirits to give it away and drop him into the reality of a "stone" — that was the one revenge of an "I" who knew he had no "love" of his own to give. In July the singing suddenly stops. Shibata Haruko, the singer, who speaks to him on the way back from the swimming pool, tells him, "You are a very hateful person. I hate you" — and later sends a long letter of farewell. And one evening in August Dai-chan comes home beside himself. "I am going to be married." "Today, today I met her. The real one."

Characters

The opening (original text)

I do not much believe in such things as a man's progressing, or his character changing altogether. A man certainly changes, but that does not mean progress, nor does it mean becoming someone else. The circuits of feeling within him, the forms in which he is satisfied, have somehow become fixed, and they cannot be exchanged for others. When I was a university student there was a season in which I moved into a friend's lodging. Sometimes I feel my experience in that lodging rise up vividly from beneath the skin like a brand. And I think that probably I shall never, as long as I live, become anyone other than the self I was then.... It is a fairly hopeless recognition; but from that hopelessness alone can I begin anything at all.

Gunkoku kayōshū by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But now, as a teacher at a certain high school, I am trying once more, with all my strength, to act in order to believe in "love." This spring I mean to marry for the first time. Of Shibata Haruko I have had no news since. What became of Dai-chan's marriage to Mochizuki Yasuko I do not know either. Nor do I see Dai-chan. Only, at the New Year, in a film I happened to see — it was the work of the same great director who once made the film in which Dai-chan played a bandit — I found Dai-chan among the samurai cut down one after another in an instant by the hero. At least in his living he must be going round the same circuit still. My mother, by the way, is in good health.

Gunkoku kayōshū by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)
