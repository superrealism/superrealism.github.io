About this work

A full-length work written to commission in 1944 for Koyama Shoten's series of regional gazetteers. Dazai spent about three weeks travelling around Tsugaru, where he was born, and lays his own upbringing over what he saw in the borrowed form of a gazetteer. The journey through Kanita, Tappi, Kanagi and Fukaura reaches its height at Kodomari, where he meets Take, the woman who raised him, for the first time in thirty years, and arrives at the discovery that "I am a friend to these people." Generally regarded as the furthest point of his autobiographical writing.

Summary

One spring the narrator spends some three weeks making a circuit of the Tsugaru peninsula at the northern end of Honshū. The stated purpose is to write a gazetteer of the region; the real one is to find out what sort of man he is. At Aomori he is warmly entertained by his old friend T, at Kanita by N and others; he goes north along the Sotogahama shore and stands at Cape Tappi, the cul-de-sac of Honshū. At Kanagi, where his family house stands, he feels the delicate distance between himself and his brothers and follows the memory of his dead mother and grandmother. He calls on his aunt at Goshogawara, travels the west coast through Fukaura and Ajigasawa, and at the last turns toward Kodomari. There lives Take, the nursemaid who brought him up as a small child. Meeting her again after thirty years in the crowd at a school sports day, Take says little; she simply seats him beside her and watches the children run. Touched by the deep affection under that show of composure, he understands at last what his own upbringing really was: he is by no means a man of genteel breeding — he takes after this sad woman who raised him. "I am a friend of these people" — and with that confession of a friend gained, this new gazetteer of Tsugaru, written under a holy war, comes to its close.

The opening (original text)

One spring I made, for the first time in my life, a circuit of the Tsugaru peninsula at the northern end of Honshū, which took me some three weeks; and it was one of the more important events in my thirty-odd years. I was born in Tsugaru and was brought up there for twenty years, yet I had seen only Kanagi, Goshogawara, Aomori, Hirosaki, Asamushi and Ōwani — of the other towns and villages I knew nothing whatever.

Return to Tsugaru by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Now, without meaning to give myself the airs of the old sage who laid down his brush at the capture of the unicorn, I think this new gazetteer of Tsugaru, written under a holy war, may well stop here with its author's confession of a friend gained. There were any number of other things I still wanted to write; but it seems to me that the living atmosphere of Tsugaru has, in the main, been told. I have not dressed it up. I have not deceived my readers. Farewell then, reader; if we live, another day. Let us keep our spirits up. Do not despair. And so, goodbye.

Famous passages

I have practiced no vanity. I have not deceived my reader. Farewell, then, reader — if I am still alive, we shall meet again some other day. Let us go on in good spirits. Do not despair. And with that, so long.

The closing of this travel account through his native region. These few lines, addressing the reader directly in farewell, leave behind the brightest aftertaste of any of Dazai's works.

“Tell me, why are you going on a journey?” “Because I'm suffering.”

The opening of the main narrative, “Pilgrimage.” In this light exchange — answering “Because I'm suffering” when asked why he travels — the author's unease and self-conscious humor show through as he sets out on a journey around his native region.
