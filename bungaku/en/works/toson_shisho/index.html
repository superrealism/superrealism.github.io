About this work

A collection Shimazaki Tōson selected himself from his four books of poems — Wakanashū, Hitohabune, Natsukusa and Rakubaishū. From the first love that begins with hair only just put up, the works that announced the start of modern Japanese poetry are gathered into one volume. The line in his own preface — that at last the time of a new poetry has come, and that it was like a beautiful dawn — is famous as a declaration of the birth of modern verse.

Summary

"At last, the time of a new poetry has come. It was like a beautiful dawn" — the author's own preface opens with these famous lines, looking back on the movement for a new-style poetry around 1897. Some cried out like prophets of old, others called aloud like poets of the West, and all seemed drunk on bright light, new voices, and fancy. Youthful imagination woke from a long sleep and adorned itself in the words of the folk, legends came to life again, and nature took on new colors once more — so Tōson writes, recalling that most of the new singers were simply earnest young men, whose art was crude and imperfect, but held neither falsehood nor affectation. The book collects self-chosen works from his four volumes of poems. "First Love," with its lines "When I saw you beneath the apple tree, / your hair only just put up in front"; "A Traveller's Feeling on the Chikuma River," with "Beside the old castle of Komoro, / white clouds, and the traveller grieves"; "The Coconut," "Song of the Autumn Wind," "Six Maidens" — love, travel, and the melancholy of youth are sung to the flowing rhythm of seven-five meter. Near the end of the book, "Wash It and Sew It Again" piles up its refrain — "Wash it and sew it again, wash it and sew it again, / that sleeve stained with grease, / that sleeve wet with tears, / rinse it clean, and then let there be no more grieving" — calling on the reader to cast off the grime of an old dream and return to a new world, and with this the self-selected collection closes.

The ending (original text)

Wash it and sew it again, wash it and sew it again — Even the cicada, with its thin, wing-like sleeves, Sings as it comes out of its shell into the world; Rinse it clean, and then let there be no more grieving. Wash it and sew it again, wash it and sew it again — Has your grief, then, grown so old and stale? Hasten, return to a new world, Rinse it clean, and then let there be no more grieving.

Tōson shishō by Shimazaki Tōson (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

By the old castle at Komoro, the clouds are white, and the traveler grieves.

The opening of “Song of Travel on the Chikuma River.” Setting the solitude of a traveler standing on the ruins of an old castle to a seven-five meter, it became a representative poem of Tōson as a poet.

From a distant island whose name I do not know a single coconut has drifted here. Leaving the shore of your homeland, how many months have you spent on the waves? Does the tree you came from still grow thick? Do its branches still cast their shade? I too, with the beach for my pillow, am a lone traveler sleeping adrift. Taking the nut and holding it to my breast, I feel anew the sorrow of wandering. When I watch the sun sink into the sea, the tears of a strange land come pouring down. I think of the eightfold tides between — on what day shall I return to my own country?

The whole of the poem “Coconut” (first collected in Rakubaishū). Written from Yanagita Kunio's account of seeing a coconut washed ashore at Cape Irago, it was later set to music and has long been loved as a song.

At last, the time of new poetry has come. It was like a beautiful dawn.

The opening of the author's preface to the one-volume edition gathering his four collections. Proclaiming the arrival of the age of new-style poetry through the image of the dawn, it is known as the words that symbolize the rise of modern Japanese poetry.

Life is power. Power is voice. Voice is words. New words are, in themselves, a new life.

A passage from the same preface. Linking life to power, to voice, to words, it holds that finding new words is itself a new way of living — the creed of Tōson's poetry.
