About this work

"My father died in the spring when I was eight. And he died by his own hand" — the short story published in the fourth Shinshichō that made Kume Masao's name, based on his own experience. It draws the three days leading to the suicide of his father, an elementary school principal at Ueda in Shinshū, who took responsibility for the loss of the emperor's portrait in a fire, entirely through the eyes of a child of eight. Not understanding what is happening, the boy is even intoxicated by the strange happiness his father's death brings. A cold-eyed piece catching, from the standpoint of a child's ignorance, the moment when a modern institution kills a man.

Summary

"I" — Tatsuo, the second son of the principal of an elementary school at Ueda in Shinshū — am eight years old, and lie ill with my father, both of us poisoned by eel. Left alone in the dark room, the boy is afraid that he and his father will die together; and trying to pray to God he loses the thread of the words, and wonders what would happen if only his father died. "Besides such things, there must surely be something great and sad if my father dies. What can it be?" — but a child's reasoning goes no further. That midnight the alarm bell rings wildly. The girls' school where his father teaches is burning. The white six-sided tower that looks down over the town falls in flames in a moment, and the Imperial portrait and the important papers kept on the upper floor are all burned. Next morning, from the gossip of the crowd gathered at the fire, the boy learns in fragments that his father tried to run into the burning building and was held back by them all, and that if the Imperial portrait is burned it seems to be the principal's responsibility. His father, appearing out of the smoke, goes hurriedly away without noticing when his son calls to him. At home his father shuts himself in his study, forbids anyone to come in, and goes on examining something. An unease like held breath fills the house, and everyone goes in and out of the rooms as though walking on tiptoe. Unable to bear it, his mother sends the boy in with a pretense of innocence to see how his father is. Opening the sliding door, his father sat upright, gazing steadily far ahead. The boy is almost disappointed that no sign of agitation appears — how should he find the anguish in those eyes that gaze into eternity? The next day his father takes his own life with a Yoshimitsu dagger he had kept hidden at the back of his bookshelves. An old retainer of the domain, his father's friend at go, comes running and points at the shallow cut in the belly and cries almost in tears with delight, "He was a samurai after all. He knew the proper form!" — and even his mother fixes a brave thought in her tears. But the boy could not understand why that was fine. On the day of the funeral a band went before the procession. Carrying the censer behind the coffin, the boy feels a vague pleasure in being looked at and remarked upon along the way, and walks with as much dignity as he can manage. "What a strange happiness my father's death has brought!" Going out to relieve himself in the middle of the sutra reading and coming back to look over the black crowd in the temple yard, tears rise in him for the first time. And there a stranger in black Western clothes taps his shoulder and takes his hand and shakes it again and again, saying: "Grow up to be a great man like your father."

Characters

  • I (Tatsuo)　Eight years old, the second son of an elementary school principal
  • Father　The principal of the school at Ueda, who takes his own life over the fire
  • Mother　Who weeps with her whole body
  • Elder brother　Four years older, who carries the tablet in the procession
  • Elder sister　Ill with her lungs and bedridden. She is the first to find the suicide

The opening (original text)

1 My father died in the spring of my eighth year. And he died by his own hand. 2 The spring of that year was a warm early spring, unlike Shinshū. On the bed of the Chikuma river that washes the skirts of the town of Ueda where we lived, the riverside wormwood was pushing up between the pebbles, and Mount Tarō, which draws its calm line across the sky of the town, was already hazing over into purple.

Chichi no shi by Kume Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

At that moment a man in black Western clothes tapped me on the shoulder. Before I could turn round he took my hand firmly and shook it again and again and said: "Grow up to be a great man like your father. Grow up to be a great man like your father." I looked steadily into that man's face. There were bright tears in his eyes. And so I gripped his hand back and nodded. Bathed in the yellowing light of the westering sun, that stranger and I stood silent a while with our hands clasped. I wish I could have gone on living forever under the feeling of that moment.
