About this work

A short story Masamune Hakuchō published in Waseda Bungaku in 1916. Set in a poor fishing village on the Inland Sea suggesting the author's own home country, it recalls, through a boy's eyes, the fear and the old customs that epidemic disease brings to the villagers. Not long after local self-government was introduced, the narrator's father is elected headman without ever settling into it. When dysentery gets into the village in the middle of the festival, patients are sent off in a broken boat, as good as exiled, to the isolation hospital on an island offshore. The villagers curse the headman, the constable and the doctor, and at night the constable walks about watching who goes to the privy. Hakuchō sets down evenly, at a distance, the bleak scene of a village afraid of illness and death.

Summary

Not long after the system of local self-government had been established in towns and villages, "my" father, ill at ease with the honor, finds himself chosen as village headman. It is simply the way of things in a remote village so poor in men of ability that no other suitable candidate can be found, and he settles for the meager stipend of the honorary post, spending his days from morning to night handling the troublesome affairs of a sprawling village made up of five hamlets. As Father grows busier, the household grows busier too, to Grandmother's and Mother's dismay, and when men of the Liberal Party come to hold a rally, Father's policy of banning the festival's portable shrine on grounds of a contagious disease in a neighboring village is attacked in the coarsest terms. Before long, the disease that had been spreading through the neighboring village, taking advantage of the gluttony and drunkenness of festival time, invades "my" own village as well. Dysentery patients are loaded onto shabby boats and sent off to the isolation hospital built on a lone island half a league out to sea. "I" lean against the upstairs railing and, with a fearful heart, watch the boat of the sick glide across the quiet early-autumn sea. The island, once so picturesque, now seems, ever since the hospital was built there, an uncanny place crawling with disease. Father, as village headman, goes out to each new patient's house together with the doctor and the policeman, lighting a lantern at midnight to escort the patient by boat, and crossing to the island himself as well. "Get sick, and it's exile to the island. Going to that island is as good as going to be killed," the villagers say, and the parents and siblings of the afflicted come to resent the headman, hate the policeman, and curse the doctor. So many hide their illness that the policeman patrols the village every night, watching for trips to the privy, until a rumor spreads that at the sound of his footsteps, people hold back even from going to relieve themselves. Father roused from sleep at midnight, a woman's scream at the door — Hakuchō sets down, through the recollections of a boy, in a cool and steady hand, the bleak, desolate scene of a village terrorized by the shadow of sickness and death.

The opening (original text)

It was not long after the system of local self-government had been established in towns and villages. My father was chosen and became village headman. By his own nature he had no liking for so troublesome a post, but in a remote village so poor in men of ability that no other suitable candidate could be found, he seems to have been chosen, and to have accepted, for want of any other choice.

Hibyōin by Masamune Hakuchō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In the end, the rumor that spread as the truest of all was that this was how the whole affair had come about. "There's no way Wasuke could make a living just tilling furrows out on that island. He might well have meant, from the very start, to turn the place into a gambling den," Grandmother said, giving full credit to the rumor.
