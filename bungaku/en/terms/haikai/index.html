What is Haikai?

Category
Form / Genre

Poetry that split off from renga, prizing wit and humor. In contrast to renga, bound by the conventions of waka, it established itself in the late Muromachi period as "haikai no renga" (comic linked verse), which used colloquial and Chinese-derived words freely, and it flowered in the Edo period as a popular literary form. In its early phase, Matsunaga Teitoku's Teimon school, centered on wordplay, spread it across the country, and then the Danrin school, led by Nishiyama Sōin, made it popular with freewheeling, uninhibited verses. It was Matsuo Bashō who led it to artistic heights, refining the technique of building on the resonance of the preceding verse and leaving behind the anthology "Sarumino" ("The Monkey's Straw Raincoat") and the travel journal "Oku no Hosomichi" ("The Narrow Road to the Deep North") under the principles of sabi and karumi. Haikai was originally a collaborative composition (renku) in which multiple poets alternated long verses (5-7-5) and short verses (7-7) in succession, and it was the opening verse of that sequence, the hokku, coming to be appreciated on its own, that later became the haiku. After the mid-Edo master Yosa Buson and Kobayashi Issa, who sang of the feelings of the common people, the late Edo period entered an age of formulaic, mass-produced "tsukinami" verse, until Masaoka Shiki, from 1892, put forward "haiku," as against "haikai," as a single poetic form in its own right, and modern haiku began.
