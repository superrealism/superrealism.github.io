About this work

The last of the three stories about Poe's great detective Auguste Dupin (1844), following "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" and "The Mystery of Marie Rogêt." In Sasaki Naojirō's translation. A letter that the Prefect of Police and all his men could not find though they went over the whole house, Dupin carries away on a single visit. The idea of matching one's own reasoning to the intellect of one's opponent, and the paradox about the hiding place, had a decisive influence on later detective fiction, and have been taken up again and again in psychoanalysis and philosophy.

Summary

On an autumn evening in Paris, while Dupin and "I" sit smoking, their old acquaintance Monsieur G—, the Prefect of Police, calls. A letter has been stolen from an exalted personage of the royal household by the Minister D—. If its contents were made public the lady's position would be in danger. It is certain that the minister keeps it by him as a political weapon, and the police have searched his house from top to bottom in his absence. They have probed the legs of the furniture with needles, examined the stuffing of the chairs, taken up the floorboards, and gone over even the paving stones of the garden with a microscope; and the letter has not come to light. Dupin hears him out and sends him away with nothing but "search again, and more thoroughly." A month later, when the Prefect comes again, Dupin produces the letter in exchange for a check. His explanation is this: the police measured the wit of a minister who is both poet and mathematician by their own rule alone. The minister did not hide the letter at all; he left it lying carelessly, turned inside out and crumpled, in a card rack exposed to every eye. Dupin called wearing green spectacles, and, taking his chance during a disturbance in the street, exchanged it for another. On the blank sheet he left behind was written a line hinting at an old grudge.

Characters

  • Auguste Dupin　A master of reasoning living in Paris
  • I　The narrator, who shares his rooms with Dupin
  • G—　The Prefect of Police of Paris, who searched the house from top to bottom and could not find the letter
  • The Minister D—　He has stolen the letter from an exalted lady and holds it as a political weapon

The opening (original text)

At Paris, just after dark one gusty evening in the autumn of 18—, I was enjoying the twofold luxury of meditation and a meerschaum, in company with my friend C. Auguste Dupin, in his little back library, or book-closet, au troisième, No. 33, Rue Dunôt, Faubourg Saint-Germain. For one hour at least we had maintained a profound silence; while each, to any casual observer, might have seemed intently and exclusively occupied with the curling eddies of smoke that oppressed the atmosphere of the chamber. For myself, however, I was mentally discussing certain topics which had formed matter for conversation between us at an earlier period of the evening; I mean the affair of the Rue Morgue, and the mystery attending the murder of Marie Rogêt.

The ending (original text)

"To which I told him, quite good-humoredly, that I should remember it. So, as I knew he would feel some curiosity in regard to the identity of the person who had outwitted him, I thought it a pity not to give him a clue. He is well acquainted with my handwriting, and I just copied into the middle of the blank sheet the words — '—— Un dessein si funeste, S'il n'est digne d'Atrée, est digne de Thyeste.' They are to be found in Crébillon's 'Atrée.'"
