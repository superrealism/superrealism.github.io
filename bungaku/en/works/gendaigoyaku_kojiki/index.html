About this work

The Kojiki, Japan's oldest history, put into plain modern Japanese by Takeda Yūkichi, a leading scholar of ancient literature. Compiled by imperial command, from the traditions Hieda no Are had learned by heart and Ō no Yasumaro set down, it was presented in three books in 712. From the beginning of heaven and earth and the generation of the gods, through Izanagi and Izanami making the land, Amaterasu in the rock cave, Susanoo and the eight-headed serpent, Ōkuninushi yielding the land and the descent of the heavenly grandchild, to the reigns from Jimmu to Suiko, it unfolds myth, legend and song on a grand scale — the source of both Japanese mythology and Japanese literature. An approachable way in before reading the original, written in a hybrid Chinese script.

Summary

The Kojiki consists of a preface and three books, upper, middle and lower. Ō no Yasumaro's preface records how Emperor Tenmu, wishing to correct the confusion in the imperial chronicles and old accounts held by the various houses, had Hieda no Are learn them by recitation; how Empress Genmei took up the work and ordered Yasumaro to set them down; and how it was completed in the fifth year of Wadō (712). The upper book is the age of the gods. It begins with the deities who appeared at the beginning of heaven and earth, and tells how Izanagi and Izanami gave birth to the land and to the gods, of Izanami's death and Izanagi's journey to the land of Yomi, and of the birth of Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi and Susanoo from his purification. Then follow Amaterasu's withdrawal into the rock cave of heaven at Susanoo's violence, which shut the world in darkness; the slaying of the eight-headed serpent by the banished Susanoo; the land-making of his descendant Ōkuninushi and his yielding of the land to the heavenly grandchild; the descent of Ninigi to Takachiho; and the story of the luck of the sea and the luck of the mountain. The middle book begins with the eastward campaign of the first emperor, Jinmu, and goes on to the expeditions and tragic death of Yamato Takeru and the story of Empress Jingū. The lower book carries the deeds of the emperors from Nintoku to Suiko, with tales of love and strife about the court, told along with many songs. Here is the source of all Japanese myth, legend and ancient history. Takeda Yūkichi renders this difficult classic, on the basis of exact scholarship, into readable modern Japanese.

Characters

  • Amaterasu　The sun goddess, who rules the Plain of High Heaven
  • Susanoo　Amaterasu's younger brother, who slays the eight-headed serpent
  • Ōkuninushi　The god of the land-making in Izumo, who yields the land to the heavenly grandchild
  • Emperor Jinmu　The first emperor

The opening (original text)

I, Yasumaro, humbly report. At the beginning of the universe the first of all things came into being, but its nature was not yet complete; it had no name and no motion, and there is no one who knows what shape it had. Thereafter heaven and earth were first divided, and the deities Ame-no-Minakanushi, Takamimusubi and Kamumusubi became the first gods, who brought all things forth; and then the two sexes, male and female, became distinct, and the deities Izanagi and Izanami became the parents who gave birth to all creation.

The ending (original text)

Her younger sister, Her Majesty Toyomikekashikiya-hime (the Empress Suiko), dwelt in the palace of Owarida in Yamato and ruled the realm for thirty-seven years. She passed away on the fifteenth day of the third month of the year of the elder brother of earth and the rat, a day of the younger brother of water and the ox. Her tomb was at first on the hill of Ōno, but was later moved to the great tomb at Shinaga.
