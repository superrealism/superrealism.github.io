About this work

Miyoshi Tatsuji's first collection, published by Daiichi Shobō in 1930. Fusing the influence of French symbolism with the Japanese classical tradition, it moves freely between free colloquial verse and fixed classical forms in a shapely diction. It holds "Snow," famous for the line about snow falling on Tarō's roof as Tarō sleeps; "On the Paving," which draws a solitary figure walking a temple path; and "The Pram," calling on a mother to push the pram — brief, well-bred lyrics that mark the starting point of Shōwa lyric poetry.

Summary

The book gathers poems in many forms — prose poems, free verse in the spoken language, fixed forms in the literary language. The opening piece, "Cape in Spring," entrusts the sadness of travel to the gulls at a journey's end; "The Perambulator" that follows lays a painful appeal to the mother over a pram going along an avenue of trees at dusk. "Snow," in two lines only, draws the sleep of children and the stillness of the world through the snow piling on the roofs of Tarō and of Jirō; and "On the Paving Stones" sings a solitary state of mind, walking one's own shadow through the spring of a temple where petals are drifting. Memories of boyhood, the mood of travel, the quiet of old capitals and Buddhist temples, and thoughts of the sea are all concentrated in transparent and restrained language; and at the end of the book stands the poet's half-comic self-questioning as he wanders the shore on a clouded day with Pan, his dog.

The opening (original text)

Cape in Spring At a cape in spring, the gulls at the end of my journey float on, and have gone far away. Snow It puts Tarō to sleep; on Tarō's roof the snow piles down. It puts Jirō to sleep; on Jirō's roof the snow piles down.

The Surveying Ship by Miyoshi Tatsuji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Come, let us go home, Pan. My Sancho Panza, walk more like an aristocrat on those short legs of yours. That's it — head up; and the tail up too. A careless crab has slipped and fallen from the lock of the canal. I liked the sound of that water — don't take offense, Pan; that was criticism.

The Surveying Ship by Miyoshi Tatsuji (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Let Tarō sleep, snow piles high on Tarō's roof. Let Jirō sleep, snow piles high on Jirō's roof.

The complete poem “Snow.” Through nothing more than two lines of matching form, it perfectly captures the stillness of a snow-laden night and the sleep of children — a masterpiece of modern Japanese poetry.
