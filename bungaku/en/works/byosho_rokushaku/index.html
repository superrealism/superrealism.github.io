About this work

The essays Masaoka Shiki serialized in the newspaper Nihon until just before his death: 127 short pieces written from his sickbed between 5 May and 17 September 1902, while he fought the agony of spinal caries. The subjects run wide — daily incident and observation, criticism of haiku and tanka, writing on art, comment on society — and a record of extreme physical pain lives alongside a record of a mind that goes on looking and thinking. Two days after the serialization ended, Shiki died at thirty-four. A high point of the modern Japanese essay and of sketch-from-life prose.

Summary

Struck down by tuberculosis of the spine and unable to move from a sickbed six feet long, Shiki went on writing his daily reflections from May 1902 as a serial in the newspaper Nihon. He sets down without concealment the fierce pain, the haze of the drugs and his fear of death; and at the same time there is no end to his subjects — talk with visitors, the flowers in the garden, his opinions of items in the newspaper, corrections and criticism of haiku and tanka, appraisals of the albums of paintings he loved. Much of it is critical writing that attacks the stale conventional manner of the haiku world of the day and sets out the ideal of sketching from life, so that what emerges is a Shiki who, in the midst of the suffering of his illness, loses neither his vigorous critical spirit nor his eye for observation. The series broke off with the entry of September 17, and two days later he died.

The opening (original text)

○ A sickbed six feet long — this is my world. And even these six feet of sickbed are too wide for me. I can just stretch out a hand and touch the matting, but I cannot stretch a leg beyond the bedding and let my body rest at ease. At the worst I am tormented by extremity of pain and cannot move my body half an inch. Pain, distress, crying out, narcotics — the futility of seeking one narrow path of life within the path of death, and snatching a little ease. And yet, so long as one is alive, there are things one wants to say. What I look at every day is nothing but the newspapers and magazines, and there are many times when I suffer so that I cannot even read them; but when I do read, there are things that make me angry, things that irritate me, and now and then, it must be said, things that somehow please me so that I forget the pain of my illness.

The ending (original text)

○ A letter from Hōhi Sanjin Respectfully: of late your "Sickbed Six Feet" has run to no more than two or three inches, which I find most unsettling, and so I write to inquire after you. This Daruma has been shut in since about the Bon season, doing austerities under the bamboo-pipe waterfall with a rope band about his head, and has been out of touch. Sick with poetry, he must be dreaming — a cuckoo. And who was it that put him to such torture? (September 17)

Famous passages

This sickbed, six feet long, is my whole world.

The opening of the essays he kept writing for the newspaper from his deathbed. Shiki's fierce resolve is fixed on a sickbed a mere six feet long, which he calls his whole world.

Until now I had misunderstood what Zen calls enlightenment. I had thought enlightenment meant being able to die calmly, whatever the circumstance — but I was wrong. Enlightenment means being able to go on living calmly, whatever the circumstance.

A passage from the essays kept written from his deathbed. Restating enlightenment not as dying calmly but as living calmly, it is celebrated as the point Shiki finally reached.

Ideals are the sort of thing that try to leap onto the roof in a single breath and often fall into the pond instead. Sketching from life is plain, but in return it makes no such blunders.

A passage on the meaning of sketching from life. Likening idealism to someone who tries to leap onto the roof in one breath and falls into the pond, it argues for the reliability of plain description. Shiki's view of literature is set out here in an easily grasped form.
