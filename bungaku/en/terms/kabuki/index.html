What is Kabuki?

Category
Other

A traditional Japanese theater form, beginning with the kabuki dances of Izumo no Okuni at the start of the Edo period, that achieved full artistic maturity through overwhelming popularity among the common people. It produced a wealth of varied repertoire — the stylized beauty of aragoto (rough-style) and wagoto (soft-style) acting, sewamono plays depicting the conflict between duty and human feeling, and grand jidaimono history plays — and playwrights such as Tsuruya Nanboku and, in the closing years of the shogunate, Kawatake Mokuami left many celebrated works. With the Meiji era came a theater-reform movement seeking a realism and literary quality suited to modern drama, rejecting the outlandish, and Tsubouchi Shōyō, adopting techniques from Western historical drama, wrote "Kiri Hitoha" ("A Paulownia Leaf"), opening the way for "new kabuki" — plays created by playwrights in their own right. Okamoto Kidō in particular left masterpieces of dignified new kabuki that probed characters' inner psychology, including "Shuzenji Monogatari" ("The Tale of Shuzenji," 1911) and "Banchō Sarayashiki" ("The Dish Mansion at Banchō"), depicting the artistic soul of a master craftsman. Izumi Kyōka's fantastical plays, too, found life on the kabuki and shinpa stage, and traditional theater, deeply intertwined with modern dramatic literature, lives on to this day.
