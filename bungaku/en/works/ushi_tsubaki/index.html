About this work

A children's story published in the magazine Shōkokumin Bunka in 1943, the year Niimi Nankichi died. It draws Kaizō, a rickshaw man, setting out to dig a well beside a camellia tree so that people passing on the road may wet their throats. A deed done for others past all calculation of profit — and after Kaizō falls in the Russo-Japanese War, his wish lives on as a well of clear water. Nankichi's clear-voiced song about the preciousness of a sincere heart.

Summary

Beside a mountain road stood a single young camellia tree. Risuke, the ox-driver, always tied his ox to it there, and Kaizō, the rickshaw puller, made it his habit too to set his rickshaw down at its foot to rest. One hot day, climbing the slope with his throat parched dry, Kaizō found himself resenting the fact that there was no fresh spring nearby. If only there were a well beside this camellia tree, he thought, how many travelers would be saved, their thirst quenched — and so Kaizō resolved to dig a well himself. It was work that drew laughter from others and cost money besides, but Kaizō worked steadily, little by little, saving up money, negotiating with the landowner, and moving his preparations for the well forward bit by bit. The villagers, cool toward his aim at first — for it went beyond all thought of profit or loss — were gradually moved by it all the same. But before the well could be finished, war broke out between Japan and Russia, and Kaizō crossed the sea as a soldier, falling as one of the flowers of the Russo-Japanese War. Yet the wish Kaizō left behind did not die with him. Even now, in the shade of the camellia tree, clear spring water still wells up without ceasing, and travelers weary from the road quench their thirst there, recover their strength, and go on their way once more. A piece in which Nankichi, with a clear and limpid pen, sings the nobility of a man's true heart, given freely and beyond all thought of gain.

Characters

  • Kaizō　A rickshaw puller. Resolves to dig a well beside the camellia tree to quench the thirst of travelers on the road, but is sent off to the Russo-Japanese War and dies before the well is finished
  • Risuke　An ox-driver. Ties his ox to the young camellia tree; a companion of Kaizō's

The opening (original text)

Beside a mountain road there was a young camellia tree. Risuke, the ox-driver, would tie his ox to it. Kaizō, the rickshaw puller, would set his rickshaw down at the camellia's foot as well. A rickshaw was not an ox, so there was no need to tie it up.

Ushi o tsunaida tsubaki no ki by Niimi Nankichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Japan and Russia had begun fighting each other, far across the sea. Kaizō was to cross that sea, and go into the midst of that fighting. In the end, Kaizō never came home again. He fell bravely, as one of the flowers of the Russo-Japanese War. But the work Kaizō left unfinished lives on, even now. In the shade of the camellia tree, clear spring water still wells up without ceasing, and travelers weary from the road quench their thirst there, recover their strength, and go on their way once more.
