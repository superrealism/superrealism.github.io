About this work

Inoue Kōbai's translation of Lu Xun's best-known work (1921–22), which stands at the starting point of modern Chinese literature. Included in the Kaizōsha "Complete Works of Lu Xun" (1932), it was the undertaking that first made it possible to read Lu Xun's short stories together in Japanese. It follows Ah Q, a day laborer whose surname and given name are alike uncertain, as he lives by remaking himself into the winner through his "method of spiritual victory" whenever he is beaten, and is swallowed by the wave of the 1911 Revolution and shot. It has been read on both as a scathing criticism of the Chinese national character and as a universal human comedy.

Summary

There was a man who slept in the Tutelary God's Temple at Weizhuang and kept himself alive by day labor at the reaping and the hulling. Neither his surname nor his given name nor where he came from was clear, and people called him Ah Q. If they laughed at the scars of ringworm on his head he lost his temper; but after being struck he would recover his humor by thinking, "It is as though I had been beaten by my own son" — this method of spiritual victory, of reading a defeat as a win, was his way of living. He is struck by His Honor Zhao, mocks a nun, makes advances to a maidservant and is driven out of the village, then goes into the town and does well for a while selling stolen goods. When rumors of the revolution reach Weizhuang and he sees the gentry of the village take fright, Ah Q comes to long for "rebellion" and goes about announcing that he too is of the revolutionary party. But those who actually coil up their queues and call themselves revolutionaries are the Budding Talent and the Imitation Foreign Devil. Never admitted among them, Ah Q is arrested, charged with a robbery at the Zhao house that he did not commit. Not understanding the questions put to him, made to draw a circle in place of a statement, and fretting that he had not drawn the circle well, he is paraded through the streets and shot. The village people say with one voice that his being killed proves he was bad, while the people of the town complain that as a spectacle it was disappointing.

Characters

  • Ah Q　A day laborer whose surname and given name are alike uncertain. By his method of spiritual victory he turns defeat into triumph
  • His Honor Zhao　One of the gentry of Weizhuang, who strikes Ah Q
  • Young D　A young man who competes with Ah Q for work
  • Amah Wu　A maidservant of the Zhao house, whose being courted by Ah Q sets the whole village in an uproar
  • The Imitation Foreign Devil　A man back from studying abroad, who calls himself a revolutionary

The opening (original text)

Chapter One: Preface It is more than a year or two now since I first meant to write the true story of Ah Q. But while I meant to write it I kept thinking better of it, which shows well enough that I am not a man who "leaves words behind him." From of old an immortal pen has been required to hand down an immortal man, so that the man is handed down by the writing and the writing by the man; and in the end it grows less and less clear which depends upon which. And yet it came round at last to handing down Ah Q, as though there were some ghost in my thoughts. But no sooner had I set pen to paper to write this quickly perishing piece than I felt a thousand difficulties. The first was the title. Confucius says, "If the name is not correct, the words will not run straight."

The ending (original text)

The next to feel the effects was the Zhao household. Because the Budding Talent had gone into the town to lay an information, he had had his queue cut off by bad elements among the revolutionaries, and had lost twenty strings of cash in reward money besides, so that the whole house wept and wailed. And from that day a flavor of the old loyalist began little by little to arise among them. As for public opinion, in Weizhuang there was no dissenting voice. Of course they all said Ah Q had been bad. That he had been shot down was the proof of it: if he had not been bad, how should he have come to be shot? But opinion in the town was not so favorable. Most of them were dissatisfied, holding that a shooting is not so fine a spectacle as a beheading; and then what a spiritless condemned man he had been, paraded so long through the streets and never once singing a line from an opera. They had followed him for nothing. (December 1921)
