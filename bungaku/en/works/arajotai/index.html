About this work

The novella that marked Tokuda Shūsei's first turn toward naturalism, serialized in the Kokumin Shimbun from October to December 1908. It draws the small tangles of feeling that creep into the daily life of a couple running a sake shop, in a matter-of-fact hand without exaggeration. As Kawabata Yasunari said, Shūsei's road to naturalism opened with this work: a landmark that forms the pattern of everything after.

Summary

Shinkichi, who keeps a liquor shop, has long been irritated by his wife O-Saku, whom he took four years ago — by her want of charm and her clumsiness with customers. Just then O-Kuni, the wife of his acquaintance Ono, who has been taken up over an affair of promissory notes, comes to them in her destitution, and busily takes on the housework and the shop. At O-Kuni's fair skin and her winning ways and her briskness, Shinkichi finds his heart moving without his knowing it. O-Saku goes back to her parents' house heavy with child, miscarries there, and comes back listless. And even with O-Kuni's brisk bearing before his eyes, feeling for O-Saku wells up in Shinkichi's breast instead. The days pass as though nothing had happened, and at the end of that autumn O-Saku was with child again.

Characters

  • Shinkichi　The master of a liquor shop. The hero, who is irritated by his wife's clumsiness and, through O-Kuni's presence, discovers his feeling for O-Saku again
  • O-Saku　Shinkichi's wife. She has no charm and is unfit for trade, but she is plain and warm-hearted
  • O-Kuni　The wife of Shinkichi's acquaintance Ono. While her husband is in prison she takes shelter in Shinkichi's house and works busily

The opening (original text)

It was in the winter of exactly four years ago that Shinkichi took O-Saku, when he was twenty-five and she was twenty. At fourteen, his oversensitive boy's mind stirred by success stories of great merchants and the like, he had bolted for Tokyo, and for eleven years had worked recklessly and without a glance to either side at a liquor wholesaler's in Shinkawa. Even when he rented a little house on the main street and opened a new shop for liquor and soy sauce, firewood, charcoal and salt, he worked and worked without stopping, grudging the very time it took to eat. With his sleeves tied back and his feet bare in his socks, he would sit at the shopfront and shovel down his rice in silence, as though in a hurry.

The ending (original text)

Shinkichi drew her to him and tried to kiss her on the cheek. O-Saku's cheek was cold as ice. * * * At the end of that autumn, when straw-wrapped casks were piled up in Shinkichi's shop with "In celebration of the third year of business...," O-Saku was with child again.
