About this work

A short story published in Chūō Kōron in November 1911. Tired of city society, the narrator hides himself in a room at a temple in Asakusa, gives himself to the perverse pleasure of walking the night streets dressed as a woman, and is drawn into blindfolded meetings with a former lover. Scattered with the motifs — disguise, anonymity, voyeurism — that would become the ground of Tanizaki's later work and of Edogawa Ranpo's world. A central early work of the aesthetic and fantastic.

Summary

Wanting to escape a busy social life, the narrator rents a room at a Shingon temple in Matsubachō in Asakusa, and takes to spending his days reading books on magic and detective stories and his nights wandering the back streets in disguise. One evening, captivated by a woman's lined kimono he finds at a secondhand shop, he goes out through the night streets of Asakusa dressed as a woman. Made up and wrapped in women's clothes, he loses himself in the crowd in a rapture in which even his skin seems to have become a woman's. Inside the Sanyūkan theater he happens to meet again a woman with whom he had an affair on the passage to Shanghai. She sees through his disguise, and there begins a series of meetings in which she has him carried blindfolded to her house by rickshaw. He is intoxicated by the sweetness of a secret love in which neither will reveal who they are; but before long he cannot master his desire to find out where her house is, and, reasoning out the blindfolded route from memory, he tracks the house down in broad daylight. The woman proves to be Yoshino, a well-to-do widow near the Kaminarimon. The moment every riddle is solved his heart goes cold, and he abandons her. No longer satisfied with so mild a pleasure as a secret, he gives up the temple and goes in search of a more highly colored, bloodier delight.

Characters

  • I　A man hiding at a temple in Asakusa, given over to dressing as a woman and to a secret affair
  • The woman (Yoshino)　A widow with whom the narrator had an affair aboard ship, who keeps her identity hidden through their blindfolded meetings

The opening (original text)

At that time, out of a certain whim, I wanted to get away from the lively atmosphere that had until then enveloped me, and to slip secretly out of the circle of men and women with whom I had gone on associating for one reason or another; and after searching here and there for a suitable hiding place, I found a Shingon temple in the neighborhood of Matsubachō in Asakusa, and at last arranged to rent a room in its priests' quarters.

Himitsu by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The woman was Yoshino, a well-to-do widow of that neighborhood. Just as with the signboard of the seal-carver's shop, every riddle had been solved. I abandoned her from that day. Two or three days later I suddenly gave up the temple and moved out to Tabata. My heart was ceasing, little by little, to be satisfied with so mild and faint a pleasure as a "secret," and was leaning toward the search for a more highly colored, bloodier delight.

Himitsu by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)
