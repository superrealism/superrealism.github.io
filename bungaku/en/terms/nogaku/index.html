What is Nōgaku?

Category
Other

A general term for the masked drama "noh," perfected in the Muromachi period, together with "kyōgen," the comic interludes performed between noh plays. Up through the Edo period it was called "sarugaku," and it was renamed "nōgaku" with the founding of the Nōgakusha in 1881. From the Nanboku-chō period into the Muromachi period, the father-and-son team of Kan'ami and Zeami refined the art under the patronage of Ashikaga Yoshimitsu, and Zeami left the principles of "yūgen" (mysterious depth) and "hana" (flower, or artistic brilliance) in treatises such as "Fūshikaden." The chanted text, called yōkyoku, takes its subjects from "The Tale of the Heike" and court tales and perfected the mugen nō (dream-vision noh) form, in which a ghost appears before a traveling priest and tells of the past; its writing, rich with the rhetoric of waka, is counted among the finest flowers of medieval literature. It has cast a shadow over modern literature as well: Izumi Kyōka left a representative work of the artist's-path novel in "Uta Andon" ("The Song Lantern"), depicting the atonement of a noh actor who drove a woman to her death in a chanting contest, and Yumeno Kyūsaku, in his debut work "Ayakashi no Tsuzumi" ("The Ghostly Drum"), took up the uncanny curse of a noh hand drum — the title's word "ayakashi," too, echoes the noh term for a supernatural being. Together with the narrative arts born in the same medieval period, and with early-modern kabuki, it stands as a wellspring of Japanese theater and verse.
