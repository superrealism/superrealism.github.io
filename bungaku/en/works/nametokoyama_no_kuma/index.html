About this work

A children's story by Miyazawa Kenji, published in 1934 after his death. On Nametoko mountain, famous for bear gall, it draws the sad, deep bond between Fuchizawa Kojūrō, a gentle master bear-hunter, and the bears he shoots — between the one who kills and the ones who are killed. Kojūrō shoots bears to live, and yet cannot hate them. When at last one bear pins him and he dies, the bears gather about him on a snowy moonlit peak as if in prayer. Kenji's thinking on people and nature, life and death, crystallized in quiet compassion.

Summary

Mount Nametoko is famous for bear gall. Fuchizawa Kojūrō, who lives by taking bears on that mountain, is a master hunter, and gentle in a way that does not match his great body. He shoots bears in order to live, and yet in his heart he does not hate them, and there is something in the bears too that seems to be fond of him. Once, hearing a mother bear and her cub talking as they looked out over the valley, he quietly turns back without firing. Another time a bear begs him to wait two years and then to let him die — and two years later, as promised, the bear lies dead before Kojūrō's house, having coughed up blood. But for all that he knows the mountain and the bears so well, the skins and the gall he takes at the risk of his life are beaten down to nothing by the hardware dealer in town, and his life never grows easier. Then one winter day a great bear pins him down at last. Hearing the bear's voice — "Ah, Kojūrō, I did not mean to kill you" — he quietly accepts his death. Three days later, on a night with a moon like ice, on the flat top of a mountain ringed by snowy peaks, great black bears gather in a circle and lie prostrate in the snow, like Muslims at prayer, around Kojūrō's body set on the highest place, and do not move for a long, long time. And the frozen face of Kojūrō looked, if anything, as though it were smiling.

Characters

  • Fuchizawa Kojūrō　A master bear hunter of Mount Nametoko. A big man, gentle in heart, who does not hate the bears he must shoot to live, and who is pinned down at last by one of them and dies
  • The bears　The bears of Mount Nametoko, who are fond of Kojūrō and who gather about his body on the moonlit snowy peaks as if in prayer
  • The hardware dealer　The tradesman in town who beats down the price of Kojūrō's skins and gall to nothing

The opening (original text)

The bears of Mount Nametoko make a good story. Mount Nametoko is a big mountain. The Fuchizawa River comes out of it. On most days of the year Mount Nametoko is breathing in and breathing out a cold mist or cloud. All around it too are mountains, dark blue-black like sea cucumbers or sea monsters. Halfway up the mountain a great cave opens, gaping. Out of it the Fuchizawa River comes down all at once in a waterfall of some three hundred feet, roaring through thickets of cypress and maple.

Nametokoyama no kuma by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

On that flat place at the top of the mountain, ringed by chestnut trees and white snowy peaks, a great many large black shapes had gathered in a circle, each casting its black shadow, and lay prostrate in the snow, still as Muslims at prayer, and did not move for a long, long time. And by that light of snow and moon one could see, set at the very highest point, the body of Kojūrō, half sitting up. Whether it was only fancy or not, the face of the dead and frozen Kojūrō looked as clear as when he had been alive, and even seemed somehow to be smiling.

Famous passages

Bear. I didn't kill you because I hate you. It's my trade, so I have to shoot you. I'd like to do some other work with no sin in it, but I've got no fields, the trees all belong to the government, and when I go down to the village nobody will deal with me. I've no choice but to be a hunter. If it's your ill luck to be born a bear, it's my ill luck to be in this trade. Hey. Next time, don't be born a bear.

The words Kojūrō speaks to a bear he has just shot, over its body. In the dialect of his region he talks of the fate that binds creatures who must kill in order to live, and Kenji's prayer for all life shows through.
