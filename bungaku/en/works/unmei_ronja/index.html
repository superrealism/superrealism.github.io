About this work

A short story Kunikida Doppo published in the magazine Yamabiko in 1903. On the shore at Kamakura in late autumn, the narrator meets a young gentleman who calls himself a fatalist and says that no one can ever escape the power of fate. Behind that conviction lay a history of his own, strange and tragic beyond measure. A meditative piece in which Doppo draws with a taut hand an inescapable destiny bound up with blood and karma.

Summary

In late autumn, on a nearly deserted stretch of beach near Kamakura, "I" fall into conversation with a young gentleman who sits sunning himself while reading a book. Quiet and refined, the man calls himself a "fatalist," insisting that human beings are wholly at the mercy of destiny, and that no power on earth can undo a fact once it has come to pass. Behind this conviction lies an appalling history of his own. He has learned that his own birth mother was in fact his father's mortal enemy — and, more terrible still, that the woman he came to love with his whole heart and took as his wife is his blood sister. He can neither bring himself to hate the mother who bore him, nor extinguish the love he bears his sister-wife. What has already happened lies beyond any human power to change. "And so it is fate," he says quietly, and stands at the crest of the lonely dune, gazing out over the distant sea. "I" shake his hand and part from this unhappy young man, never to see him again. A meditative piece in which Doppo, with taut, controlled prose, traces the crushing weight of a fate bound up in blood and old wrongs.

Characters

  • The young gentleman (the fatalist)　A quiet man whom "I" meet on the Kamakura shore. Learning that his own mother was his father's enemy and that his wife is his blood sister, he becomes convinced that no one can escape fate
  • I　The narrator, who lives in Kamakura and talks with the young gentleman on the dunes

The opening (original text)

One Once autumn has passed its midpoint and winter draws near, every seaside, whatever its name, grows desolate — and Kamakura is no exception. Aside from those like myself who live here the year round, one finds on the beach only village children, children of the shore, men hauling their beach seines, or peddlers passing along the strand; the sight of anyone who looks a proper townsman is a rare thing indeed.

The Fatalist by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"That is why it is fate. Even if I divorced her, the fact that my true mother was my father's enemy would not be erased. Even if I divorced her, my love for my sister, whom I love as my wife, would not change. So long as no human power can erase a fact once it has come to pass, no man, I think, can ever truly escape the power of fate." I shook his hand, bowed to him in silence, and parted from this unfortunate young gentleman. The sun had already set, its lingering brilliance dyeing the evening clouds; and looking back, I saw my fatalist standing at the top of the lonely dune, gazing far out over the offing. I have never met that man again since.
