About this work

A travel piece from the summer of 1890, when Mori Ōgai, twenty-eight, stayed at the Yamada hot spring in Shinshū. The trouble of the horse tram over the Usui pass, life at a hot-spring inn in the hills, the boasting of the man in the next room, and the power of Western clothes forced on his notice along the way — the young Ōgai's eye takes in the details of a journey very well.

Summary

August seventeenth: the first train out of Ueno. From Yokokawa, a railway coach like a blue-painted wooden box climbs toward Usui Pass. The knees of its twelve passengers cross like dog's teeth, and once the sailcloth curtains are drawn shut, the smell of sweat fills the carriage until one's head feels ready to split — from the very opening lines of the account, Ōgai's eye is fixed on the concrete particulars of travel. Passing through Karuizawa, Ueda, and Suzaka, and borrowing the back of an ox to climb the mountain road, he arrives at last at the hot springs of Yamada, in Shinano — a mountain hamlet of only fifteen households, where a guest from Tokyo is never seen. The inn's meals consist of nothing but vegetables and river trout; bedding costs one and a half sen a head; and one washes one's face each morning in the stream before the house. In the bath itself, men and women lie about the edges pouring water over themselves, so that to move forward one must step over necks and legs alike. His diary during the stay records all manner of things: a sharp-eyed sketch of the bearded man in the next room who talks of nothing but the prefectural office; conversations exchanged with the innkeeper; a drawing made from life of the crematorium's construction; and even the tale, heard from a fellow guest, a judge, of Usui Rokurō's vendetta. On the way home, the railway lines cut apart by flood damage, he presses on in straw sandals, crossing mountains and fording ravines to piece his journey back together. What Ōgai sets down at the very end is the "power of Western dress" he had witnessed throughout this journey: that in the finest rooms, the tatami mats can scarcely be trod upon except by one in Western clothes — something, he notes, any traveler would do well to keep in mind.

The opening (original text)

On the seventeenth of August, Meiji 23, I set out on the first train from Ueno. Along the way I changed trains once, and the line itself ended at Yokokawa. From there I hired a railway coach and set off up toward Usui Pass. This coach was a wooden box painted blue on the outside, and within, the twelve passengers riding together had their shoulders and hips pressed against one another, their knees crossing like dog's teeth.

Michi no ki by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Throughout this whole journey, I lost count of the times I saw the power that Western dress holds. At teahouses, at inns, and elsewhere, the tatami of the very finest rooms can scarcely be trod upon except by one in Western clothes, and a man in Western dress, however late he arrives, may still be served food and drink ahead of everyone else. Whether one tips much or little is very much a secondary matter; what matters most of all is simply whether one's clothing is Western or Japanese. This is something any traveler ought to bear in mind. And yet there is no need to go to any great expense over it — sailcloth will serve, so long as it is cut in the Western style. I have seen a man in a white jacket, its armpits already gone yellow with wear, held in higher regard than a man in a fresh new yukata.

Michi no ki by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)
