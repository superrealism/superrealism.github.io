About this work

Kusuyama Masao's version of the tale by Charles Perrault known the world over as Cinderella; the original title is "Cendrillon, or the Little Glass Slipper." The pumpkin coach, the promise about midnight and the glass slippers, all familiar today, come from this version of Perrault's. The ending, in which she forgives her spiteful stepsisters at the last and takes them into the palace, stands in contrast to the ferocious close of the Grimm version, and it finishes with the lesson that a gentle heart, more than a beautiful face, is the fairies' gift.

Summary

The woman a certain gentleman took as his second wife was proud and willful, and the two daughters she brought with her were exactly like their mother. Only the daughter of the first wife took after her dead mother, being honest and gentle, and for that her stepmother hated her, put her down into the kitchen, and worked her like a servant. When her work was done she would crouch among the ashes, and so her sisters called her Cinder-wench. On the day of the prince's ball she dresses her sisters' hair and sees them off, and as she sits weeping alone her fairy godmother appears. A pumpkin becomes a coach of gold, mice become horses, lizards become footmen, and her rags become a gown of silver and gold — on the condition that at the stroke of midnight everything will be as it was. Famed at the ball as the most beautiful of all, she gets away at the last moment the first night, and on the second forgets the hour, runs, and drops one glass slipper on the stair. The prince has the owner of that little slipper searched for throughout the kingdom. The foot of the cinder girl goes straight into the slipper that neither sister could get on, and she draws the other from her pocket to show them. Made a princess, she embraces her sisters when they beg her pardon, takes them into the palace, and marries them to noblemen.

Characters

  • Cinderella　The first wife's daughter, put down into the kitchen and called Cinder-wench
  • Stepmother　The proud and willful second wife
  • The two sisters　The stepmother's daughters, who do not recognize her at the ball
  • The fairy godmother　She turns the pumpkin into a coach and warns her about midnight
  • The prince　He searches for the owner of the glass slipper

The opening (original text)

1 Once upon a time there was a gentleman who lived in want of nothing. But the woman he took for his second wife was the haughtiest, proudest, most overbearing woman that ever was — there was not another like her. She had had two children by her first husband and brought them with her when she came; and those daughters were, in everything from first to last, exactly like their mother — bad, willful girls. Now this gentleman had one other young daughter, by his first wife; and she took after her dead mother, who had been as good a person in disposition and in heart as could be found, and she was the most honest and gentle child in the world.

The ending (original text)

— and begged them only to be kind to her from then on. Cinderella, dressed just as she was in her fine clothes, was taken before the prince. He liked her better than ever at the sight of her, and four or five days later they were happily married. And because Cinderella was as good of heart as she was fair of face, she had her two sisters lodged in the palace as well, and on the very day of her wedding married them to two noblemen of the court. Beauty of face and figure is a precious treasure in man and woman alike. But you must know that a gentle and modest heart is the greatest of all the fairies' gifts.
