What is Shichigochō and Goshichichō (Seven-Five and Five-Seven Meter)?

Category
Style & Technique

The syllabic meters that form the basis of Japanese verse, in which phrases of five and seven syllables are combined and repeated. When the phrases are grouped in the order five-seven, the meter is called goshichichō (five-seven); when grouped seven-five, it is called shichigochō (seven-five). Five-seven meter is common in the chōka and tanka of the "Man'yōshū" and is said to carry a weighty, rustic strength; in tanka it tends to produce poems whose sense breaks after the second or fourth phrase. Seven-five meter, by contrast, gradually gained ground from around the end of the Nara period and had become the mainstream of waka by the time of the "Kokin Wakashū"; it is said to have a light, graceful tone, and in tanka it appears in poems that break after the first or third phrase. Imayō and wasan hymns, which stack four seven-five phrases, as well as the iroha poem, are in seven-five meter, and it was carried on in war tales, nō librettos, the travel passages (michiyuki) of jōruri, and the celebrated lines Kawatake Mokuami wrote for kabuki. In the Meiji era, the new-style verse that began with "Shintaishishō" in 1882 started out with forms centered on seven-five meter, and Shimazaki Tōson's "Wakanashū" ("Seedlings," 1897) announced the arrival of modern poetry with its fresh seven-five cadences. In his translated anthology "Kaichōon" ("Sound of the Tide," 1905), Ueda Bin rendered the Parnassian poets mainly in forms based on seven-five meter, while taking liberties with the form for the Symbolists. Colloquial free verse, which began at the end of the Meiji era and was established in the Taishō era, sought a new poetic language by leaving these fixed syllabic meters behind.
