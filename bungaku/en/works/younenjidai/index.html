About this work

The first of the three autobiographical novels Murō Saisei published in Chūō Kōron in 1919. Born illegitimate and given away for adoption soon after birth, the narrator's lonely, acutely sensitive childhood in Kanazawa is drawn here. Going back and forth between the house he was born into and the house that took him, the boy's longing for a birth mother whose face he does not know and for a kind elder sister is caught with a poet's fine sensibility. The starting point of Saisei's move from poetry into fiction.

Summary

By the time "I" could remember anything, I had already been given away for adoption. In the town of Kanazawa there are two houses: the house where I was born, and the house where I now live. In the days under my adoptive mother there is something never quite satisfied, and though I warn myself that I must not go, almost every day I go to the house of my birth and steep myself in the slow, warm time that flows there. Longing for the mother I bore me and have never seen, and love for my gentle elder sister, gather in the breast of a lonely and sensitive boy. The fine beauty of the nature of Kanazawa — the scenery along the Sai river, the apricots in the garden, the turning of the seasons — is drawn as if to wrap the boy's solitude and his keen sensibility. There is no great plot to speak of; the sorrow of one born a bastard, and the love for his own flesh and blood that grows the stronger for it, rise quietly out of a soft lyricism caught by a poet's eye.

The opening (original text)

I often went to play at the house of my birth. It was a small, snug house in a back street, surrounded by a wide orchard. There was a spear hung in the entrance and four heavy doors of cypress. My father was already past sixty, but my mother was a fair-skinned woman in her forties, with the traces of her eyebrows showing blue. When I came bursting into the sitting room —

The ending (original text)

Under the forlorn lamp I would sometimes lie with my great eyes open for a long while, thinking of my sister and remembering my mother. The sound of the shallows of the river behind, and the night wind, came quietly as far as my pillow. The winter of my thirteenth year was already drawing to its close.
