About this work

A long novel following Koizumi Jun'ichi, a young man who has come up to Tokyo meaning to be a writer, as he mixes with the literary young men of the city, is drawn to an older widow, and gropes for his own way of living. Said to have been written under the stimulus of Sōseki's Sanshirō, it copies down whole the intellectual and literary air at the end of Meiji: a central long novel by Mori Ōgai.

Summary

Koizumi Jun'ichi is a twenty-one-year-old youth who has come up to Tokyo from N Prefecture aspiring to become a novelist. Map of Tokyo in hand, he calls at a boarding house in Hongō and begins his life in the capital relying on an old acquaintance, Ōishi Kentarō. Jun'ichi finds himself set down among the literary youth of late Meiji, in a world alive with debate over naturalism, Ibsen, Nietzsche, and Bergson. He attends a lecture by Hirata Fuseki, a towering figure of the literary establishment (a character evoking Sōseki), talks with the reflective elder Ōmura Sōnosuke, visits theaters and exhibitions, and sets down his thoughts each day in his diary. In the midst of all this, Jun'ichi meets a beautiful widow, Sakai Reiko. Though powerfully drawn to this older woman, he remains troubled by her free-spirited manner and the calculation he senses beneath it. A proposed match with Oyuki, a girl from his home province, and the faint affection of Okinu, the maid at his lodgings, further unsettle his heart, leaving him unable to make up his mind. In time, at a hot-spring inn in Hakone, Jun'ichi meets Mrs. Sakai again. After the events of a single night, he looks back over what it was he had truly been seeking, and resolves to turn toward the work he is meant to write. On New Year's morning, under a clear sky, his carriage descends a misted road on Mount Yusaka — "The novel Young Man ends here, for the present," Ōgai adds at the close, noting that he has written only a small part of what he set out to write, and so brings this long novel to a close.

The opening (original text)

One Koizumi Jun'ichi left the inn at Hikage-chō in Shiba, and, map of Tokyo in hand, kept pestering people with questions as he boarded the streetcar bound for Ueno at Shinbashi stop. The bewildering transfer at Sudachō went off without mishap. Getting off the streetcar at Hongō 3-chōme, turning right at Oiwake along the Higher School, he arrived, at eight in the morning of some day toward the end of October, before a boarding house called Sodeurakan, at the top of the front slope of the Nezu Gongen shrine.

Seinen by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Ōgai says: the novel Young Man ends here, for the present. I have written only a small part of what I set out to write, and the days that have passed within the story amount to no more than sixty or seventy. Beginning with the season when the frost first starts to fall, I have only just managed to row my way through to a winter that has yet to see any real snow. And in the time it has taken me to write even that much, two years have somehow slipped by. In any case, let this be the end, for the present.

Seinen by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)
