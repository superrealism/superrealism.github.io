About this work

"A scientist has to be slow-witted" — Terada Torahiko's famous paradox, put into the mouth of an old scientist. The clever are like travellers who walk fast and miss what matters at the roadside; it is exactly being a slow country fellow, he argues, that a researcher needs. Still quoted again and again in science education.

Summary

"To become a scientist one must have a good head" — this is a proposition the world often utters. But the proposition "a scientist must have a bad head" is, in a certain sense, just as true. Terada, giving the words of an old scientist he is close to, unravels these two apparent paradoxes. To let go of not one link in a chain of logic, and not to take the wrong road at a fork, one does indeed need an exact and close mind. But at the same time it is even more important for a researcher to find, among the everyday matters that everyone thinks perfectly obvious, some unintelligible point of doubt, and to labor over it. A man with a good head is like a traveler with quick feet: he arrives ahead of others, but for that very reason he overlooks the important things at the roadside and on the byways. It happens that the man with the bad head, coming along far behind, picks up that treasure without knowing why. The man with the good head looks at the summit of Fuji from the foot of the slope, takes in the whole of it and goes home; the man with the bad head climbs step by step and sees great forests and valleys and craters he had never expected. Terada then goes on to say that to read the book of nature one needs not only a head but hands and feet and eyes; that misfortune and failure are nourishment for originality; and that one must not mistake science for the whole of human wisdom. The worlds of Laozi and Socrates and Bashō and Hiroshige are human facts to which the country of science has as yet no road.

The opening (original text)

An old scientist with whom I am close told me one day the following. "To become a scientist one must have a good head" — this is a proposition commonly uttered by people in the world. In a certain sense I believe it to be true. But on the other hand the proposition "a scientist must have a bad head" is, in a certain sense, just as true.

Kagakusha to atama by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Anyone who feels displeasure at reading this old scientist's rambling must surely be an enviably fine-headed scholar. And anyone who reads it and lets out a smile of agreement must surely be an enviably bad-headed and admirable scientist. And anyone who reads it and thinks nothing at all is likely, I fancy, to be some science educator or science tradesman who has no connection with the world of science.

Kagakusha to atama by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

A clever person cannot fall in love. Love is blind. To become a scientist, one must take nature as one's beloved. Nature, after all, opens her heart only to her lover.

A passage from an essay that describes, through paradox, the qualities a scientist needs. It holds that nature reveals her secrets only to those who take her as their beloved and devote themselves to her blindly.

A person afraid of getting hurt cannot become a carpenter. A person afraid of failure cannot become a scientist. Science, too, is a temple built upon a mountain of the corpses of reckless dullards, a flower garden blooming beside a river of blood.

A passage from the same essay. In fierce metaphors, it tells how science has been built upon the countless sacrifices of “dull-witted” people unafraid of failure.

In short, one must be dull-witted and, at the same time, clever.

The sentence that sums up the essay. The heart of Torahiko's view of science: that a scientist must combine the “dullness” to face nature with simple honesty and the “cleverness” of precise observation and reasoning.
