About this work

A very short piece Hirotsu Ryūrō wrote during the Russo-Japanese War, dated 1905. It draws the crush at Shinjuku station as soldiers leave for the front, through the eyes of a young woman come to see them off. Amid the waves of flags and the cheering, she notices two contrasting women: one with sharp eyes, who curses the war in a shrill voice without minding who hears her and cries that they shall not take her brother, and a poor farmer's wife with a baby at her breast, holding back her grief and sending her husband off in silence. A small piece copying in a restrained hand the partings and sorrow that war forces on ordinary people, later looked back on as a forerunner of antiwar literature.

Summary

On a bitterly cold winter morning, "I" go with my friend Wakako to Shinjuku station to see off her elder brother, who is departing for the front. The station is packed with soldiers leaving for war and their families, colorful flags fluttering in the morning wind. In the crowd, "I" notice several people. One is an unpleasant student who keeps hovering persistently nearby. Another is a woman, beautiful in face but with sharp eyes that seem to curse everything around her — she rails, in a shrill voice heedless of who might hear, against war itself, demanding to know why soldiering is a trade in dying, why anyone should be forced to send another's child off to be killed, and she cries, weeping, at her own soldier brother, "Never die," to his evident distress. And then there is another woman, a poor farmer's young wife, carrying an infant scarcely five months old. Holding back her tears, she quietly bids farewell to her soldier husband, saying, "Even if you die in battle, I'll try to accept it, telling myself it was for the sake of everyone in Japan," and shows him the baby's face. Everyone nearby who witnesses her modest grief cannot help but weep along with her. After the train departs, that same sharp-eyed woman glares at the farmer's wife, muttering that she said things she didn't mean. A short piece that renders, through the contrast between these two women, the parting that war forces upon ordinary people.

The opening (original text)

Part One Nishida Wakako-san's elder brother, who had been stationed with the Sendai division, was this time bound for the front, and since he was to pass through Shinjuku station, I went along with Wakako-san to see him off. It was a bitterly, bitterly cold morning, cold enough that my earlobes felt as though they might snap right off, and the soles of my shoes seemed to freeze fast to the ground. Even on so cold a morning as this, the station was already full of people. When I thought that all these many people must have some connection to someone departing for the front, and had gathered here to grieve the parting, my heart grew so full I could hardly bear it.

Shōkōjō by Hirotsu Ryūrō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"I think I'll change my principles too, and feel some sympathy for that farmer's wife." It was the voice of that unpleasant student, so we hurried out of the station and climbed into our rickshaw, which had been waiting for us, and rode home. As for that sharp-eyed woman, I found myself wondering whether she was really Japanese at all, or perhaps a foreigner. And yet, no matter how I looked at her face, it was, unmistakably, the face of a Japanese woman!

Shōkōjō by Hirotsu Ryūrō (Aozora Bunko)
