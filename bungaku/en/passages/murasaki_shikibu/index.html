Famous passages by Murasaki Shikibu

Well-known lines from the works of Murasaki Shikibu.

4 passages

The Tale of Genji

In the reign of a certain emperor—which one it was, I do not know—among the many consorts and ladies of the court, there was one who, though not of the highest birth, enjoyed his deepest affection.

The opening of The Tale of Genji in Yosano Akiko's modern Japanese translation. Rendering the original's “In the reign of which emperor was it?” in plain words, it begins the story of the Kiritsubo Consort, who will become the mother of the Shining Genji.

“Inuki let my baby sparrow go — and I'd kept it in the basket so it couldn't get away!”

The words of the young girl — later Murasaki — whom Genji glimpses in the northern hills, as she complains tearfully to the nun. Her innocence, crying over a lost sparrow, leaves a vivid impression of the fateful first meeting of Genji and Murasaki (in Yosano Akiko's modern translation).

It came to be the season when the autumn wind blows through the village of Suma. The sea was a little distance off, yet the sound of the waves — the autumn waves that Yukihira sang of, high enough to cross even the barrier of Suma — echoed loud especially at night, and nothing was so unbearably lonely as autumn in a place of exile.

Autumn in the lonely dwelling to which Genji has withdrawn at Suma, far from the capital. The sound of the waves, drawn from a poem by Ariwara no Yukihira, heightens the loneliness of exile in one of the finest scenes of the tale (in Yosano Akiko's modern translation).

Someone came out toward this doorway singing in a young and noble voice, “There is nothing like a night of hazy moonlight.” Delighted, Genji suddenly caught hold of her sleeve.

On the night of the flower banquet at the palace, Genji catches the sleeve of a lady who appears murmuring “There is nothing like a night of hazy moonlight.” The scene marks the beginning of his dangerous affair with Oborozukiyo (in Yosano Akiko's modern translation).
