About this work

A short story published in Bungakukai in 1942 alongside "The Moon Over the Mountain," as part of "Old Tales." In Nineveh, capital of ancient Assyria, the old scholar Nabu-ahhe-eriba is ordered to determine whether there is such a thing as a spirit of letters, and at the end of his enquiry into writing falls prey to a terrible "disease of analysis." An intellectual, fantastical parable about letters, meaning and existence.

Summary

Is there such a thing as a spirit of letters, or is there not? In ancient Assyria, in the reign of the great king Ashurbanipal, a rumor goes about that whispering voices are heard every night in the library of the palace at Nineveh. It must be the work of a "spirit of letters," and the king orders the old scholar Nabu-ahe-eriba to study this unknown spirit. Unrolling ten thousand volumes of clay tablets brings him no answer, and the scholar simply stares at a single character. And then — as he looks at it long enough, the character comes apart, until he can see nothing in it but a crossing of meaningless lines. Why should a mere collection of lines have a sound and a meaning? Here the scholar grants the existence of a spirit of letters; but his discovery brings a dreadful disease upon him. Not letters only: houses, the human body, every ordinary business of life, break apart as he looks at them into an assembly of meaningless parts. It is the "disease of analysis," in which the whole foundation of human life comes to look doubtful. Nearly out of his mind, the scholar presents his report to the king with a criticism of the worship of letters appended, and this displeases the king, who orders him confined to his house. Then, on the day of the great earthquake, the scholar, who happens to be in his library, is crushed to death under a mass of falling clay tablets — by the revenge of the spirit of letters. It is one of Nakajima Atsushi's finest stories, crystallizing the strangeness of writing, meaning and existence into a fantastic fable.

The opening (original text)

Is there, after all, such a thing as a spirit of letters, or is there not? The Assyrians know spirits without number. Lilu, who prowls the darkness by night, and his female Lilitu; Namtar, who scatters plague; Etimmu, the spirit of the dead; Labasu, the abductor — countless evil spirits fill the Assyrian sky. But of a spirit of letters no one has ever yet heard.

The ending (original text)

But that was not the end of it. Some days later, when a great earthquake struck the region of Nineveh and Arbela, the scholar happened to be in the library of his own house. His house was old, and the walls crumbled and the shelves went over. A vast number of books — several hundred heavy clay tablets — came down on this slanderer along with the terrible curses of the letters, and he was crushed to death without mercy.

Famous passages

Whether there is, after all, any such thing as a spirit of letters—

The opening. From this question begins the story, set in ancient Assyria, of an old scholar who stares at letters too long and falls under the curse of their spirit.

If you think that you or I are using writing to set things down, you are gravely mistaken. It is we who are the servants, worked hard by the spirits of writing.

Words spoken to a young historian by the old scholar who studies the spirit of writing. The reversal — that we do not use writing, but are used by it — still speaks to a present surrounded by words and information.
