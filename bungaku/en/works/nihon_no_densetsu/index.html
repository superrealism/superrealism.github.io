About this work

An introduction to legend that Yanagita Kunio wrote for young readers. Gathering strange tellings from all over the country — about cherry trees, springs, camellias and stones — it leads the reader gently to the folklorist's question of why the same legends should be found in so many places.

Summary

"Japan is a country with a surprising number of legends" — so Yanagita Kunio begins. There was a time when in every district there were five or ten people who remembered them well and were willing to tell them. But lately fewer people are glad to listen to such stories, and the legends are being forgotten and got wrong. Regretting that, Yanagita wrote this book for young people who like to read. Cherry trees, springs of clear water, camellias, stones, mountain lakes — the various things handed down all over the country are told here with life in them. What Yanagita finds strangest of all is that one and the same legend is scattered here and there throughout Japan, and that the people of every district believe it to be "something that really happened in our own place." There must be some hidden reason for it, but the reason is not yet clear — and Yanagita means to plant in his young readers the "scholarly curiosity" to solve that riddle. A gentle yet deep book of legends, written as a doorway into folklore studies.

The opening (original text)

Words on sending it into the world again Japan is a country with a surprising number of legends. In earlier days there were, in every district, five or ten people who remembered them well and were willing to tell them. But lately all sorts of new things have arisen that must be thought about, and since fewer people are glad to listen to stories of this kind, there are gradually fewer occasions for remembering them, and they are being forgotten and got wrong. Regretting that so much, I have tried writing this book, first of all for young people who like to read.

Nihon no densetsu by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Japanese writing has, on the whole, used too many difficult words that are unfamiliar to the ear. For things such as legends, which have been handed down for so long only in spoken words, some other way of setting them down is surely needed; but I have been lacking in that preparation as well. Those who look at this book for the first time must give their attention to that point too.

Nihon no densetsu by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)
