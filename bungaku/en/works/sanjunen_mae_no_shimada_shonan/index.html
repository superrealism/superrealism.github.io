About this work

The politician Shimada Shōnan — Saburō — mourned by the whole country as a man of spotless integrity. On his death, Uchida Roan goes back thirty years in memory and draws him without idealizing: the odd habit of making a display of poverty, the scandal of a first wife known for her love affairs, the virtue of forgiving an ungrateful student who betrayed him, and the fastidiousness that lay behind the pure poverty.

Summary

A recollection in which Uchida Roan, refusing to make an idol of him, draws Shimada Shōnan (Shimada Saburō) — the politician buried with great ceremony as a man of integrity — at his true size. He begins with the way Shōnan skillfully saw off those who called on him with "Come again at your leisure"; and with the "pose of honest poverty" by which he made a show of being poor when he had no need to, living in luxury and yet telling hollow tales of poverty when his staff asked for a rise. There are also his changes of mind from morning to evening as president of the Mainichi, and the faithlessness of notifying a member of the staff of his dismissal on a single postcard. At the center is the affair in which, while Shōnan was abroad, his former wife, famous for her beauty, misconducted herself with Y, a pimpled student who kept the entrance; and the fine story of how Shōnan, far from resenting the man who had smeared his own face, pardoned Y, admonished him patiently, gave him money for the journey home and books for his self-improvement, and looked after him until his death — all told in detail by an author who knew the circumstances closely at the time. While admiring this great magnanimity deeply, he judges that Shōnan was not a great character with a magnanimity like the sea, but a fastidious man who could not bear small faults and petty offenses; and he writes that why he should have been generous to Y alone is a riddle to him still. He touches too on the famous speech in which Shōnan impeached the Siemens affair, and does not hide the gap between his last years, when he preached the abolition of licensed prostitution, and his prime, when he was famous for frequenting it; and he offers a Shōnan just as he was, with the flaws and the fine qualities alike uncovered.

Characters

  • Shimada Shōnan　Properly Shimada Saburō. A politician and journalist famous for his integrity, and president of the Mainichi. He impeached the Siemens affair and in his last years gave himself to the movement against licensed prostitution
  • His former wife　Shōnan's wife, famous as a beauty of Banchō, whose name was soiled by the affair while he was abroad
  • Y　The student who kept Shōnan's entrance. He misconducted himself with his benefactor's wife and was pardoned
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who at the time of the affair was on close terms with Y

The opening (original text)

Shimada Shōnan was buried as a great statesman. The whole world mourned him as a gentleman of an integrity and purity such as is seen but once in a hundred years. The judgment that is settled when the coffin is closed had in it enough to exalt the political achievements of Shōnan's life more than any brilliant decoration. I had not met Shōnan for the last fourteen or fifteen years. Even before that we were acquainted only enough to exchange greetings when we met, and I had visited his house only two or three times. And even on those two or three occasions I was always kept waiting more than half an hour, and having at last sat down and finished my business in ten minutes or so, it was always ended at once with, "Do please come again at your leisure and pass the time with me," asking for another visit another day; so that I could not help taking my leave on the spot.

Sanjūnen mae no shimada shōnan by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Shōnan gave his last years too to purifying public morals, running east and west and never resting from his arguments for the abolition of licensed prostitution and of drink. But in his prime Shōnan was famous rather for patronizing than for abolishing it. In those days, however, such things were taken for elegance and not made a moral question. Even General Nogi, who is enshrined as a god and the very crystal of loyalty and filial piety, tied his horse in his youth in the quarters of the willows and the flowers; so that to dig out the other side of the young Shōnan's dissipations does not in the least injure his virtue. Shōnan hated lies. It was not two or three times that I heard from Shōnan's own mouth, "I have never told a lie." To speak honestly of a naked Shōnan just as he was, with the flaws and the fine qualities alike uncovered, will give the Shōnan beneath the moss a smile and more satisfaction than to treat him as an idol, a wooden Puritan.

Sanjūnen mae no shimada shōnan by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
