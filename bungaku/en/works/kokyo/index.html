About this work

A short story by Lu Xun (1921) in which "I," returning to his old home after twenty years, meets again the Runtu with whom he was close as a boy, and is struck down by how he has changed. In Inoue Kōbai's translation, included in the Kaizōsha "Complete Works of Lu Xun." In Japan it was long included in middle-school readers, and the closing passage is widely known: "Hope is not a thing one may call existent, nor a thing one may call non-existent. It is like the roads upon the earth — at first there is no road, but when many people walk, a road comes into being."

Summary

Braving the bitter cold, "I" come back after more than twenty years to the old home two thousand li away, to give up the old house that has been sold and to take my mother and my nephew Hong'er away to another district. The home in my memory ought to have been a far better place, but what lies before me is a lifeless, wasted village. In talking with my mother the name of Runtu comes up: a boy who came to the house thirty years ago, in the year of the great sacrifice, as the son of a hired man. He wore a silver collar, and told me of spearing a zha in the melon fields by the sea, of shells and of leaping fish. To me he was the whole world that lay outside a life shut in by high walls. When Runtu at last appears, his gray face is cut with deep lines and his hands are like the bark of a pine. The mouth that once called me Xun says respectfully, "Master." With that one word I understand that a thick wall stands between us. Many children, bad harvests, heavy taxes — what he asks for is an incense burner and candlesticks. On the boat leaving home, thinking of how Hong'er and Runtu's son Shuisheng had promised to meet again, I waver between hope for a new life and the suspicion that this hope too may be an idol of my own making.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, who comes back to his old home after more than twenty years to give up the old house
  • Runtu　The playmate of his boyhood, now a tenant farmer worn down with hardship
  • Mother　She gives up the old home and moves to another district
  • Hong'er　My nephew, who makes friends with Runtu's son Shuisheng
  • Second Sister-in-law Yang　The woman from the bean-curd shop across the way, who cadges the household goods while making sarcastic remarks

The opening (original text)

Braving the bitter cold, I came back to the old home from which I had been parted more than twenty years, and which lay more than two thousand li away. It was deep in winter, and as I drew nearer to my old home the weather darkened, and a wind that cut to the bone blew into the cabin of the boat and howled there. Looking out through the gaps in the matting, I saw lying here and there under a sallow sky a few desolate villages without a trace of life in them, and I could not hold down a sadness that stirred in me. Ah! Was this the old home I had thought of again and again these twenty years? The old home I remembered was nothing at all like this. My old home had far more that was fine about it. But to set down what was fine about it I have neither image nor words; and so I may as well say that it was something like this after all. And then I explain it to myself, and say that my old home was always like this.

The ending (original text)

And thinking so, I was suddenly ashamed. When Runtu had asked for the incense burner and the candlesticks, I had laughed at him in my heart: he could not help worshipping idols, I thought, and could not put them out of his mind at any time. And now was not what I call hope an idol of my own making? Only that his hope was near at hand, and mine far off and dim. Half asleep and half awake, there spread out before my eyes a wide green stretch of sand by the sea, and above it, hanging in a deep blue sky, a full moon the color of gold. Hope is not a thing one may call existent, nor a thing one may call non-existent. It is like the roads upon the earth: at first there is no road, but when many people walk, a road comes into being. (January 1921)
