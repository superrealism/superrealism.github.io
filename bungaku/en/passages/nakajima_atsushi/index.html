Famous passages by Nakajima Atsushi

Well-known lines from the works of Nakajima Atsushi.

19 passages

The Moon Over the Mountain

Ri Chō of Longxi was a man of broad learning and keen talent; in the last years of the Tianbao era he passed the civil examinations while still young and was appointed a district officer in the south of the Yangtze. But he was proud and unbending by nature, thought a great deal of himself, and could not bring himself to be content with the post of a petty official.

An opening that introduces its protagonist in a single dignified sentence of Sino-Japanese cadence. Already it hints at the tragedy to come: a man whose pride as a poet will bring him down into the body of a tiger.

Both are the doing of my own cowardly self-regard, and of a shame so overweening it became pride.

A scene in which Ri Chō, now a tiger, reveals his heart to an old friend. This line, naming both a poet's pride and his fear, is often quoted as a figure for the human heart itself.

It was not only the night dew that had left his fur wet.

A line Ri Chō, now a tiger, lets slip as he parts from his friend. This restraint — conveying tears without ever saying he wept — sets the dignified tone of the entire story.

While I toyed with glib epigrams like “Life is too long to do nothing, yet too short to accomplish anything,” in truth all I was, was a cowardly fear that my lack of talent might be exposed and a laziness that shrank from hard work.

The confession of Ri Chō, now a tiger, regretting a past in which he failed to polish his talent. Quoting a famous epigram with self-mockery, he gazes painfully at his own weakness in avoiding effort.

They say every man is a tamer of wild beasts, and that the beast is each man's own nature. In my case, this overweening shame was the beast. It was the tiger.

Ri Chō explaining why he became a tiger. He realizes that everyone keeps a beast within, and that his present form is the result of failing to master his own.

“That voice—is it not my friend, Ri Chō?”

What En San cries out, having nearly been attacked by a tiger, when he recognizes the voice from the thicket. This unexpected reunion with an old friend draws out Ri Chō's confession.

If the human heart within me were to vanish completely, I would probably be the happier for it. And yet the human being within me feels that very thing to be the most terrible of all.

Words from the scene in which Ri Chō, turned into a tiger, opens his heart to his old friend. Knowing that he would be at ease if he lost his human heart, yet fearing that above all else — the ache of being human is condensed in this passage.

The Curse of Letters

Whether there is, after all, any such thing as a spirit of letters—

The opening. From this question begins the story, set in ancient Assyria, of an old scholar who stares at letters too long and falls under the curse of their spirit.

If you think that you or I are using writing to set things down, you are gravely mistaken. It is we who are the servants, worked hard by the spirits of writing.

Words spoken to a young historian by the old scholar who studies the spirit of writing. The reversal — that we do not use writing, but are used by it — still speaks to a present surrounded by words and information.

Gojō shusse

We live standing only upon the moment called the present. And that present beneath our feet at once vanishes and becomes the past.

A passage from a story whose hero, Sha Wujing of Journey to the West, keeps asking what the self is. Like a traveler standing on crumbling sand, it speaks of a present that vanishes moment by moment.

The Master

A man named Ji Chang, who lived in Handan, capital of the state of Zhao, set his mind on becoming the greatest archer under heaven.

An opening that introduces its protagonist in the tone of a storyteller's tale. It begins a whimsical fable that leads, at the far end of mastering the bow, to “the shooting that shoots not” — the forgetting of the bow itself.

Ah, the master — the master, unrivaled in archery through all ages — has forgotten the bow entirely? Ah, even the very name of the bow, and what it is for!

The closing, in which people marvel at the old master who has returned home having forgotten even the name and use of the bow. It depicts one who has reached the utmost extreme of mastery, with a touch of comedy.

The highest action is no action; the highest speech has no words; the highest shooting is not to shoot.

Ji Chang's answer when asked why he has come home without a bow. It shows a Taoist state in which the ultimate act is not to act, and the ultimate shot is not to shoot.

“I no longer know the distinction between self and other, nor the difference between right and wrong. My eyes seem like ears, my ears like a nose, my nose like a mouth.”

The reflection of the aged Ji Chang in his last years. Speaking of a state in which the distinctions of self and other, right and wrong, and even the senses have melted together, it depicts the master's culmination with a strange stillness.

Deshi

Zhong You, styled Zilu, a swaggering man of Bian in the state of Lu, took it into his head to humiliate a certain scholar of Zou, one Kong Qiu, whose reputation as a sage had lately grown loud.

It opens with the entrance of Zilu, who marches in to teach Confucius a lesson. Nakajima Atsushi's powerful work goes on to depict the life of a disciple who serves his master faithfully to the very end.

Their greatness was merely that they were useful for this or that. With Confucius it was entirely different. It was enough simply that there existed a human being named Confucius.

A passage depicting how Zilu, having met Confucius, is won over by the man. Zilu learns that there are people whose greatness lies not in their usefulness but in their very existence.

Of good men winning final victory—whatever may have happened in ancient times, in the present world one hardly even hears of such a thing. Why? Why?

A passage in which Zilu rages at a reality in which the righteous go unrewarded. His straightforward anger, demanding to know what Heaven is watching, lends deep shadow to this story of master and disciple.

Li Ling

In the ninth month of the autumn of the second year of Tianhan, under Emperor Wu of Han, the cavalry commandant Li Ling led five thousand foot soldiers north from the border fortress of Juyan.

A firm opening that traces the manner of a history book. This posthumous work of Nakajima Atsushi sets three men against one another: Li Ling, who surrendered to the Xiongnu; Su Wu, who kept his integrity; and Sima Qian, who wrote the Records of the Grand Historian.

If what lets him laugh off, unperturbed, unimaginable hardship, want, bitter cold, and solitude (and for all the long years until his death) is pride, then this pride must be called something truly fearsome and magnificent.

A passage in which Li Ling marvels at Su Wu, who herds sheep alone by the northern sea and keeps faith with Han. The contrast with himself, who surrendered to the Xiongnu, deepens Li Ling's anguish.
