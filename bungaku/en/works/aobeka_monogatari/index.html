About this work

A linked autobiographical sequence serialized in Bungei Shunjū in 1960 and 1961. Set in the fishing town of Urakasu — modelled on Urayasu in present-day Chiba, where the author lived as a young man — it renders the people of a town known for its shellfish, its seaweed and its fishing grounds as things the narrator saw and heard. From being sold a battered flat-bottomed boat, the Aobeka, to the boat inns at the steamer landing, the women of the cheap eating houses, and the fishermen and children living by their wits, it sets down in humour and warmth the human comedy of a poor town full of life.

Summary

Urakasu is a fishing town spread out at the mouth of the Nedo river. In this town, where the shellfish cannery and the seaweed-drying racks and the boat inns for the anglers and the cheap eating houses called gottakuya stand side by side, "I" have settled down for a while. One day the old man of a boat inn, Yoshi, talks me with great smoothness into buying a battered old flat-bottomed boat painted blue, with the bottom nearly out of it. This is the Aobeka, and from then on I go about among the lives of the townspeople almost as one of them. The boat-inn keeper and his wife, sharp as knives where money is concerned; Chō, a glib-tongued boy; the young men who gather at the steamer landing; the women of the gottakuya who live by selling themselves; the people who scratch a living on the great waste ground called the Million Tsubo Offshore — every one of them lives out his poverty with his cunning and his folly quite exposed, and yet somehow comically, and doggedly. "I" set their comedy and their sorrow down at once at arm's length and with deep affection. When "I" visit Urakasu again after thirty years, Chō, the boy of those days, has become the father of five children — and remembers almost nothing, either of the Aobeka or of me. A book the author himself calls a clearing-out of his notebooks: an elegy for a lost town and its people.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, who once settled down in the town of Urakasu. A young writer who suggests the author himself
  • Old Yoshi, the boat-inn keeper　Who talks "me" into buying the old flat-bottomed boat, the Aobeka
  • Chō　One of the boys who hang about the steamer landing. Quick and glib. Thirty years later he is the father of five children
  • Gorō-san and Tome-san　The fishermen and boatmen who live in the town, getting through their days by cunning and toughness

The opening (original text)

The town of Urakasu was a fishing town at the very bottom of the Nedo river, known for its shellfish, its seaweed and its fishing grounds. The town was not particularly large, but the cannery for shellfish, and the works where shells were burned to make lime, and the countless seaweed-drying racks that went up from winter into spring, and the boat inns for the customers who came to fish, and the great number of cheap eating houses called gottakuya, all gave it a character unlike other towns. The town stood by itself. To the north were fields, to the east the sea, to the west the Nedo river, and to the south spread the vast waste ground they called the Million Tsubo Offshore, and beyond that again was the sea.

Aobeka monogatari by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

From beginning to end I called Chō by his bare name, and Chō accepted it as the most natural thing in the world. Counting it up, it is a full thirty years since I left Urakasu. Chō is forty-one, and has five children, they say. When I call him "Chō" and he answers "Aye," there is no distance of thirty years in my heart. And yet he has no memory of me. I asked him about the Aobeka, but it seemed he remembered even that only very faintly, and he turned the talk aside, saying, "Well now, it's such a long time ago, isn't it!" And so what became of the Aobeka of my title must remain unknown to the end, and there I must finish this story.

Aobeka monogatari by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
