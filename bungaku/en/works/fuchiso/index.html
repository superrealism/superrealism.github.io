About this work

A central postwar work following Banshū Plain. Her husband Jūkichi comes home after a long imprisonment, and Hiroko has waited. Around a married life resumed after more than ten years, it looks quietly at the sense of release just after the defeat and at the subtle misalignment the separated years have put between the two. Beginning from a small scene of roasting sweet potatoes at a research institute in Musashino, it sings freshly the stirring of a new Japan.

Summary

In a corner of a research institute surrounded by the wooded groves of Musashino, people relax together, roasting thin-sliced sweet potatoes. In the autumn of defeat, the long age of oppression has finally come to an end, and Hiroko's husband Jūkichi, who endured over a decade in prison, has returned to her. Their married life begins again after the long years of waiting. But even amid the joy of reunion, Hiroko senses the subtle rift that the long years of separation have opened between them — a gap in their sense of daily life, in the very way they think. Even husband and wife, she quietly comes to accept, can find some differences in their personal way of living too deep to close. The streets overflow with gatherings that herald the sprouting of democracy, and Hiroko, watching a newsreel reporting the abolition of the Peace Preservation Law and the release of her comrades from prison, feels a chill run through her and weeps. The memory of the murdered Kobayashi Takiji, the vision of Jūkichi walking toward her from among the ranks of the liberated — turning over these many thoughts, she feels, with her whole body, the stirring of a new age. A fine work depicting the rebirth of a marriage and the innocent, hopeful start of postwar Japan, in a transparent lyricism.

Characters

  • Hiroko　The protagonist. Looks back on her life with her husband Jūkichi, who has returned after his long imprisonment, with both joy and a quiet bewilderment
  • Jūkichi　Hiroko's husband. Released from prison after enduring over a decade of imprisonment (modeled on the author's own husband, Miyamoto Kenji)

The opening (original text)

On the great experimental table lay countless test tubes, large and small, beakers with glass rods still thrust into them, flasks, and covered glass vessels arranged in order of size. A long, twisted glass pipe of some unknown name and purpose hung down from the shelf above. Late-October afternoon light fell upon the glassware, casting transparent shadows against one another. The research institute, built among the wooded groves of Musashino, was wrapped in the deep stillness of nature, and inside the laboratory the faint sound of gas burning could be heard. In a sunken corner, some kind of chemical was being boiled. At the end of the experimental table nearest the door stood a small electric hot plate. On it lay a scrap of wire netting, on which thin slices of sweet potato had been laid out. Around it were scattered teacups, a can of salmon more than half emptied, and homemade bread. In the quiet, clear air, the faint smell of roasting sweet potato began to drift.

The ending (original text)

As Hiroko watched, the scene before her eyes seemed to layer itself over and over, until the characters on the horizontal signboard she had seen hanging over the entrance of the Yoyogi office the day before seemed to float up before her. Written in bold, slightly cursive characters was "Japan Communist Party." It was the sort of lettering some construction-worker's boss might have painted himself on a great board with enthusiastic strokes — thick and heavy, sprawling, not the least bit elegant. And yet, seeing that great signboard catching the mild winter sun full on its face beneath the porch roof, one had the impression of a crude but sincere, big-bearded man laughing with joy. Looking up at it from the street, Hiroko found her own mouth curving involuntarily into a smile, and, as women of old used to do to stifle their laughter, she pressed the sleeve of her Genroku-style kimono to her mouth. Inside that entrance, many people had already been at work the day before. Not a few of them, like Jūkichi and his comrades, worked with their hair grown out in an interesting way. Even Jūkichi's once close-cropped chestnut-burr head had, by the time Hiroko touched it, grown out with the springy softness of a very young chestnut bur, responding to her palm. People with hair like that were bustling in and out of that building, busy with preparations for the Fourth Party Congress.
