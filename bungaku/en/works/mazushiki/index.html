About this work

The work that brought the seventeen-year-old Miyamoto Yuriko into the literary world as a girl prodigy. The narrator, the daughter of a wealthy family staying in a poor village in Fukushima, her grandmother's home country, sees the farmers there worn down by hunger, ignorance and illness, and burns with a pure wish to save them. But neither charity nor instruction reaches them, and her goodwill spins uselessly at every turn. A novella drawing, freshly and painfully, a young soul suffering from the gap between the ideal and the real.

Summary

"I," a girl raised in comfort in the city, stays in a poor mountain village in Fukushima, her grandmother's home region. There she sees poor farmers gripped by hunger, ignorance, sickness, and superstition — children who spend their days fighting over potatoes, the sick who die without ever seeing a doctor, a girl sold into service. My heart wounded, I, driven by my own innate passion, work desperately to save them and open their eyes. I give them money, read books aloud to them, and care for them with all my heart. But the villagers, even as they flock to my charity, never truly accept what is in my heart — instead they use me, and mock me behind my back. My goodwill never reaches them, and I suffer at my own inability to love them as fully as I want to. The death of Zemma-baka, the "Good Fool," who drowned himself in a marsh clutching a dog, seems to stand as a symbol of that bitter defeat. The author turns an unsparing eye on herself as well, showing both the arrogance born of her privileged birth and the young sincerity that, even so, cannot help but care for others. Published in Chūō Kōron by its seventeen-year-old author and hailed by Tsubouchi Shōyō and Shimamura Hōgetsu, this early masterpiece won Miyamoto Yuriko her literary reputation in a single stroke.

Characters

  • I　A girl raised in a wealthy city family. Stays in her grandmother's poor home village and burns with the ideal of saving its people, but suffers when her goodwill comes to nothing
  • Zemma-baka ("Good Fool")　A young man mocked by the villagers as a simpleton. Drowns himself in a marsh clutching a dog
  • The villagers　Poor farmers living amid hunger, ignorance, and superstition. They flock to "my" charity but never accept what is in my heart

The opening (original text)

Along the road running north and south through the village stands a single farmhouse. Inside, in a house so filthy it might better be called some creature's den than a human dwelling, it is very dark, for there are few windows. On the earthen floor, some three tsubo in size, all manner of household clutter lies scattered about, and from the roost above the beam, hot with the day's heat, comes the sound of a hen clucking cluck-cluck-cluck as she sits on her nest. On the rungs of the round pole-ladder hanging by the wall, meant for the chickens, white and yellow with droppings and molted feathers, a thin rooster has perched for a moment, keeping watch over the hen above. Amid all this squalor, stench, and poverty, three boys have gathered around the hearth, waiting, wearily, for their food to finish cooking.

Mazushiki hitobito no mure by Miyamoto Yuriko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

How glad, how glad my dear sun, who raised me and loves me so, must be saying now! That good, kind sun. ... Zemma-baka's body was found that night. He had drowned in the marsh at the edge of the neighboring village, clutching a dog. They say a long procession of tiny shrimp kept passing in and out through his long, streaming hair.

Mazushiki hitobito no mure by Miyamoto Yuriko (Aozora Bunko)
