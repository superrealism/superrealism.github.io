About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Fuji in 1940. Samori Shinkurō, an elder of the Akita domain, is obstinate and full of awkward corners, and forgetful beyond any ordinary measure, so that they call him Forgetful Samori. A humorous piece drawing one bracing scene in which a senior retainer full of such faults shows his peculiar eye for people.

Summary

Samori Shinkurō is a retainer of the Akita domain. With a stipend of a little over eight hundred koku, his family, of long standing in the Satake house, held weight for generations as elders. The office of elder was something like a councillor, auditing the domain's administration and, at times, even vetoing his lord's own orders — an important post, and a much-resented one. Shinkurō succeeded to the headship of his house at twenty-five, and for five years now had held the foremost seat among the elders, handling that difficult office with a sharp mind. Not that he was an even-tempered, all-round brilliant man, however. He was obstinate and full of awkward corners, and, worse still, forgetful beyond any ordinary measure — so bad that people nicknamed him Forgetful Samori for it. And yet the fact that he had performed his important office so well must have come from some peculiar virtue in his character, one that made even his own flaws work to his advantage. A senior retainer full of such faults as this, and yet second to no one in his eye for people. At the close of the story, Shinkurō, grinning broadly and squaring his shoulders, declares: "Never once has a man I've marked out as such-and-such proved me wrong. I'm no ordinary judge of these things, you know." With a great glare of his large eyes — that was the scene Samori Shinkurō was proudest of, in all his career. A piece of Yamamoto's, drawing with humour the rousing eye for people held by a senior retainer riddled with flaws.

The opening (original text)

Samori Shinkurō is a retainer of the Akita domain. His stipend was a little over eight hundred koku, but his family, of long standing in the Satake house, held weight for generations as elders. The office of elder was something like a councillor — an undemanding office in itself, but an important post to which only senior retainers were chosen.
