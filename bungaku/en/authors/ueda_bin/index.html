Biography

Born in Tsukiji, Tokyo, in 1874, into a family descended from former shogunate retainers; his pen name was Ryūson. Hailed as a language prodigy from childhood, he took part in founding the magazine "Teikoku Bungaku" while studying English literature at Tokyo Imperial University, and his English ability was highly praised by his lecturer, Lafcadio Hearn (Koizumi Yakumo). After graduating he was active in criticism and translation, contributing to "Myōjō" and other magazines and being among the first to introduce fin-de-siècle European literature and French Symbolist poetry to Japan. In 1905 he published the translated-poetry collection "Kaichōon" ("The Sound of the Tide"), dedicated to his close friend Mori Ōgai, and with famous translations such as "Yama no Anata" ("Over the Mountains") and "Ochiba" ("Fallen Leaves") shaped the course of modern Japanese poetry. From 1908 he taught as a professor at Kyoto Imperial University, and, as an advisor to Keio University's literature department, recommended Nagai Kafū as a professor together with Mori Ōgai, helping to launch "Mita Bungaku." He also wrote the novel "Uzumaki" ("The Whirlpool") and numerous critical works, but died suddenly in 1916 of uremia at the age of 41. After his death, a second collection of translated poems drawn from his papers, "Bokuyōshin" ("The Pastoral Muse"), was compiled.
