About this work

Uchida Roan, who knew The Eight Dog Chronicles of Nansō Satomi from childhood, discusses Bakin's great labour, twenty-eight years in the making and completed by dictation after he went blind. He ranges freely over the structure and ending of the work, the clumsiness of the account of the battle between the two deputies, anachronisms such as the firearms, and other questions of history and geography, as well as the difficult character of Bakin as it appears in his diary, and closes with twenty-four Chinese poems by Hashimoto Yōtō as notes on the characters. A study of the work with a great deal of flavour.

Summary

A study in which Uchida Roan discusses Kyokutei Bakin's Nansō Satomi hakkenden from six angles. He begins with his own reading — he grew up in a house where his great-grandfather on his mother's side had copied out Bakin's works and treasured them, and from childhood he knew the illustrations well and had the names of the eight dog warriors by heart — and praises as unmatched in any age the great steadiness with which Bakin carried the work through in a hundred and six volumes over twenty-eight years, exactly as he had planned it at the outset. He describes in particular the bitter labor with which, even after he lost his sight, Bakin went on dictating without the least slackening of spirit, and introduces the manuscript preserved at Waseda University, saying that it truly deserves respect. In his general judgment he holds that the Hakkenden ought properly to end at the hundred and seventy-first chapter, where the eight dogs are complete, and that the account of the battles with the two deputies is a following of the Three Kingdoms and the Water Margin and the poorest part of it; he finds human feeling in Dōsetsu and in Funamushi, and points out that Bakin was better at drawing rogues and wanton women than loyal retainers and filial sons. In the chapter on history and geography he takes up such anachronisms as country samurai of the Chōroku era carrying muskets, and the handling of Edo castle, and warns against the partiality that overpraises Bakin's scholarship. Further, from Bakin's diary he draws the difficult, crotchety man who fussed without end over the smallest household matter, and his quarrels with Kyōden and Kyōzan; and even so he holds it a settled judgment beyond dispute that as a writer Bakin is the greatest Japan has produced. At the last he gives, as a key to the characters, a selection of the twenty-four regulated verses in which Hashimoto Yōtō sang the people of the Hakkenden, and so closes his remarks.

Characters

  • Kyokutei Bakin　Takizawa Bakin. He wrote the Nansō Satomi hakkenden over twenty-eight years and completed it by dictation after he lost his sight. He was known too as a difficult and crotchety man
  • Santō Kyōden　The senior writer on whom Bakin relied in his youth, and with whom he later came to compete
  • Hashimoto Yōtō　The author of the twenty-four regulated verses on the characters of the Hakkenden
  • Uchida Roan　The author, familiar with the Hakkenden from childhood

The opening (original text)

In old times people were not so busy as now, and everyone seems to have had some leisure. What we call popular works were read and were in fashion enough; but since living was frugal and there was some leisure, people borrowed rather than bought, and copied out or made extracts from what they found interesting. In a house with an old person in it especially, copying was one of the occupations of retirement; so that although by now they have mostly been thrown away, in my youth almost any old house had copies made by a grandfather or a great-grandfather. In the house of my great-grandfather on my mother's side there were Bakin's Bishōnenroku and Gyokuseki dōjikun and Asaina shimameguri no ki and Kyōkakuden. In the end there is nothing to say but that it overflowed from a devoted love of Bakin.

Hakkenden dan'yo by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The twenty-four regulated verses that the old Hashimoto Yōtō once made on these characters as his subject amount to a key to the persons of the Hakkenden. I give a selection of them as a synopsis with which to close a famous work. The twenty-four above are the finest pieces in the Yōtō shū. Among lovers of Chinese verse there are some who know them by heart; but since few among lovers of fiction, and especially among the devotees of Bakin, know them, I give them here in selection and so close these remarks.

Hakkenden dan'yo by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
