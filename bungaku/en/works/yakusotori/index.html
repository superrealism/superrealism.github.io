About this work

A fantastical novel set on Mount Iō in Kaga, serialized in the Niroku Shinpō in May 1903. A medical student who has gone into the mountains for medicinal plants for a sick person tells the flower-seller who falls in with him on the road about a strange experience of his childhood: how, going after a flower that shone red in the moonlight, he was carried off by bandits and saved by a beautiful girl the robbers kept. With "The Holy Man of Mount Kōya," a highly regarded mountain fantasy in Kyōka's work.

Summary

In early summer, chanting a sutra as he pushes his way into Mount Iō, Kōsaka is hailed from behind: "If I may be so bold, let me offer you alms." He turns to find a flower-seller woman in a Kaga sedge hat, a basket of flowers on her back. As she guides him along the forbidden mountain path, Kōsaka begins to tell the story of his past. Twenty years before, when his mother had fallen gravely ill, the boy he then was became convinced that if he could only put a flower glowing red in the moonlight into his mother's hair, her sickness would be cured, and he slipped from the house and set out for the mountain. He crossed a ferry crossing, was soaked through by rain, and at last came to a bandits' den. A beautiful girl, taking pity on him, drove off a demon that barred the way with a sickle in her hand, led him on to Bijo-ga-hara, and there at last picked the red flower for him. But that girl was— Now, once again in need of that same medicine, Kōsaka has come back to the mountain.

Characters

  • Kōsaka　A medical student who has gone into Mount Iō to gather medicinal herbs. Tells the flower-seller woman of an experience from his childhood
  • The flower-seller woman　A mysterious woman who calls out to Kōsaka on the mountain and accompanies him as far as Bijo-ga-hara
  • The girl　A woman who saved the young Kōsaka, drove off a demon with a sickle, and picked the red flower for him
  • Kōsaka's mother　A woman who fell ill and was once cured by that flower

The opening (original text)

"If I may be so bold, let me offer you alms." At the gentle voice calling from behind him, Kōsaka, who had just come out from beneath the dense, tangled trees on the mountainside of Mount Iō into what felt suddenly like daybreak — the sky clear, the air pure, the summer just beginning yet the sun hanging over the far peaks ahead like an autumn moon seen by day — turned with a start. He had never dreamed, so far had he come from any village, already deep into the mountains, that he would hear a human voice, let alone see a human form. And yet it was a woman who had called to him.

The ending (original text)

"Ah, how dear. Whoever it is you love and worry for — their sickness will surely be cured by this." Alas, Kōsaka's tightly closed hand had only closed on the empty stem; his sleeve swept through air, and though Bijo-ga-hara remained in full bloom about him, the girl seemed to sway and drift away before him, and her form grew distant. He rose to give chase, but where the peonies bloomed thick upon the rock, rising toward a peak like the great white head of an elephant, vanishing into cloud, he caught one last glimpse of her clear, fine brows — and then there was only the moon over a windless field. Kōsaka sat down where he was. And so, keeping that one red blossom pressed to his breast as a token, he laid his sutra upon the peony beside him, its bloom a full foot across, and through the whole night, hands pressed together, chanted the chapter of the Medicine King.
