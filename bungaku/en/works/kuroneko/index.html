About this work

Sasaki Naojirō's translation of Edgar Allan Poe's 1843 tale of terror "The Black Cat," from his prewar complete Poe published by Daiichi Shobō. On the eve of his execution, the narrator confesses flatly what became of him: how drink changed him, how a motiveless malice grew toward the black cat he had loved, and how it ended in the killing of his wife. Built on Poe's theme of perverseness — doing wrong precisely because one knows it is wrong — and one of the central works of his weird fiction.

Summary

The narrator, who from childhood loved animals and was gentle and tender by nature, keeps many creatures with a wife of like mind, and of them all he loves best a large, clever black cat named Pluto. But year by year he gives himself up to drink; his temper grows coarse and short, and he begins to use violence on his wife and on the animals. One night, coming home dead drunk, he flies into a rage at the cat for avoiding him and cuts out one of its eyes with a penknife. When the wound has healed and he sees the animal flee from him in fear, he is seized by the spirit of perverseness — doing a thing precisely because he knows he must not — and one morning he hangs the cat from the branch of a tree. That night the house burns to the ground, and on the one wall left standing there appears the figure of a cat with a rope about its neck. Later, in a tavern, he finds a one-eyed black cat, the very image of Pluto but with a patch of white on its breast, and brings it home. His wife makes much of the creature, but he comes to loathe it, and as the white patch takes on the shape of a gallows his terror and hatred grow together. One day, going down to the cellar, he stumbles over the cat winding about his feet and in his fury raises an axe. His wife catches his arm to stop him, and with that axe he strikes her dead on the spot. He hides the body by walling it up in the brickwork of the cellar; the cat too has vanished, and for some days he sleeps in peace. On the fourth day the police come to search the house and find nothing. In his triumph he raps hard with his cane on the very wall behind which his wife stands entombed — and from within the wall a cry answers him that is like nothing of this world. When the men pull the wall down, there upon the head of the rotting corpse sits the black cat, its red mouth open and its one eye burning. He had walled the animal up along with his wife.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, once a gentle lover of animals, who takes to drink and turns on his wife and his cat
  • Pluto　The large black cat the narrator doted on. Its eye is cut out, and it is hanged
  • The second black cat　A one-eyed cat resembling Pluto, with a patch of white on its breast
  • The wife　A good-natured companion, who loses her life trying to stop him

The opening (original text)

For the most wild, yet most homely narrative which I am about to pen, I neither expect nor solicit belief. Mad indeed would I be to expect it, in a case where my very senses reject their own evidence. Yet, mad am I not — and very surely do I not dream. But to-morrow I die, and to-day I would unburthen my soul. My immediate purpose is to place before the world, plainly, succinctly, and without comment, a series of mere household events. In their consequences, these events have terrified — have tortured — have destroyed me. Yet I will not attempt to expound them. To me, they have presented little but Horror — to many they will seem less terrible than baroques. Hereafter, perhaps, some intellect may be found which will reduce my phantasm to the common-place — some intellect more calm, more logical, and far less excitable than my own, which will perceive, in the circumstances I detail with awe, nothing more than an ordinary succession of very natural causes and effects.

The Black Cat by Sasaki Naojirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Of my own thoughts it is folly to speak. Swooning, I staggered to the opposite wall. For one instant the party upon the stairs remained motionless, through extremity of terror and of awe. In the next, a dozen stout arms were toiling at the wall. It fell bodily. The corpse, already greatly decayed and clotted with gore, stood erect before the eyes of the spectators. Upon its head, with red extended mouth and solitary eye of fire, sat the hideous beast whose craft had seduced me into murder, and whose informing voice had consigned me to the hangman. I had walled the monster up within the tomb!

The Black Cat by Sasaki Naojirō (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Who has not, a hundred times, found himself committing a vile or a silly action, for no other reason than because he knows he should not?

A passage in which the narrator discusses the spirit of perverseness as the cause of his own ruin. Pinning down the human impulse to do something precisely because it must not be done, it shows the heart of Poe's psychological horror.
