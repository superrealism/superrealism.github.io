About this work

Two months after Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's suicide in July 1927, his close friend Kikuchi Kan wrote this memorial piece for Bungei Shunjū. He dismisses the speculation about the cause and records coolly that illness and worldly vexations must have made his pessimism practical; and at the same time he writes that they had not met in the last month, and that the light in Akutagawa's eyes as he glanced at him in front of the Hyōtei restaurant would be a source of regret for the rest of his life. Candour as a friend and an explanation to the public as an editor stand together: a first-rate contemporary witness.

Summary

Of Akutagawa's death, it seems that one could write all sorts of things, and yet when one begins nothing can be written — and having begun so, Kikuchi first speaks of the cause of death. There is no concrete cause that would satisfy the world; the chief cause must be the "vague uneasiness" of which Akutagawa himself spoke. To that were added the bodily exhaustion and nervous prostration of two or three years, and troublesome worldly worries. There was, for instance, the affair of the Modern Japanese Literary Reader. This book, which the painstaking Akutagawa edited with the work of a hundred and twenty or thirty writers in it, did not sell, and the royalties, once divided, left him almost nothing; and yet a groundless story arose that he had made money by it and built his study with it, and there were even writers who complained. Though Kikuchi told him again and again not to mind it, in the end Akutagawa could not rest until he had sent every writer included a ten-yen gift voucher. That scrupulousness saddened Kikuchi. ... The conjectures about the woman who appears in his manuscript are a profanation of the dead, Kikuchi declares, and in the will addressed to himself there is nothing but a farewell. A friendship in which, though their tastes and their natures were entirely opposite, no distance of feeling arose in ten years. That he, known for "Kikuchi's express letters," never once sent an express letter to Akutagawa alone. And that Akutagawa, just before his death, twice visited the offices of Bungei Shunjū and sat vacantly in the reception room, as he learned afterward. If only he had gone about with him more and stirred his power of living — but it is all too late. In closing Kikuchi rates Akutagawa as the representative writer of a Japan in transition, uniting in one person the learning of Japan, China and the West, and declares that the column "Words of a Dwarf" will be kept in the magazine.

Characters

The opening (original text)

Of Akutagawa's death, all sorts of things seem as if they could be written, and yet when I begin to write, nothing can be written. As to the cause of death, not even we know it clearly. It is not that we do not know; in the end it would be truest to say that there is no concrete cause sufficient to satisfy the world. In the end the chief cause must be, as Akutagawa himself said, a "vague uneasiness."

Akutagawa no kotodomo by Kikuchi Kan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Let me add a word. In a passage of his latest writing there was a line: "I beg the forgiveness of all men, and would forgive all men." If there is anyone in the literary world, or elsewhere, who bore any reserve toward the dead, I hope that he will take in this feeling of his and let his mind be cleared of it now.

Akutagawa no kotodomo by Kikuchi Kan (Aozora Bunko)
