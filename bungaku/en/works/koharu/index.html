About this work

On a fine day in November, shut in his study rereading Wordsworth, the narrator is carried by a blue line he once drew in its pages back to the days seven years before when he steeped himself in nature at Saiki in Bungo. Walking in the suburbs with Koyama, a young man who wants to be a painter and has come to call, he wonders whether, if a life were likened to the seasons, his own is not this small spring — a short story in which Kunikida Doppo sets the lost heat of youth back into the quiet light of autumn.

Summary

On a day in November "I" had shut myself in my study since morning, reading Wordsworth. The volume, which I got eight years ago, has lain gathering dust in a corner of the shelf these last year or two, and the lines and passages that once pierced my breast have lost their power to move me now. To myself saying proudly, "I have grown ripe," the book holds up the line: "Men descend into the vale of years." ... Reading the poems over again in the clear air of an Indian summer, I come upon the piece that begins "Five years have past," the poem of Tintern Abbey, and murmur without meaning to, "Seven years have past." The time I read Wordsworth most eagerly was the year I spent as a country schoolmaster at Saiki in Bungo. Days in which I was less a teacher than a pupil learning nature at poetry's hand. While I am taking out the diary of those days and reading here and there, Koyama, a young man from my own province who means to be a painter, comes to call. Going out with him into the suburbs to sketch, we find the sumac leaves flashing red in a wind not of the shore but of Musashino, and the range of hills at the border of the province faintly visible. Watching Koyama through a gap in the trees as he begins to draw a half-broken bridge, and giving myself up to the rustling of the withered oak leaves, I feel as if I had touched again the music that used to sound in my breast until three or four years ago. And at that moment Koyama, still facing away, asks suddenly: if a man's life were likened to the four seasons, about what age would the Indian summer be?

Characters

  • I　A man who loves Wordsworth and calls himself "the ripened master"
  • Koyama　A young man from my own province who means to be a painter, and calls me "elder brother"

The opening (original text)

On a day in November I had shut myself up in my study since morning, reading. The book was Wordsworth's poems; for this one volume has no ordinary connection with me. It was eight years ago that I got it — I have not forgotten, on the night of the twenty-first of September. Ah, eight years! Remembering, it is like a dream. And particularly in the last year or two even this volume has been badly neglected, even among the mere twenty or thirty books that make up my library, and left in the dustiest corner of the shelf.

Koharu by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"One like me, I suppose, is the Indian summer!" I answered without thinking; and unawares a sorrow such as I had never known rose and struck at my breast. "If you are spring, then I am the Indian summer, the Indian summer; winter will be here before long!" "Ha, ha, ha, ha — but when winter has gone it will be spring again," answered Koyama lightly enough. All about us became quiet again. Koyama draws on, whistling. And I thought: are not these two, rather, a subject with meaning in it for a picture?

Koharu by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)
