What is Genbun'itchi (Unification of Spoken and Written Language)?

Category
Style & Technique

The single greatest stylistic reform in modern Japanese literature: the attempt to unify the written language (bungotai) and the spoken language (kōgotai) and establish a new prose style that wrote as one spoke. Out of a recognition that the classical and Sino-Japanese written styles inherited from the Edo period could not readily capture the subtleties of modern psychology, Futabatei Shimei, prompted by Tsubouchi Shōyō's theoretical suggestions, attempted a colloquial style ending in "da" in "Ukigumo" ("Drifting Clouds," 1887), and Yamada Bimyō likewise experimented with a style ending in "desu" in "Musashino" and other works. At first the style was criticized as unnatural, and because Ozaki Kōyō and other Ken'yūsha writers won popularity with the mixed classical-colloquial style instead, its adoption was slow, but after the First Sino-Japanese War it spread into newspaper and magazine prose, and once Naturalist literature of the late 1900s adopted the "da/de aru" style as its standard, it became fully entrenched. Almost without exception, the modern novel thereafter has this style as its foundation, and it transformed the Japanese written language itself.
