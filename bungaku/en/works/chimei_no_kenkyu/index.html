About this work

The summation of thirty years of study of place names by Yanagita Kunio, the founder of Japanese folklore studies. From the distribution of unremarkable names such as Tashiro and Karuizawa he reads the trades, the beliefs and the movements of the people who lived there. A book that tried to reconstruct the history of the Japanese islands from the names of places, on the view that a place name is the oldest fossil of language.

Summary

"The first time I ever made an issue of Japanese place names was over Tashiro and Karuizawa, both of which appear within this very book" — so Yanagita writes in his own preface, revealing that the whole undertaking began with his puzzlement over why the place name Tashiro turns up, wherever one goes, deep in the mountains. That was, by his own reckoning, already a full thirty years before. The work had remained so minute and fussy a task, he notes, that few scholars had ever cared to take it up. This volume gathers essays — "On Place Names," "Place Names and Geography," "Place Names and History," "Studies in Place Names" — built on a single, clear premise: place names are not given by chance, but are records of how the people who once lived there used the land, what they feared, and what they worshipped. Trace the nationwide distribution of place names sharing a single root word, then, and both the word's meaning and the lives of those who coined it come into view. Yanagita follows out countless such words — sori and kano, meaning burned-over swidden fields; ya and yachi, marking wetlands; tawa and oroshi, naming mountain passes and the routes across them — each traced through examples drawn from every corner of the country. Beyond mere matters of elevation or water access, place names open windows onto the history of manorial estates and local stewards, the migrations of hunters and wanderers, even the traces of sites where the gods once were worshipped. Near the close of the book, Yanagita traces how place names containing the word "arrow" have been explained region by region, and shows that the custom of hanging arrows on trees or stones to honor the gods and pray for military fortune was, in truth, nothing other than a prayer for the safety of one's own domain.

The opening (original text)

Author's Preface The first time I ever made an issue of Japanese place names was over Tashiro and Karuizawa, both of which appear within this very book. It began with my sense that there must be some reason why Tashiro, wherever one goes, turns up only deep in the mountains. Counting the years, it has already been a good deal more than thirty since then.

Chimei no kenkyū by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

To honor the gods with arrows and pray for military fortune was likewise, surely, a prayer for the safety of one's domain. In the medieval warrior houses, "military fortune" meant nothing other than the fortune of the house itself; in Edo, one would call it the safety of one's own quarter of town. That the arrow-festival is spoken of as an especially refined custom is merely the private opinion of antiquarians. In both China and Japan, wealth, rank, and peace of health were truly the great concerns of ordinary people, and in this one respect alone there is no distinction to be found across time or place.

Chimei no kenkyū by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)
