About this work

A historical novel by Sakaguchi Ango, published in the first issue of "Kikan Sakuhin" in 1948. Unfinished. It opens with the arrival at Kiyosu of an envoy carrying an imperial rescript, and draws Nobunaga at thirty-four, grown up wild as the boss of a pack of stray dogs. There is the curiosity that sent him into the water in nothing but a loincloth to look for the great serpent of Hebi-ike, and the caution that made him change to long spears and then to muskets. At the root of it Ango sees fear and "a soul that does science," and calls Nobunaga a devil. Around a line from a little song Nobunaga liked — "death is certain" — the piece lights up this man's life from the point at which neither the realm nor ambition is anything at all.

Summary

When Tateiri Sakyōnosuke came to Kiyosu bearing two imperial rescripts and a lady-in-waiting's letter, Nobunaga was out hawking. The court was desperately poor; there was money neither for the crown prince's coming-of-age nor for the repair of the palace. Fearing the glare of that old viper Matsunaga Danjō, Madenokōji, the major counselor, wrote out the rescript muttering all the while about how awkward it was. In substance it amounted to a request for a loan, but it also meant that Nobunaga's power had been recognized as worth asking. He was in the highest spirits, had geese and cranes taken for the envoy, and feasted him. He was thirty-four at the time — a gang captain who listened to nobody's orders and had cobbled himself together in his own way. At about twenty-eight, presented with a hawk, he had said blithely that he would keep it until he took the realm. Yet he also had the caution of a boy who saw the disadvantage of the short spear and had spears three and a half ken long made instead, and who then changed his main weapon to the musket. Behind that lay fear. In Nobunaga going into the water himself to see the great serpent of Hebi-ike, Ango finds the rarest thing of all, "a soul that does science." It would have been all one to him to be eaten by the serpent and die. Strip off a layer and death is certain, and that is all there is to it — and to a man who can say so flatly, unifying the realm and ambition alike are nothing. Before long Nobunaga sweeps into Kyoto with Ashikaga Yoshiaki, and forms an unaccountable friendship with the old viper Matsunaga Danjō.

Characters

  • Oda Nobunaga　Lord of Kiyosu. A gang captain grown up wild, who chose the long spear and the musket precisely because he knew fear
  • Matsunaga Danjō Hisahide　An old viper, neither snake nor goblin. He holds power over the realm, but is no great soldier
  • Tateiri Sakyōnosuke　The envoy from the court, who comes to Kiyosu with two rescripts and a lady-in-waiting's letter
  • Takeda Shingen　A man who judged Nobunaga rightly, and yet could not understand what lay at the bottom of his curiosity
  • Ashikaga Yoshiaki　Brought up to the capital under Nobunaga's escort, he takes the office of shogun

The opening (original text)

Death is certain; what shall I leave for remembrance? Surely they will talk of me. — a little song Nobunaga liked — When Tateiri Sakyōnosuke came to see Nobunaga bearing two imperial rescripts and a lady-in-waiting's letter, Nobunaga was out hawking. The envoys from the court were only two, Isogai Shin'emon Hisatsugu as guide and Tateiri as envoy, and their ostensible business was a pilgrimage to the Atsuta Shrine.

Oda Nobunaga by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Not only was the old viper's life spared: he was left his main castle at Shigi, and the whole province of Yamato was given over to him to take as he could. It was a friendship between devils. The old viper came at once to pay his thanks, and most earnestly, pouring out all his accumulated learning, gave advice on this and that in the way of politics. And yet this unaccountable friendship must be called a very clean thing. It is a riddle that human beings cannot understand. How, then, did this friendship grow, and how did it come at last to break? (Unfinished)
