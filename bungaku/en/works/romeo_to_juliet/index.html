About this work

Tsubouchi Shōyō's translation of Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet, published in October 1933 as a volume of his revised complete Shakespeare. It is known for a classical cadence that recalls the delivery of kabuki. Shōyō, who did so much to establish modern Japanese theatre and letters, completed the whole of Shakespeare single-handed in his later years, and this translation is part of the work that laid the ground for how Shakespeare is read in Japanese.

Summary

In the Italian city of Verona, two great houses, the Montagues and the Capulets, have gone on fighting over an ancient grudge. Romeo, the Montague son, slips into a masked ball and there meets Juliet, the Capulet daughter, and the two fall at once in love. Knowing the enmity of their houses and yet unable to hold down what they feel, they are married in secret with Friar Laurence's help. Just then Romeo's friend Mercutio is killed in a quarrel with Juliet's cousin Tybalt, and Romeo in his anger kills Tybalt. Sentenced to banishment for this, Romeo spends one night with Juliet and escapes to Mantua. To avoid the marriage her father presses on her, Juliet takes, on Friar Laurence's advice, a draught that will make her seem dead; but the letter that would tell Romeo of the plan never reaches him. Believing her death to be real, Romeo hurries with a poison to the tomb where Juliet lies, and beside his sleeping bride drinks it and dies. Waking to find Romeo already dead, Juliet takes her own life on the spot. Before the bodies of the two, the houses at last put aside the grudge of years and are reconciled.

Characters

  • Romeo　The Montague son. The protagonist, who meets Juliet at the masked ball and falls in love
  • Juliet　The Capulet daughter, who plights herself to Romeo and follows a tragic fate
  • Friar Laurence　The Franciscan who marries the two
  • Mercutio　Romeo's friend, killed in the quarrel with Tybalt
  • Tybalt　Lady Capulet's nephew, who kills Mercutio and is killed by Romeo

The opening (original text)

Enter Chorus. Chorus. Two households, both alike in dignity, In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean. From forth the fatal loins of these two foes A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life; Whose misadventured piteous overthrows Do with their death bury their parents' strife. The fearful passage of their death-mark'd love, And the continuance of their parents' rage, Which, but their children's end, nought could remove, Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage; The which if you with patient ears attend, What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend. Exit.

The ending (original text)

Prince. A glooming peace this morning with it brings; The sun for sorrow will not show his head. Go hence, to have more talk of these sad things; Some shall be pardon'd, and some punished: For never was a story of more woe Than this of Juliet and her Romeo.

Famous passages

O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo? Deny thy father and refuse thy name; Or, if thou wilt not, be but sworn my love, And I'll no longer be a Capulet.

Juliet's speech in Act Two, the balcony scene, as she reveals her heart alone. The famous lines that distill the tragedy of love between enemy houses into a single point — a name — can be savored here in Tsubouchi Shōyō's old-fashioned translation.

What's in a name? that which we call a rose By any other name would smell as sweet;

The continuation of the same scene. Declaring that a name does not change the nature of a thing, Juliet expresses, through the memorable image of the rose, her wish to be united with Romeo even if he must cast off his family's name.
