About this work

A novella published in Bungakukai in 1937. Fukuichi, the son of a goldfish seller, pours his hopeless love for Masako, a girl of good family he adored as a child, into a single object: to create the most beautiful goldfish in the world. Educated in the city, he returns nonetheless to the muddy ponds of his birthplace and burns year after year at breeding toward his ideal. The passion of an unrewarded love raised into a craving for aesthetic creation, drawn in lustrous colour: a central work of Okamoto Kanoko's sensual hymn to life.

Summary

Fukuichi, born into a house that lives by raising goldfish, had as a child a faint love for Masako, the daughter of the landowning family. But the difference in station forbids him even to speak of it. Sent to the city to study on the support of Masako's father, he comes back in time to the muddy ponds of Tanikubo at home and gives himself to the breeding of goldfish. What he is after is not a fish that will sell. To make with his own hands one supreme fish, unmatched and unrepeatable anywhere in the world — that becomes the obsession of his life. Masako marries, and appears before him again, grown into a beautiful woman; but Fukuichi's love no longer turns toward any living woman, and all of that unfulfilled longing is poured into the craving to bring forth an ideal fish. Year after year he sorts through the numberless fry that hatch, is disappointed, and cannot give it up, and goes on with his breeding. The passion of an unreturned love and the artist's compulsion to create beauty melt into one and drive him to a pursuit close to madness. And at the end of long years he finds on the surface of the muddy pond one fish more strange and more sumptuous than anything he had imagined. Before that beautiful goldfish, moving at its ease with a train of others behind it, Fukuichi sinks down into the mud in joy and stupefaction. In lustrous color and sensuous prose, Kanoko draws a hymn to life and to the craving to create.

The opening (original text)

Today too Fukuichi scooped up one and then another of the fry that had at last begun to change color, put them in a dish and studied them intently through a magnifying glass — and then: a failure again this year. Again this year the goldfish he wants will not be produced after all. Muttering it, he flung the dish and the glass out onto the veranda and dropped flat on his back with no expression at all. The new green of this hollow of Tanikubo, seen from the veranda, was at its height. With a gaudiness one could hardly call mere foliage, the treetops were shaking out a five-colored dyeing of young leaves, based on the new green and running from tea-red to something almost purple. When it stirred in the wind, the smooth red clay face of the cliff flashed back like a gold screen.

Kingyo ryōran by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Ah — it is like neither Masako nor the Picture of the Divine Fish Garland.... More than that... more than that... it is a more beautiful goldfish. A goldfish!" Disappointment? No — something beyond joy. Overcome, Fukuichi's body sank down and down into the mud at the edge of the pond. And on the water before him, while he went on breathing from the shoulders with his eyes shut, the beautiful fish he had just discovered, like a new star, moved at its ease with a train of lesser goldfish behind it, its breast thrown out, its rich and gorgeous tail fins glittering in the sunlight in a riot of splendor.
