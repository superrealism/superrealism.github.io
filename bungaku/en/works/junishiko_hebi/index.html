About this work

The piece for the year of the snake — the folklore and legends of the serpent — serialized in four parts in Taiyō in 1917. It begins by examining how twelve animals came to be assigned to the twelve branches, and ranges over the mizuchi as master of the waters, the relation to the kappa, and beliefs the world over that treat the snake as a divine beast. Appended is the later essay "On the Evil Eye," which discusses how widely, East and West, the eye has been held to have magical power.

Summary

It is said that the assignment of living creatures to the twelve branches first appears in Wang Chong's Lunheng; but there are instances of the sheep being assigned to the eighth branch in the Huainanzi and in the commentaries on the Zhuangzi as well, so that its origin is old, Kumagusu explains. Was the assignment of the snake to the sixth branch likewise practiced before the Former Han? Snakes are fond of going near water and into it. Even the snakes of the desert swim well, and the hooded snake of India goes into wells and follows ships far out to sea. Therefore the water monsters of which many countries speak are for the most part snakes living in or beside the water. In Japan too a snake living by the water and feared by men was called mizuchi, meaning "master of the water," and written with the character for the water dragon. The mizushi, medochi and mintsuchi of today are the kappa; but originally, he reasons, it was snakes that took human shape and did violence that were called mizuchi, and in later ages the name was taken from them by the kappa, which is a transformation of the same thing. Of snakes now living there are more than sixteen hundred species, inhabiting the fields and forests, marshes, lakes and seas of the whole world save the frigid zones and a few islands; some carry a violent venom and some swallow their prey whole, and from of old they have been deeply bound up with the history of mankind. Therefore the folklore and legend of snakes is inexhaustible. Kumagusu sets up his headings — the name, the habitat, the habits, the beliefs, the tales — and gathers up a part of it.

The opening (original text)

In the five hundred and thirty-first book of the Kokon yōrankō it is argued that "although the assignment of living creatures to the twelve branches first appears in Wang Chong's Lunheng, the Huainanzi says that what is called the master of the day of the eighth branch in the mountains is the sheep, and the Shiwen glosses the Zhuangzi's phrase 'never having pastured, and yet born in the southwest corner' as the region of the eighth branch, so that the assigning of the sheep to that branch is also of old origin."

Jūnishikō (hebi) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Addendum. In the preceding section I gave, as the earliest instance in which the phrase "sidelong looking" appears, the note of Li Shan in the Wenxuan, written some twelve hundred and seventy years ago in the Xianqing era of the Tang, where it says that the cock is fond of looking sidelong. Searching afterward, I found something at least five hundred and twenty years older, in Zhang Pingzi's Xijing fu of the Later Han.... This "sidelong glaring" means, like "sidelong looking," the evil eye, and is probably one of the oldest instances of a word expressing the evil eye in China.

Jūnishikō (hebi) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
