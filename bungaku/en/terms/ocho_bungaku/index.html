What is Ōhō Bungaku (Court Literature)?

Category
Historical Period

A general term for the literature that flowered in the Heian period (794 to the end of the twelfth century), centered on the imperial court, where the aristocracy held the center of politics and culture. Also called "chūko bungaku" ("middle antiquity literature"). The invention of kana, a cursive simplification of Chinese characters, made it possible to set down the delicate feelings of the Japanese heart freely in writing, and court literature came to full ripeness above all in the hands of women serving at court. In tale literature, following the oldest surviving "The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter," Murasaki Shikibu's "The Tale of Genji" (early eleventh century), depicting the life of Hikaru Genji and the human drama of the court across fifty-four chapters, reached a peak among long novels unrivaled anywhere in the world, and in the essay, Sei Shōnagon's "The Pillow Book" stood alongside it with its witty, sharply observant eye. The women's diaries beginning with Ki no Tsurayuki's "Tosa Nikki" ("The Tosa Diary"), and the elegant, delicate waka crystallized in the first imperial anthology, the "Kokin Wakashū" (905), are likewise the finest flowers of this age. The deeply felt sentiment of "mono no aware," the ideal that "The Tale of Genji" pursued, has been carried on in later literature as the wellspring of the Japanese aesthetic sensibility.
