About this work

A short story Oda Sakunosuke published in Shinsei in 1946. With Narao, a boy born slow-witted and short-sighted whose only gift is catching flies, for its protagonist, it draws with humour the life of an oddity who is shunned by the world and yet never bends his own way of doing things. Born the son of a concubine and forever compared with Shūichi, his able and worldly half-brother, Narao works as an unlicensed assistant physician, throws himself into study on his own, and pushes ahead on his own road, refusing both his mother's help and any regard for appearances. In Narao, turning his back on the world with an almost witless honesty, one feels the anti-conventional spirit and the warm eye for people that mark Oda of the Buraiha.

Summary

Narao was slow-witted from birth, nearsighted, and clumsy at everything he tried, but he had one gift — catching flies — and whenever loneliness came over him, he would catch a fly and find comfort in it. His father Keisuke felt not so much pity as bitterness toward him, and whenever he came home, which was seldom, would grumble at him to stop his foolish antics and take after Shūichi instead. Narao was the child born between his father Keisuke and his mistress Hisae, and was forever being compared to his half-brother Shūichi, the child of his father's proper wife in Ashiya, always treated as the lesser of the two. Grown to manhood, Narao, without ever obtaining a proper medical license, worked as a substitute doctor at hospitals and local clinics, while devoting himself single-mindedly to self-study and research — going so far as to spend his days at a bacteriology laboratory, silently washing test tubes, a job no one else wanted. Living in poverty with his sweetheart Yukie, he stubbornly rebuffs both the money his mother Hisae sends him and her meddling over marriage and appearances. His half-brother Shūichi, by contrast, is drawn as a shrewd, worldly man who cares for nothing but his own advancement and marriage. At last Narao declares, "Since I am a man the world despises, I will go work at a leprosarium — a place the world despises too," and moves to sever his ties with society for good. When his worried mother and brother finally manage to reach him, Narao will only agree to meet under the strange condition that "no words be spoken except over a game of shōgi." On the appointed day, Narao appears in shabby, unkempt clothes, and shows them the wooden clogs he has carved into the shapes of shōgi pieces, engraved with the characters for "bishop" and "dragon." Realizing there is nothing more to be said, Shūichi gives up and begins setting out the pieces. Oda depicts, with warm humor, the life story of an eccentric who lives entirely by his own logic, utterly indifferent to the world's yardstick.

The opening (original text)

Narao was slow-witted from birth, nearsighted, and clumsy at whatever he was set to do, but there was one thing he excelled at — catching flies — and whenever his heart grew lonely, he would catch one. A fly, he would explain, can see sideways and above, but never straight ahead, so all you need do is bring your hand straight in; and in the time it took to say so, he would already have one caught in his palm — and it seemed to comfort him, and he seemed, too, to take a secret pride in how deftly he did it, which only made him look, all the more, like a dull, downcast child.

Roppaku kinsei by Oda Sakunosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Here, take a look at this," he said abruptly, and slipped off his clogs onto the concrete. Looking at them, Shūichi saw that they had been carved into the shapes of shōgi pieces, each face engraved with the characters for "bishop" and "dragon." Shūichi caught his breath and stared at Narao's face for a while, but at last, resigning himself to the fact that there was nothing more to be said to this man, he said, "All right then, come on," and began setting out the pieces.
