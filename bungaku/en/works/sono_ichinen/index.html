About this work

Four years after the war, Obata Shinji, seventeen, begins going to the American tank camp at Chigasaki as a band boy for his brother's orchestra — a novella Yamakawa Masao published in Bungakukai. In a dance hall roaring with primary colours, he cannot take his eyes off the one woman who wears black and dances nothing but slow numbers. A piercing record of one year in which, between humiliation, jealousy and a boy's dreams, he learns the adult rule that indifference is our only hygiene.

Summary

Under a great sunset, pushed up like baggage into an American army truck, Shinji rides toward the tank battalion camp at Chigasaki. He is band boy to the band in which his elder brother plays the trumpet, at two hundred and fifty yen a time. Since the drummer has left, and Adachi the manager has coaxed him with "you have only to sit there," he goes onto the stage that very night. The floor is packed with Japanese women dancing with American soldiers; and what Shinji felt disgust and hatred for was in fact not the soldiers but those sturdy women. "Indifference is our hygiene, our morality," laughs Adachi. — At a table in the corner there was a woman in a black suit. She dances nothing but slows, and dances only with a fair-haired corporal. In the whirl of harsh primary colors, that woman, wrapped in a clear air where alone the sound seemed to have stopped, draws Shinji to her. In the lunch hour he pays twenty yen and learns nothing but the steps of the slow, and week after week he goes there with nearly ten thousand yen in his inside pocket, earned by broking cigarettes. But what he truly wanted was not to possess the woman; it was to be beaten by her, to turn himself into one clear blank. One night in February a drunken second lieutenant forces his attentions on the woman, and she goes out of the hall with the corporal. Going outside to look for her, Shinji runs into that same lieutenant trying to force himself on a waitress in the lavatory, is knocked down, and is made at last to put his lips to the girl's. He rushes at him with a stone in his hand and is knocked down again. — Because of that trouble Shinji is dismissed from the band. Before long the Korean war begins, the soldiers of the camp go off to it, and Adachi is beaten to death by American soldiers. That autumn Shinji meets "the woman in black" again at Sakuragichō station. Carrying a gaudy suitcase and chewing gum, she was walking with long strides at the right end of a group of women going off to work in Kyushu.

The opening (original text)

On the hills and the woods that ran on along both sides, changing their shape near and far, there was no longer any trace of the afterglow. The wind had grown cold as well. Rounding the foot of a low hill, from about the time they passed Hodogaya the dusk deepened. The American army truck began to gather still more speed. In the windows of a down train on the Tōkaidō line, running parallel beyond the embankment, the lights were lit. Obata Shinji watched the landscape moving backward and backward from inside the dim canvas hood of the truck. The blackening branches of the pines along the road swayed in a slow wave and shrank quickly away. The houses on both sides grew sparse, and thatched roofs and old cockscombs like clotted blood fled leaping away behind the truck, and a bus they had passed grew rapidly distant.

The ending (original text)

"Dum beat!" he shouted. He struck down with the sticks as hard as he could. It would not come right. He took off his jacket and tried again. "Dum beat!" Dum beat. Striking with his body bent to one side, Shinji was thinking of Adachi, who used to shout it. The first stroke of the drum. He had entrusted himself to the drum. I have not yet finished beating out my own dum beat. Shinji threw the sticks onto the side drum. The sticks made nothing but an irritated noise. He smiled bitterly. "It is no good," he said. "I am worse than I was. You think so too, don't you."
