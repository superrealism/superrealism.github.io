About this work

Shimamura Hōgetsu's translation of Ibsen's A Doll's House (1879), printed in Waseda Bungaku in January 1910 and published with small corrections afterwards. In three acts, Nora recognizes that she has been a doll-wife for her husband to pet, and leaves the house, and her three children with it. In 1911 Hōgetsu's Bungei Kyōkai staged it at the Imperial Theatre with Matsui Sumako as Nora, which became the starting point for the actress and for modern theatre in Japan, stirred the women of Seitō and set off the "Nora question." A long commentary by Hōgetsu is included separately.

Summary

The day before Christmas, at the house of Helmer the lawyer. Her husband is to become manager of the bank in the new year, and Nora comes home in high spirits with her parcels. Helmer calls her his little lark and his little squirrel, scolds her lightly for spending and gives her money. But Nora has a secret her husband knows nothing of. When he fell dangerously ill soon after their marriage, she borrowed the money for the journey to Italy that would cure him, on a bond signed with her dead father's name; and ever since she has been paying it back little by little out of copying work and the like. The man who lent it is Krogstad, who now works under Helmer at the bank. About to be dismissed for an old piece of dishonesty, he threatens Nora with the forged signature on the bond and demands that she persuade her husband to keep him on. Nora pleads; Helmer will not hear of it, for the sake of appearances. Driven into a corner, she dances the tarantella like a mad woman to draw his eyes away and gain time. Then Krogstad's letter of denunciation falls into the letter box. Nora waits for the miracle — the miracle of her husband taking the whole of her guilt upon himself. But when Helmer reads the letter, what comes from him is abuse: hypocrite, criminal's daughter, you have ruined my happiness and my honor. She cannot be trusted with the children; they will remain husband and wife in appearance only. Then Krogstad, having changed his mind, sends the bond back. "I am saved!" cries Helmer, and turns about at once and says he forgives her everything. And at that Nora takes off her dancing dress, puts on her ordinary clothes, and sits down before him. In eight years they have never once talked seriously together; in her father's house she was his doll-child, and in this house she has been her husband's doll-wife, and the children in turn became her dolls. So she tells him, and goes out to learn about herself and about the world. Helmer, left behind, murmurs "The miracle — the miracle —" and from below comes the sound of a heavy door closing.

Characters

  • Nora　Helmer's wife, who secretly borrowed the money for her husband's cure
  • Torvald Helmer　A lawyer, who is to become manager of the bank in the new year
  • Nils Krogstad　The bank clerk who lent Nora the money, and who presses her with the forged signature on the bond
  • Mrs. Linden　An old friend of Nora's, widowed and come to look for work
  • Doctor Rank　A friend of the Helmer household, who confesses his hidden feelings to Nora
  • Anna　Nurse to the three children

The opening (original text)

A room furnished comfortably and with taste, but not expensively. At the back, a door on the right leads to the hall, one on the left to Helmer's study; and toward the front there is a window, with a small round table beside it, two or three armchairs and a little sofa. Engravings hang on both walls. On the shelves stand china and small ornaments, and there is a small bookcase filled with handsomely bound books. The floor is carpeted, and a fire burns in the stove. It is a winter day. A bell rings in the hall; immediately afterward the outer door is heard to open, and Nora comes in humming to herself, in high spirits.

The ending (original text)

NORA (taking up her travelling bag): For that, Torvald, the greatest wonder of all would have to happen. HELMER: What is the greatest wonder of all? NORA: That both of us should so change that — Oh, I no longer believe in wonders. HELMER: But I believe in them. That we should so change that — NORA: That what is between us should become a true marriage. Goodbye. (Nora goes out.) HELMER (sinking into a chair by the door with his face in his hands): Nora! Nora! (He looks about him and rises.) There is no one here. She is gone. (A hope breathes through him.) Ah — the wonder! The wonder — (From below is heard the sound of a heavy door closing.) CURTAIN

Famous passages

Helmer: Before all else, you are a wife and a mother. Nora: I don't believe that any longer. I believe that before all else I am a reasonable human being, just as you are — or, at all events, that I must try and become one.

Nora's reply to her husband Helmer in the final act, as she prepares to leave home. Her declaration that before being a wife and mother she is first of all a human being became a symbol of women's liberation and caused a great stir in Japan at the end of the Meiji era as well.
