About this work

Ten cats in a house at Kamakura. Small horse mackerel, whiting, flounder and tuna sashimi delivered by the fishmonger morning and evening, and sometimes beefsteak and liver — an essay in which Muramatsu Shōfū, well known as a cat lover, sets down the outrageous diet of his cats. Written in a voice that could be boasting or self-mockery, it closes with the sentence that the Japanese are very much a race of cats, and that their palates come nowhere near those of the cats in his house.

Summary

There are at present ten cats in my house. It began about seven years ago when a female cat wandered in and had her kittens. The kittens were thrown away for me, but since it seemed cruel to drive out the mother, who had just given birth, I gave her milk and fish, and while I was about it she settled in for good. She was a kind of tabby, called in these parts a Kanazawa cat, and a wonderful figure of a cat; and she died with her belly cut open after a spaying went wrong. Just before she breathed her last she leapt up onto my knees as I sat in my chair, and died there. I have wept three times in my life: when my eldest son died, when a woman I loved long ago died, and when this cat died. ... I began to keep the kittens she left, and with two more that wandered in from elsewhere there are now ten. From the first I gave them no rice but only fish. Their staple is the raw small horse mackerel famous at Kamakura, and the same dried without salt; and there are whiting, flounder, bonito, boiled and dried bonito, raw tuna, egg yolk and milk, and sometimes beefsteak and liver. One will eat nothing but raw fish; another nothing but small horse mackerel. They are strong creatures of habit: they will never eat anything caught in Tokyo Bay, and if it is less than fresh they merely smell it and turn away. All over the house there are cat chairs and cat towers and cat baskets, with great cushions of purple crepe and damask laid in them. Sand is brought once a month from the shore by a large lorry, and a veterinary surgeon comes from Yokohama every ten days to examine all of them. "It is about the way of life of the King of Monaco," Shōfū writes.

The opening (original text)

There are at present ten cats in my house. As to how the cats came to be so many: about seven years ago a female cat wandered into my house and had her kittens. Discovering it, I hurriedly asked the young man at the fishmonger's to throw the kittens into the sea for me.

Neko ryōri by Muramatsu Shōfū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

For these reasons, in my house every kind of fish is cat's food. And since they are all from the famous fishing ground at Kotsubo, the material at a Tokyo tempura shop smells so of fish that I cannot eat it. (...) The Japanese are very much a race of cats. And yet their palates cannot begin to match those of the cats in my house.

Neko ryōri by Muramatsu Shōfū (Aozora Bunko)
