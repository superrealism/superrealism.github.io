About this work

Miyamoto Yuriko's first postwar work, beginning from the day of the defeat. With the prospect of meeting the husband still in prison in her heart, the heroine Hiroko travels east and west across a Japan in ruins. Changing trains, jolted along in carts, she crosses a country in which ruin and the presentiment of recovery are mixed. A landmark of postwar democratic literature, singing in clear lyricism the rising of one woman's spirit toward liberation after enduring long years of suppression.

Summary

On August 15, 1945, Hiroko, evacuated to Fukushima, greets the day of Japan's defeat. Her husband Jūkichi remains long imprisoned under the Peace Preservation Law. On the night the blackout is lifted, Hiroko's heart is stirred by both the anticipation of a liberation finally within reach and by thoughts of all the years she has endured until now. Before long she sets out across a burnt-out Japan, bound for her husband, and for the beginning of a new age. Jolted along in a train packed to bursting, she watches, at every stop along the way, both the scars the war has left and the people determined, even so, to begin their lives anew — Koreans moving west toward the straits and home, a factory reduced to ruins, and still, the autumn of the Banshū plain shining gold. At journey's end, Hiroko rides a horse-drawn cart, or walks alongside it with the young people beside her, moving down a peaceful highway toward her destination. "All of Japan is stirring, moving, like this." Hiroko feels, with her whole body, the sense that the long age of oppression has ended, and that the entire country is beginning to stir toward a new life. Rooted in the author's own experience, this representative work, filled with the lyricism of hope and liberation, announced the beginning of postwar literature.

Characters

  • Hiroko　The protagonist. Greets the day of defeat with her husband Jūkichi still imprisoned, and travels across a burnt-out Japan carrying hope for liberation and reunion
  • Jūkichi　Hiroko's husband. Long imprisoned under the Peace Preservation Law (modeled on the author's own husband, Miyamoto Kenji)
  • Sae and Yukio　A married couple with whom Hiroko lodges. They greet the day of defeat together

The opening (original text)

On the evening of August 15, 1945, as his wife Sae was setting out the family's dinner dishes on the low table in the sitting room, beneath the old wall clock, "Father, what shall we do," she asked. "About the light — I suppose it's fine to leave it bright tonight, isn't it —" From the veranda of that sitting room, the Adatara mountain range could be seen far off to the south. Yukio, who had spent the whole afternoon in silence, smoking and gazing only at the mountains, said, "Well..." and turned around with that unhurried manner of his that never seemed to be in a rush. He looked steadily at Sae's face for a while, then said, "Might it not be safer to leave it as it is a little longer?"

Banshū heiya by Miyamoto Yuriko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The autumn sun, combed by the faint breeze, melted the mountains of Banshū, the fields, the small towns and their trees into gold, and the horse-cart, clattering and creaking, moved along the single-lane highway toward its destination. The rattling sound of the wheels blended, strangely, with the young people's high spirits, and matched itself to the many feelings overflowing in Hiroko's heart. To be carried along this highway like this would surely never happen again in her whole life. She looked, with an impression that would never fade, at the hedges of the small town now passing by, at the rust-red ruins of the great factory standing beyond the pine groves of Akashi. All of Japan was stirring, moving, like this. Hiroko felt this with keen and bitter clarity.

Banshū heiya by Miyamoto Yuriko (Aozora Bunko)
