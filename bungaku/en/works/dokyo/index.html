About this work

Dōkyō, handed down as the great wicked monk who aimed at the throne — Sakaguchi Ango draws him again as an entirely different man. Starting from the guideline of what he calls the age of women in Japanese history, he lights up the loneliness of the empress Kōken, raised as a living god and never permitted to live as a woman, and places beside her a monk with a plain and single-hearted soul. One of Ango's central historical works, turning received opinion inside out.

Summary

There was in the history of Japan a period which may be called an age of women — so the story begins. Readers will no doubt imagine the Heian court; but the brilliance of Murasaki Shikibu and Sei Shōnagon existed in order to be loved by men, and was not an age of women in the proper sense. The true age of women, says Ango, was the Nara period, when empresses held real power. And he calls the Empress Kōken (Shōtoku) "a magnificent cripple." She was raised as the first person of the realm, as the most august living deity in the world, and was taught nothing about the men whom a woman's heart must naturally seek. Her father and mother, the Emperor Shōmu and the Empress Kōmyō, seeing that her grandmother Genmei and her aunt Genshō had both remained unmarried and of firm conduct, believed too lightly in the temper of their daughter. ... Beside that solitary empress appears Dōkyō. The Dōkyō whom Ango draws is no schemer after power. In his prayers his heart is raised without abasement; he vanishes into nothing like a fragrance, and rises like a rock; he thinks only of the empress's happiness in the next world and nothing of himself. He loved the empress. His heart and his body were sunk wholly in everything of hers, and his spirit was as straightforward and single as an infant's. In time the empress died, and Dōkyō, having lost his support, is exiled to Shimotsuke. Everything was already over. There was nothing left to throw away. When he looked at the clouds the clouds went into his breast; when he looked up at the hills the hills went into his breast. And yet he held to one bracing dignity in his breast and would not let it go, and holding it, he had only to wait idly for the day his life should end.

Characters

  • Dōkyō　The monk who served the empress. A man of a straightforward and single spirit
  • The Empress Kōken (Shōtoku)　The empress raised as a living deity and never allowed to live as a woman
  • Fujiwara no Nakamaro (Emi no Oshikatsu)　The powerful minister who set himself against the empress's authority

The opening (original text)

There was in the history of Japan a period which may be called an age of women. This story must begin from the character of that particular age. When one says an age of women, readers will no doubt imagine chiefly the Heian court — that period when Murasaki Shikibu and Sei Shōnagon and Izumi Shikibu swept the world with their brilliant gifts.

Dōkyō by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Beyond the thought of being parted from Her Majesty there was no thought that tormented him. Everything was already over. There was nothing left to throw away. When he looked at the clouds the clouds went into his breast; when he looked up at the hills the hills went into his breast. And yet he held to one bracing dignity in his breast and would not let it go. It was more precious than any image of the Buddha, more precious than anything. Holding it, he had only to wait idly for the day his life should end.

Dōkyō by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)
