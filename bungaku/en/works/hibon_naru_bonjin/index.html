About this work

The life of a man called Katsura Shōsaku, told by the narrator to his friends. A boy of middling gifts and no little mischief, he meets "Saigoku Risshihen" — the Japanese version of Samuel Smiles's "Self-Help" — and from then on carries out one by one every plan he sets himself, earning his school fees by writing in sand and selling newspapers, graduating from the technical school and becoming an electrical engineer. It is not a brilliant success story but a work in praise of the plain power of the will itself, and may be called a book of encouragement that Doppo offered to the young men of Meiji.

Summary

At a gathering where five or six young men are talking about their friends, one of them begins to tell of a man named Katsura Shōsaku. Shōsaku is the narrator's friend from childhood, and is now an electrical engineer at a company in Yokohama. In his primary-school days he would refuse even to play on a Sunday and pore over "Saigoku Risshihen," and from then on he grew up with that one book for his Bible. When his father's ventures in industry failed and the family declined he could not go on to middle school and became an errand boy at a bank; but, resolved to make his way by invention, he saved his wages and laid his plan to go up to Tokyo. In Tokyo he lodged at a cheap boarding house, apprenticed himself to the old man who wrote in sand at Kudan, and as the war with China drew near earned money selling extras while studying mathematics at night school, and entered the evening course of the technical school. When the narrator, calling on him after three years, is taken to a cheap eating house behind a rope curtain and sees him eat his three meals a day without turning a hair, he swallows his tears in spite of himself, and then takes himself in hand and makes a good meal of it. He spent two thirds of the thirty yen he had saved over two years on presents and went home for a visit; and after graduating, while working as an electrical engineer, he patiently looked after the younger brother who had run away from home and made a qualified engineer of him. One day the narrator calls at the company and, seeing under the electric lights the solemn, wholly earnest face of Shōsaku as he circles a steel pole again and again absorbed in a repair, is deeply moved — and ends by asking his friends to raise a glass to this friend of his.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, Katsura Shōsaku's friend from childhood
  • Katsura Shōsaku　An electrical engineer at a company in Yokohama. He loves "Saigoku Risshihen" and carries out steadily every plan he sets himself
  • Shōsaku's father　A former samurai who turned to farming after the wars of the Restoration and failed in industry
  • Gorō　Shōsaku's younger brother, who ran away from home and became an engineer through his brother's care

The opening (original text)

Five or six young men were once gathered together, talking about their friends, and one of them said — There is a man named Katsura Shōsaku who has been my friend since childhood. He is twenty-four this year, and is now employed as an engineer at a certain company in Yokohama, working entirely in electricity; and I think there can hardly be a man of a more unusual kind than he is. He is not an extraordinary man. But neither is he an ordinary one. Nor yet is he a crank or an eccentric. "An extraordinary ordinary man" is, I think, the aptest description of him. The better I come to know this man, the more I cannot help admiring him. To say I admire him is not to admire him as one admires a Hideyoshi or a Napoleon or some other man of genius; men of that sort appear perhaps once in a thousand years or so, whereas a man like Katsura Shōsaku is one whom an ordinary society can always produce, and whom an ordinary society always needs. For that reason, every time there is one more man like Katsura, society is by so much the happier. It is in that sense that I admire Katsura. And that is why I call Katsura an extraordinary ordinary man.

An Extraordinary Ordinary Man by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

It was in the spring of this year. I called on Katsura at Nogechō in Yokohama one evening, and, being told by the people of his lodging that he was still at the company, I went on to the company, wanting to see the place as well. When I went to where Katsura was working — I do not know electricity in any detail and cannot explain it fully — several men were standing about a single thick steel pole, and Shōsaku alone was going round and round that pole again and again, earnestly at work upon something. The electric lights were already lit and shining on this group of men as though it were broad day. The others were silent, watching what Shōsaku did. It seemed that something had gone wrong with the machinery, and that Shōsaku had found the fault and was repairing it. Katsura's face, his whole bearing! He was as though in a place where no one else existed, having forgotten himself and forgotten the world, throwing body and soul into the work he was doing. I had never seen Katsura's face so earnest as that. As I watched, I was struck by a kind of solemnity. Gentlemen! Will you raise a glass to my friend, and drink to his future!

An Extraordinary Ordinary Man by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)
