About this work

An early work drawing the inner life of a woman bound to a marriage she did not want, published in Bungei Kurabu in February 1896. In a rented house in a shabby quarter, a young wife goes on telling her history to the boy who lodges there. Hating her husband and yet bound by appearances, she goes on wishing that he would die — and her mind is at last seen through entirely by the husband on his sickbed. Kyōka's subject, the unblinking study of a woman's passion, appears vividly this early.

Summary

Osada, the young wife of a house in a shabby quarter that lets out the upstairs of its old building, is twenty-one. Her husband, bound to her by a match arranged in her grandfather's day, is a bearded man who teaches at a school. To Yoshinosuke, a boy who lodges upstairs, Osada tells her life story at great length. The days of nursing her grandfather, the photographs sent every month by her husband when he went up to Tokyo, the strange habit her husband brought back with him of wearing dark glasses, and Tamaki, their small child, who never took to his father and died longing only for his mother. Married after nothing more than the exchange of three cups of sake, her body become her husband's property — telling of this unreasonableness, Osada is frightened by her own self, which goes on wishing, over and over, that he would only die. In time her husband falls ill, and, nursed day and night without rest, quietly delivers his judgment to Osada.

Characters

  • Osada　A twenty-one-year-old wife. Bound to a marriage she never wanted, she goes on wishing for her husband's death
  • Yoshinosuke (Yoshi-san)　A boy who lodges upstairs. Becomes Osada's confidant
  • Osada's husband　A bearded man who teaches at a school. Sees through to Osada's heart on his sickbed
  • Oren　Yoshinosuke's elder sister. Made a bad match while young, and drowned herself

The opening (original text)

The upstairs let out to lodgers consisted of two rooms, six mats and four and a half mats, with a further storeroom of a little over five mats, for one yen a month. The landlord's family lived below, in the six-mat middle room and the five-mat room at the back, and the front, a board floor of about eight mats, though it was no shop, had one wooden shutter left down even in daytime and stood unused and abandoned.

Bakeichō by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

She shrinks in dread from the light of day; if one were to force the shutter open and let the sunlight fall upon her skin even once, she would perish on the spot, and all would be over. Such is her loathing of the light. And so, even in the dead of night, Osada is afraid even of a single thread of lamplight, and blows it out. Thus, though still living, she has been buried away in the darkness. A wife who has killed her husband, and yet — I beg you, gentle readers, forgive her. Do not, I entreat you, shoot down this pitiable, faithful wife by forcing the light of day upon her.

Bakeichō by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)
