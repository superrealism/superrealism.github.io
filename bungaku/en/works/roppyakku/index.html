About this work

A self-selected collection compiled to mark the six hundredth number of Hototogisu. The poems run from 1941 to 1945 — exactly the five years from the beginning of the Pacific war to the defeat — arranged in order with their dates and the record of the gatherings at which they were made. What Takahama Kyoshi, who held to his principle of singing flowers and birds, was looking at under wartime and in evacuation at Komoro can be followed poem by poem.

Summary

Following Five Hundred Poems, made for the five hundredth number of Hototogisu, and Five Hundred and Fifty Poems for the five hundred and fiftieth, this is the third collection Kyoshi chose from his own work, made to mark the six hundredth. The preface was written in September of the twenty-first year of Shōwa at the mountain cottage at Komoro, where he had been evacuated. The poems are from the sixteenth to the twentieth years of Shōwa. "A calm at the New Year — and now and then a great wave comes" (New Year's Day in January, walking on Yuigahama); "On the great Buddha the winter sun lies like a surplice" — each poem carries the date of its making and a careful record of the poetry meeting or the visitors of that day, so that the book can be read like a diary. The days pass in poetry meetings and excursions, but before long the shadow of the times falls across them. To escape the air raids Kyoshi was evacuated to Komoro in Shinshū, and from then on many of the poems sing the four seasons of a mountain village and farming country at the foot of Asama. "Is this a silkworm village? — the mulberry withering"; "Crossed the mountain and came — on the pass there was snow." Of the war itself he sings almost nothing aloud; and the flowers and birds and the bearing of the seasons set down there convey, on the contrary, the stillness and the weight of that time. "Warming my poems like jewels — the quilted brazier"; "Obstinately hunting for a lost thing — the day is short." It closes with the record of poetry meetings at Kamakura and Komoro in December of the twentieth year of Shōwa.

The opening (original text)

Preface Some time ago, to mark the five hundredth number of Hototogisu, I brought out through Kaizōsha a book called Five Hundred Poems; and again, to mark the five hundred and fiftieth number of Hototogisu, I brought out through Sakurai Shoten a book called Five Hundred and Fifty Poems. Now, at the urging of Seishidō, I am to bring out a book called Six Hundred Poems to mark the six hundredth number of Hototogisu. These are chosen from among the poems of the sixteenth to the twentieth years of Shōwa.

Famous passages

Washing the daikon, the water all crumpled and churned.

A haiku that captures the rush of water over washed daikon radishes with the word “kushakusha.” A model of the “objective sketching from life” that Kyoshi taught, plainly recording an ordinary everyday scene.

Between heaven and earth, a light patter — the early winter rain.

Between heaven and earth, the early winter rain falls lightly. Setting the vast space against the faint sound “horo,” it shows the breadth of Kyoshi's mature style.

“As a sign that I am still alive,” he wrote — and sent new tea.

A haiku written in mourning for Wakayagi Toshisaburō, who took his own life. Recalling words from a letter sent while he was alive — sending new tea “as a sign that I am still alive” — the casual kindness deepens the grief.

An old dog sniffs at me and goes away, among the withered trees.

A haiku written at Komoro, where he had evacuated during the war. An old dog sniffs at him and walks off through the winter-bare trees: a quietly lonely haiku that plainly sets the aging poet beside the old dog.
