About this work

An essay by Aizu Yaichi, known as a poet and calligrapher and also a historian of Eastern art. People have cut their thoughts into stone that does not decay — city walls, images of gods, graves, monuments — and wished for eternity. But stone is frail against a thousand years of wind and frost, and not one in a thousand of those made survives. The author takes as his thread the fate of the stele that people set up in memory of Yang Hu, a famous minister of the Jin, and which was called the Stele of Falling Tears because it made those who saw it weep. Even that stone was knocked down and broken and recarved again and again, and is now gone. A quiet reflection, characteristic of a writer close to the old art of Nara, that books and letters carry a name further than stone does.

Summary

From the earliest of times, human beings have carved their hopes for eternity into stone that will not decay — walls, images of the gods, tombs, and steles. Yet stone cannot withstand a thousand years of wind and frost: it is lost to fire and to human hands, and not one stone in a thousand ever made survives. The author here draws on an anecdote concerning the celebrated minister Yang Hu of the Chinese state of Jin. Yang Hu, a lover of mountains and rivers, reflected sorrowfully on his own mortality upon considering how completely the great men of the past had been forgotten — and yet, after his death, those who cherished his virtue raised a stele for him upon a hill, and all who saw it were moved to tears, so that it came to be called the "Tablet of Falling Tears." Du Yu, his contemporary, mindful of his own posthumous reputation, had two identical steles made: one he set up on the hill, the other he sank in the depths of a pool, against any eventuality. Later, in the Tang dynasty, Meng Haoran and Li Bai both wrote poems about this tablet — and yet the stele itself, a mere two hundred and seventy-two years after Yang Hu's death, had already crumbled and had to be recarved, and even that recarving had again flaked away and grown over with moss. The tablet perished after its third incarnation, and today it exists no more. And yet Yang Hu's name endures, as long as the Wen Xuan and the historical records survive, while Du Yu's own work, the Collected Commentaries on the Zuo Tradition, is still read by young students even today. Stone, it turns out, is surprisingly fragile — its lifespan falls short even of paper and ink. Human beings, the author concludes, must rely on something more certain still.

The opening (original text)

For a very long time now, human beings have relied upon stone, and yet, with great persistence, they go on doing so still. They rely on stone, cling to stone, revere stone, worship stone. Out of this same impulse, walls, altars, images of the gods, and great halls have all been made of stone. In their eagerness to make things that will remain in this world forever, human beings, whether in the East or in the West, or in any other corner of the earth, have from ancient times made truly remarkable efforts. A person dies. Even if the body were simply left lying on the bare earth, once the pus and rotting flesh had all drained away, the bones alone would remain, possessing, like stone, the quality of enduring for a long time — and yet those who call themselves the bereaved family and friends gather together, light a fire, and burn the body. Even the hard-won bones are reduced to fragments. These are gathered up, buried deep in the earth, and a rough, square stone is set up above them. To go and pay one's respects is spoken of as though that stone itself were the departed, and it is worshipped as such. And the larger the stone, the more the one who raised it is praised as a chaste wife or a filial child. For the poor, filial piety is difficult even on this count.

Ippen no ishi by Aizu Yaichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Yang, though he was mindful of his posthumous name, may take comfort: even now, when the very stele raised for him by others has perished into its third incarnation, so long as the Wen Xuan, the Book of Jin, and the bibliographic treatise of the Book of Sui survive, there will never fail to be, somewhere, in whatever age, those who know his name. His departed spirit, surely, has already come to sense this. As for the stele his friend Du Yu planned — both copies of it have perished, and there is now no way of knowing where they went — yet his written work, the Collected Commentaries on the Zuo Tradition, of which he seems to have been rather proud, is still current in the world even now, and is often to be seen in the hands of young students in Japan. And so it may be said that, despite mankind's deep hopes since ancient times, stone is in fact a surprisingly fragile thing, whose lifespan falls short even of paper and ink — and that human beings must rely upon something more certain still. Du Yu's departed spirit, too, must have miscalculated rather badly, and no doubt felt some confusion at first — but having passed beyond that, he must by now have found his peace.

Ippen no ishi by Aizu Yaichi (Aozora Bunko)
