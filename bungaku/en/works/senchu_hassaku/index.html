About this work

The eight-article programme in which Sakamoto Ryōma set out his design for a new state. He is said to have dictated it in 1867 aboard the Tosa domain ship Yūgao, while travelling to Kyoto with Gotō Shōjirō, which is how it comes to be called the plan of the eight articles composed at sea. Restoring power to the emperor, establishing an assembly, drawing up a constitution, expanding the navy, revising the treaties with foreign powers: eight measures that anticipate the shape of the state after the Restoration, in one of the central political documents of the late shogunate.

Summary

The Eight Points at Sea condenses the shape of the new state that was to come into eight articles. First, that the political power of the realm be returned to the imperial court, and that decrees issue from the court. Second, that upper and lower legislative chambers be established, with members to take part in all affairs of state, so that all matters be decided by public deliberation. Third, that able men be appointed as advisers and that offices which exist in name only be abolished. Fourth, that intercourse with foreign countries be settled broadly by public deliberation and that proper treaties be newly concluded. Fifth, that a new constitution be drawn up, taking what is best from the codes of old. Sixth, that the navy be enlarged. Seventh, that a guard be established to protect the imperial capital. Eighth, that a law be made to bring the value of gold and silver and the price of goods into line with those of foreign countries. And at the end it declares that if these eight measures are carried through, it will not be hard to restore the fortunes of the imperial house, extend the strength of the country and stand together with all the nations — calling for a great and resolute decision, grounded in open and upright reason, to make the realm new.

The opening (original text)

One. That the political power of the realm be returned to the imperial court, and that all decrees properly issue from the court. One. That upper and lower legislative chambers be established, with members appointed to take part in all affairs of state, and that all matters properly be decided by public deliberation. One. That able nobles and lords and men of talent throughout the realm be appointed as advisers and granted office and rank, and that the offices which have hitherto existed in name only be abolished. One. That in our intercourse with foreign countries public deliberation be widely sought, and that proper new agreements be concluded.

Senchū hassaku by Sakamoto Ryōma (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

These eight measures, when one considers the present state of the realm and tests them against all the nations of the world, are such that if they are set aside there can be no other urgent business for saving the times. If only these several measures be carried out resolutely, it will not be thought difficult to restore the fortunes of the imperial house, to extend the strength of the country, and to stand together with all the nations. I humbly ask that, on the ground of open and upright reason, we make a new beginning with the realm by one great and resolute decision.

Senchū hassaku by Sakamoto Ryōma (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

One: That the political power of the realm be returned to the Imperial Court, and that all government orders issue from the Court. One: That upper and lower deliberative houses be established, with members appointed to take part in all affairs of state, and that all matters be decided by public deliberation.

The first two articles, calling for the return of political power to the Court and the establishment of a legislature. It is the plan for a new state that Sakamoto Ryōma is said to have shown Gotō Shōjirō of the Tosa domain; though no original survives, it is known as a document that symbolizes the Meiji Restoration.
