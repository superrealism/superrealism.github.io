About this work

An essay on taste by Kitaōji Rosanjin, well known as a gourmet. Sweetfish, he holds, are properly grilled with salt at three or four inches; he explains how hard it is for an amateur to skewer and grill a live, fresh one well, and says flatly that it is no disgrace to be unable to grill sweetfish at home. Weaving in the excellence of raw sweetfish he learned of as a child in Kyoto and memories of Yamanaka hot spring in Kaga, he sets out in a brisk voice his conviction that the beauty of taste and of form cannot be separated.

Summary

Rosanjin first declares flatly that a good sweetfish can hardly be grilled at all in an ordinary Tokyo household. It is commonly thought that ayu weaken the moment they are taken out of the water, but in fact they are lively enough to flop about even after their heads are struck off on the cutting board, so slippery that skewering them is difficult, and easily ruined over a weak charcoal fire that scorches the tail fin. He argues, therefore, that to eat good ayu one has no choice but to rely on the finest establishments in the fish's own home region. Rosanjin also tells of his surprise, as a child in Kyoto, at the extravagance of the Mitsui family, who would eat only the flesh of the ayu prepared as arai; and recalls how he later, at the Zōkirō inn at Yamanaka hot spring in Kaga, ate his fill of ayu arai and was thoroughly convinced of its excellence. Touching also on the idea of applying the same treatment to char, he concludes, in the end, that the very best way is simply to bite hot into an ordinary salt-grilled ayu.

The opening (original text)

For various reasons, an ordinary household cannot prepare ayu in a way that lets one eat it well. The proper way is, first, to salt-grill ayu of three or four inches and eat it so, but for a household it is difficult to get hold of a live or truly fresh one. There may be households here and there in the provinces where this is possible, but in Tokyo it is safe to say it simply cannot be done. The conditions of Tokyo see to that. And even supposing one did manage to get hold of a live ayu, it is not something an amateur can skillfully skewer and grill. Ayu commonly give the impression that they die the moment they are taken out of the water. For this reason people think of them as an extremely delicate fish, but in truth ayu, even set on the cutting board with their heads struck off, are lively enough to leap up again and again.

Ayu no kuikata by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In the end, the best way with ayu, fragrant above all, is the ordinary salt-grilled kind, hot enough to burn your mouth if you're not careful, bitten straight into.

Ayu no kuikata by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)
