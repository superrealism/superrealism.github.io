About this work

An aesthetic fantasy set in Nanjing under the Qing, published in Chūō Kōron in January 1917. To a young nobleman who has exhausted every luxury and dissipation, a Dutch merchant brings a mermaid from the Mediterranean to sell. It draws a love that can never be consummated, between a beautiful creature shut in a glass jar and a human being longing for her from outside the water. Published with illustrations by Mizushima Niou, and famous as a central work of Tanizaki's early fantastic writing.

Summary

Meng Shi, a young nobleman of Nanjing, has vast wealth, beauty and wit, and is sick of every amusement; neither his seven concubines nor the rare wines of the four quarters can console him. On the evening of the Lantern Festival, looking down from his terrace at the crowds in the streets, he notices a strangely dressed Westerner with a cart drawn by a donkey. The man, who says he is a Hollander, has spent half a year going from port to port in Asia to sell a mermaid he took at a coral island in the Dutch territories. Sparing neither diamonds nor rubies nor peacocks nor ivory, the nobleman buys her. Before that cold and beautiful creature shut in a glass jar he feels, for the first time in his life, a shudder go through him, and the house falls silent. In time a single cup of wine gives the mermaid human speech again, and she begs him to send her home to her own Mediterranean.

Characters

  • Meng Shi　A young nobleman of Nanjing, blessed with great wealth and beauty and suffering from tedium
  • The mermaid　The beautiful creature the Hollander brought from the Mediterranean, forbidden for ever to love a human being
  • The Hollander　The Western merchant who comes to sell her, with a noble look about him not unlike the mermaid's own
  • The seven concubines　The favorites the young nobleman had chosen for him from the beauties of the music academy

The opening (original text)

Long, long ago, when the dynasty of the Aisin Gioro was still flourishing and shining like a peony in the sixth month, there lived in Nanjing, a great city of China, a young nobleman named Meng Shi. This young man's father had for a time served at the court in Beijing, and standing high in the favor of the Qianlong Emperor had performed exploits that men envied — and had also amassed a fortune of the sort for which men shun a man; and he left this world while his only son, Shi, was still a child. Before long the young man's mother followed his father, so that Shi, left an orphan, naturally became the sole possessor of mountains of gold and silver and treasure.

Ningyo no nageki by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

It was five or six minutes later. Far out on the boundless offing, on the surface of the water where the light struck most dazzlingly and most sharply, a bold creature turned its body over, throwing up a shower of silver spray like a flying fish leaping. Startled by that uncanny shape, glittering so white that one might have thought the jade hare of the sky had fallen into the sea, the young nobleman turned that way — and in the instant that he did, the mermaid was already buried more than half in the misty water; and with both hands raised high she gave one cry of "Ah!" and sank, whirling round and round beneath the surface. And the ship went on, little by little, carrying one thread of hope in the young man's breast, toward the beloved and longed-for Europe, toward the Mediterranean that was the mermaid's home.

Ningyo no nageki by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)
