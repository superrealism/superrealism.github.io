About this work

A story by Shimizu Shikin, published in "Jogaku Zasshi" in January 1891. It takes the form of a single monologue in which a woman who goes on wearing a ring with the stone gone explains to an intimate why she does so. Married against her will at her father's command, made to suffer by her husband's faithlessness, losing her mother, and at last, through the new arguments for women's rights, awakening to her own selfhood and leaving her husband's house by her own decision — as a work in which a woman speaks of marriage and divorce in her own words, it is counted a forerunner of modern women's literature. The broken ring, she says, is a treasure she would not exchange for ten million in gold.

Summary

Asked why she still wears a ring with its stone gone, the woman begins to tell her story. The ring was given her by her husband five years ago, when she married in the spring of her eighteenth year. In those days the seed of women's education had barely been sown; she had been drilled in a Chinese-style ethics that taught her a husband is like a lottery ticket, and that whether one draws well or ill there is nothing to do but leave it to Heaven. Even so, she refused each time marriage was urged on her — until, in the New Year of her eighteenth year, her father flatly ordered it. "Because my studies are not yet enough," she began to say, and was cut off. Her mother would not take her side either. Once married, she could not grow used to her husband at all. One day he snatched away a letter that had come for him, and from that night on he began to stay out. From the maidservant she learned that a woman had been living in the house until five or six days before she came to it. The nights away became three, then four, and days followed in which he came home drunk and abused her. Her mother in her old home, guessing how things stood, fell ill and died in the autumn of her nineteenth year. To think that a marriage she had not wanted had shortened her mother's life was more than her breast could hold. Just then the argument for women's rights was beginning to appear in the world, and through Western thought she came to believe that a woman's unhappiness is not the decree of Heaven. When she remonstrated with her husband he only dismissed her — a woman, and presuming so far. And then she pulled the stone from the ring.

Characters

  • I　The woman who goes on wearing the broken ring. Married at eighteen against her will, she later left her husband's house by her own decision — the narrator
  • Husband　A man older than she and practiced in the world, still entangled with the woman who had lived in the house before her
  • Father　The man who ordered his daughter to marry. He later regrets his meddling and encourages her purpose
  • Mother　A woman who lived the precepts of the "Greater Learning for Women" to the letter. She falls ill worrying over her daughter's unhappiness

The opening (original text)

So the stone gone out of this ring of mine troubles you. Well, you are quite right: it does not look at all well to wear it broken like this, and I might have any stone at all set in its place, and that would be that…. But for me the breaking of this ring is a keepsake, and so I cannot possibly have it set again.

The Broken Ring by Shimizu Shikin (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Only, happily, my father is still in good health, and he pities me greatly for the long years of hardship I have borne, saying that his old man's meddling broke the branch of a young tree that should never have been broken; and he writes to me constantly to comfort me, so that now he praises my purpose instead and urges me on without ceasing. That is my greatest joy, and in the midst of sorrow I pass my days happily enough. If I have one wish beyond it, it is that this broken ring might be made whole again by the hand of the one who gave it — and yet, even now, that…
