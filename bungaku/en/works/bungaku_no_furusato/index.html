About this work

Perrault's "Little Red Riding Hood" — a nursery tale with no moral in it, which simply ends with a sweet girl eaten by a wolf. In it Sakaguchi Ango finds a still, transparent, aching home country that remains after one has been cast off. Written under wartime, before "Discourse on Decadence," and still read again and again as the essay that states the principle of Ango's work.

Summary

Charles Perrault's "Little Red Riding Hood" is nothing more than this: a pretty little girl goes to visit her grandmother in the wood, and is eaten up, munch munch, by the wolf disguised as the grandmother. The moral a fairy tale ought normally to have is entirely lacking here. That is why in France it is often cited as the representative case of the "amoral" — so Ango begins. A charming girl, gentle-hearted, guilty of nothing at all, is eaten, and it ends. The reader is abruptly cast off there, and in his bewilderment, feeling that the bargain has not been kept, he is struck all the same, and in that empty margin cut off with a snap he sees, does he not, a very quiet, transparent, aching "home country"? Ango then shows the same casting-off in the Japanese classics as well — the tale of the yam gruel in the Konjaku Monogatari, the kyōgen play "Busu." No rescue and no interpretation is prepared there. Is the solitude of existence, then, as cruel and as helpless as that? Just so, Ango answers. In this dark solitude there is no rescue; one only wanders in a wilderness, unable even to expect a house of rescue. And then: "Just as the fact that there is no morality is itself a morality, so the fact that there is no rescue is itself a rescue." There, he says, I see the home country of literature, or the home country of man; literature begins here. But he takes care to add that the home country is a cradle, and that the work of an adult is not to go back to it.

The opening (original text)

Among the fairy tales of Charles Perrault there is a famous one called "Little Red Riding Hood." I imagine you know it already, but to give the outline: a pretty little girl, called Little Red Riding Hood because she wore a red hood, went as usual to visit her grandmother in the wood, and a wolf had disguised himself as the grandmother, and ate Little Red Riding Hood up, munch, munch. That is truly all there is to the story.

Bungaku no furusato by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And in the end, that it is cruel, that there is no rescue — that alone is the only rescue. Just as the fact that there is no morality is itself a morality, so the fact that there is no rescue is itself a rescue. Here I see the home country of literature, or the home country of man. Literature begins here — so I also think.

Bungaku no furusato by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Just as the very absence of morality is itself a morality, the very absence of salvation is itself a salvation.

A passage from an essay that discusses the roots of literature through fairy tales and legends that offer no salvation. It says that it is precisely in stories that push us away that the true face of humanity appears.

Here I see the birthplace of literature—or the birthplace of humanity. Literature begins here—that too is what I think.

The passage in which the phrase that gives the essay its title appears. It says that where a story without salvation pushes the reader away lies the starting point of literature, and of humanity.
