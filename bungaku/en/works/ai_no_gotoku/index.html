About this work

I have always lived interested only in myself — Yamakawa Masao's central work, published in Shinchō and later giving its title to the collection issued after his death. The narrator, who supports a household of women by writing a daily radio serial, is visited every Saturday by the wife of a friend he has met again after seven years. Holding fast to the conviction that he loves no one and has no right to be loved by anyone, he cannot manage the ordinary life beginning to form inside him. The high point of a writer who died suddenly at thirty-four.

Summary

Every week from Friday to Sunday "I" go to a three-mat lodging along a private railway line. A mother who is up and down with an inflamed gallbladder, an elder sister of thirty-three, a younger sister of twenty-five. Worn out by the family's complaints, he dutifully turns out six dramatizations of newspaper serials at a time and earns the household's living. That he is interested only in himself, and is convinced that he can love no one — that was his one support. ... In April an old woman friend whom he met by chance on the train calls at his lodging. A small woman, formerly a voice actress, with whom he had had relations several times, and who is now the wife of a friend from his university days. At her second visit they become lovers, and from then on the woman comes every Saturday. The woman says, "I am a pig," and, "You are a spider. You are always absent-minded, and now and then you turn fierce like a flash of lightning, and then you disappear somewhere again." "I" called her "Boo," and she called him "Spider." Binding her hands and feet with neckties and shirts, putting a cloth in her mouth, "I" hold her as though to make sure of my own dark madness. But she chews all of it calmly and takes it for nothing but the violence of affection. On a night when the power failed in a storm, seeing in the smile they exchanged in the dark that he was sharing a madman's happiness with her, he hastily untied the cords. ... In a small park with a pond in a pine wood, he asks for the first time, "Will you come next week?" He was beginning to think that perhaps, with this woman, he could get on. But the next week she did not come. What came was a black-bordered card printed with his friend's name. The woman had been knocked down by a car and had died a week later without recovering consciousness. Except for one postcard he tore up, he has no "facts" at all. The next day, at his lodging, he wept aloud for the first time since he could remember.

The opening (original text)

I have always lived interested only in myself. Since there was nothing else certain for me, I thought that this was my own kind of justice. I was always defining myself and explaining myself, chasing my own unintelligibility, mocking and despising myself and chuckling over it, and yet, as though I had given it up for want of anything better, keeping company with no one but that same self. To keep company with oneself alone. Whether that is possible or impossible is another matter. I only wanted to do it. Whether it was for that reason I do not know. To me, myself was always the other person I disliked most. I was convinced that I could love no one.

Ai no gotoku by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Before I knew it the tears had stopped. It came to me suddenly: ah, today was that woman's funeral; it must be over by now. And I thought too that the house my mother is planning will be built next early summer at the soonest. There is still half a year. The radio serial I am doing now will probably last another half year too. Either way I must go on working at this lodging for another six months. — Gazing at the stain of the rain and wind of some past day still left on the wall, I felt that commuting here for the weekends without her would be a kind of torture. But if I left this room, would the torture end? The window was scarlet as if blood had been flung over it. I got up slowly and opened it. It was a tremendous sunset. The wide western sky, cloud and all, was burning a deep madder, and I could tell that the red was falling on my face as well.
