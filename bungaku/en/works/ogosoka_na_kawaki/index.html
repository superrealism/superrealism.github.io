About this work

The last work Yamamoto Shūgorō serialized in the Sunday edition of the Asahi Shimbun in 1967, left unfinished at his sudden death. Set in a mountain village in Fukui, it draws the human pattern of a country deep in faith and custom, where families of the True Pure Land and the Zen schools have been at odds for centuries and there are even whispers of hidden Christians. In its weighty treatment of religion and human karma one senses the ground a master of popular literature reached at the last: a work regrettably broken off.

Summary

Yamashina village, Ōno county, Fukui Prefecture. A poor village in a valley straddling a dry streambed, where the sun rises late and sets early. In this place, the Shimada family, of the Zen sect and devoted to Eiheiji, and the many households that follow the Shingon and Ikkō sects, have carried on a deep-rooted enmity and hostility for hundreds of years. Even the ordinary comings and goings for weddings, funerals and festivals have long since ceased between them, and even at the celebration for the birth of a son to the Shimada family, the village headman's house, only twelve or thirteen people of the same faith gather to show their faces. At that gathering, one of the guests whispers, "This house are secret Christians, you know — Eiheiji is only there to fool the world." A mountain village where faith has bitten deep into the roots of people's lives and their strife. The story traces the figures of people living in this land of old custom and faith, in Yamamoto Shūgorō's own weighty prose. But in February 1967, midway through its serialization, Yamamoto's sudden death cut the story off unfinished. A last, unfinished work — the final piece a veteran master of letters took up, on the theme of faith and human karma.

The opening (original text)

"That spring at Otane's cave," said Shimada Ikuzō, the village headman, "they say a thousand years ago, or maybe longer, Kōbō Daishi struck the rock with his staff and said, 'Water, come forth' — struck it three times with his staff, they say, but not a single drop of water came out. In those days, they say, this village had a water shortage, and there was never an end to the water disputes with the two neighbouring villages, and quite a few people died over it, it seems. Then Dōgen Zenji came along, gave his rosary a single rub, and just like that the water came gushing out."

Ogosoka na kawaki by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Ryūji gathered dry branches and wood and fed them into the fire, thinking — there are still this many fish in the river; nature is still alive. He was struck with a deep emotion, and it very nearly brought tears to his eyes. (His last words. Unfinished.)

Ogosoka na kawaki by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
