About this work

A piece of sketch prose Masaoka Shiki published in Hototogisu in 1899, famous as a practical demonstration of the sketch prose he advocated. Confined to his sickbed, Shiki has gone without breakfast and waits impatiently for a late lunch, and, resting his chin on his hand on the bedding, copies down as they are the flowers in the garden, the clouds in the sky, and the voices of the neighbourhood children teasing a kitten. A small piece drawing an unremarkable hour of daily life with a fine eye for observation.

Summary

I have long since made it a rule never to eat breakfast, and so, invalid though I am, it is an everyday thing for my stomach to growl as I wait impatiently for lunch. Today the noon gun has already sounded, and still no meal has come. There is nothing by my pillow — no book, no inkstone, not even a scrap of newspaper — so I have no choice but to lie propped on one elbow against the bedding, gazing vacantly out at the garden. The sky is clouded, perhaps a lingering trace of the autumn gale two days before. A row of cockscombs, battered by the wind, have righted themselves again into a line of deep crimson heads; a stalk of amaranth shows its handsome leaves against the white laundry hung out to dry; something like a great hairy knotweed towers absurdly tall, its pale red flowers spilling over the amaranth beneath. Three children who had wandered into the garden earlier catch a kitten and torment it just outside the fence — one voice worrying, "Hit it that hard and it'll turn into a ghost," another shrugging it off, "As if it would." A small yellow butterfly flutters by, a hungry quail scratches at its cage, and from the kitchen comes the clink of plates and sake bottles. With nothing else left to look at, I turn my eyes up to the sky, where black clouds and white move steadily off toward the northeast, and a little blue shows through, which pleases me. The children return, this time shutting the kitten in the rubbish bin, until the mother of one of them comes out and scolds them into letting it go. And then — the sun flashes bright across the tatami. The meal has come. A small sketch in which the bedridden Shiki captures, with the eye of shasei realism, one ordinary hour spent waiting for a late lunch.

The opening (original text)

I have long since made it a rule never to eat breakfast, and so, invalid though I am, it is an everyday thing for my stomach to growl as I wait impatiently for lunch. Today the noon gun has already sounded, and still no meal has come. By my pillow there is no book, no inkstone, nothing at all — not even a single sheet of newspaper left. There being nothing else for it, I lie propped on one elbow against the bedding and gaze vacantly out at the garden. The sky is clouded over, perhaps a lingering trace of the autumn gale two days ago. The row of some ten cockscombs, though battered by the wind, have righted themselves again and stand in a neat line of deep crimson heads. A single stalk of amaranth puts out its beautiful leaves, reflected against the white laundry hung out to dry. Something called a great hairy knotweed has grown absurdly tall, its pale red flowers spilling over the amaranth below.

The ending (original text)

The same three children have come back again outside my fence. This time they seem to be putting the kitten into the rubbish bin and delighting in tormenting it there. Before long the wife from the house across the way — the mother, that is, of the boy called Takō-chan — comes out and scolds him: "Takō-chan, you mustn't torment the cat like that. Torment it, and it'll come back to haunt you at night — let it go this instant." At this the boy called Takō-chan, his voice turning a little tearful, protests, "It wasn't me who caught the cat, it was Toshi-chan." The boy called Toshi-chan, caught out, falls silent too, and for a while things grow quiet. Suddenly the sun flashes bright across the tatami. The meal has come.
