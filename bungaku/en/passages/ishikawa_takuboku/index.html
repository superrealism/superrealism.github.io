Famous passages by Ishikawa Takuboku

Well-known lines from the works of Ishikawa Takuboku.

15 passages

A Handful of Sand

I work and I work, yet my life grows no easier — I stare at my hands.

A representative poem of the three-line format. A life that grows no easier however much one works is captured entirely in the single gesture of staring at one's own hands.

On the white sand of the beach of a small island in the eastern sea, tear-drenched, I play with a crab.

A poem placed at the head of the collection. Takuboku's representative tanka entrusts solitude and sentiment to the small scene of playing with a crab on a beach.

A day when all my friends seem better than I— I bring home flowers and share a tender moment with my wife

A tanka from "Songs of Loving Myself." A heart weighed down by comparison with friends turns toward the small comfort of bringing home flowers and spending time with one's wife. Three lines hold both a sense of inferiority and the warmth of everyday life.

Longing for the accent of home, into the crowds at the station I go to hear it

A tanka from "Smoke." The speaker goes to the station not to travel, but to hear the language of home. The figure listening amid the crowd conveys the homesickness of someone living in the city.

Carrying my mother on my back in jest, I wept at how light she was and could not take three steps.

A poem in which, the moment he carries his mother on his back as a joke, her lightness tells him of her age and hardship, and he weeps. One of Takuboku's representative poems, distilling deep feeling and regret toward his mother into a scene from everyday life.

The sadness of lifeless sand— rustling, it slips through my fingers when I grasp it.

The poem from which the collection's title, A Handful of Sand, derives. In sand that spills through the fingers however tightly one grasps it, it sees the fleetingness of a life that cannot be held.

Lying in the grass of Kozukata Castle, drawn up into the sky— my fifteen-year-old heart.

A poem about a day in his Morioka middle-school years when he slipped out of class and lay in the grass of the castle ruins. It vividly captures the openness of a fifteen-year-old heart, as if drawn up into the blue sky.

The sorrow of leaving my hometown as if driven out with stones never fades.

A poem on the experience of leaving his home village of Shibutami as if driven out. It sings that the pain of that parting—as though pelted with stones—never goes away.

The willows softly turning green on the banks of the Kitakami rise before my eyes, as if to bid me weep.

A poem of longing for home written in Tokyo. The willows softly budding on the banks of the Kitakami River rise before his eyes as if urging him to weep, singing of his growing homesickness.

Facing the mountains of home, I have nothing to say. How precious are the mountains of home.

A poem of the heart before the mountains of home. A simple, powerful poem that entrusts a wordless gratitude and nostalgia to a single phrase.

The Aoyagi-chō of Hakodate — how sad it is! My friend's love songs, the cornflowers.

A poem from “Unforgettable People.” Layering together his days in Hakodate, his friend's love songs, and cornflowers, it looks back with sad longing on a place of youth that will never return.

Once I prayed that every man who ever made me bow my head would die.

A poem from “Songs of Loving Myself.” He once prayed that everyone who had made him bow his head would die: a confessional poem that sets down, without concealment, the fierce hatred born of days of poverty and humiliation.

Sad Toys

When I draw breath, there is a sound that rings within my chest. A sound lonelier than the winter wind!

The opening poem of his second tanka collection, kept written up to the year before his death. A poignant self-observation, listening closely to the sound within lungs ravaged by disease.

Somehow, this year seems likely to bring good things. New Year's morning, clear and windless.

A poem on the bright mood of New Year's morning. Takuboku, suffering from illness and poverty in his last years, records a fleeting hope in plain words.

Jidai heisoku no genjō

The air that surrounds us young people no longer moves at all.

A passage from an essay written in 1910, as the arrests of the High Treason Incident went on, and never published in Ishikawa's lifetime. Expressing the suffocation of an age shut in by state power as stagnant air, it urged the young to awaken to their times.
