About this work

A prose poem Hara Tamiki published in Gunzō in 1949, crystallizing his experience of the bomb at Hiroshima in poetic language quite different from that of his central work "Summer Flowers," and something like a last song. The narrator, who has survived held up by the countless laments of the dead, tells himself to live precisely in order to answer those laments, looks at the faces of the dead beyond the ruins, and tries to endure an unendurable life. Counted among the high points of atomic-bomb literature: a piercing requiem.

Summary

"Do not live for yourself. Live only to answer the laments of the dead" — through the consciousness of "I," who has survived the calamity of the bombing, beautiful words and thoughts flow ceaselessly, together with the laments of the dead. The terrible days of walking through the burned ruins, supporting wounded soldiers, wandering between fire and water. With not a single grain of rice in his stomach, his body grown almost transparent, still "my" legs kept carrying "me" onward. What kept "I," asking the starry sky whether it was possible to go on living, alive at all, was the lament of countless dead. They were stars; they were flowers; they were things known from a time long, long past. Pierced through by that lament, having lost any place to return to, cast out from the world and still cast further out, "I" try to endure enduring and enduring and enduring. And then — tomorrow, the sun will rise again, flowers will bloom overflowing on the earth, and small birds will sing out clear and bright. "Earth, O earth, be forever beautiful, forever overflowing with feeling." A requiem for the dead, and a prayer of one who still means to go on living — Hara Tamiki's piercing prose poem, distilled from this, stands among the high points of atomic-bomb literature.

The opening (original text)

Beautiful words and thoughts flow almost without a break. They well up out of a gap in the clouds of the deep sky and come flying toward me. How many years is it now since I last slept? My eyes are stretched taut and my lips are dry. Even breathing feels like starvation itself in this reeling, unsteady space — is this, too, truly somewhere within the universe?

Chinkonka by Hara Tamiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

O lament, O lament, pierce me through. Pierce me through, who have lost any place to return to. Pierce me through, cast out from the world. Tomorrow, the sun will rise again and flowers will bloom overflowing on the earth; tomorrow, small birds will sing out clear and bright. Earth, O earth, be forever beautiful, forever overflowing with feeling. Tomorrow I will pass through it, filled with feeling.

Chinkonka by Hara Tamiki (Aozora Bunko)
