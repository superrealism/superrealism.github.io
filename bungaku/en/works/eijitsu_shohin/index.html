About this work

A collection of more than twenty short pieces serialized in the Tokyo and Osaka Asahi Shimbun from January to March 1909. "New Year's Day," "The Cat's Grave," "The Brazier," "Mr Craig" and the rest set down, in the sketching manner, ordinary scenes of daily life, memories of childhood and recollections of his years studying in London. Different in flavour from the long novels, they are loved for Sōseki's light, well-flavoured eye, his humour, and the passing feeling that shows through.

Summary

Beginning with the scene of callers on New Year's Day, the book sets out more than twenty short pieces, each standing on its own like a small window: "The Cat's Grave," which records the death of the cat he kept in a level voice; pieces that follow memories of childhood; and recollections of his student days in London. Best known among them is "Mr. Craig," a portrait of the Shakespeare scholar who gave Sōseki private lessons while he was abroad — a strange and single-minded man. Sōseki remembers with both amusement and regret this old scholar who meant to spend his life compiling a Shakespeare dictionary and died without ever finishing it. In these pieces, which catch the small events and movements of a mind day by day in an unforced sketching prose, one glimpses the softer face of Sōseki that lies behind the great novels. They are still widely read as fine examples of the modern Japanese essay.

Characters

  • I (Sōseki)　The narrator, who sets down small everyday scenes and memories of childhood and of his student days abroad in a sketching prose
  • Mr. Craig　The Shakespeare scholar who gave Sōseki private lessons in London, and who died without finishing the dictionary of a lifetime

The opening (original text)

New Year's Day After eating my rice cake soup I withdrew to the study, and before long three or four people came. All of them young men. One of them was wearing a frock coat. Perhaps because he was not used to it, he seemed oddly deferential toward the melton. The rest were all in Japanese clothes, and in their everyday ones at that, so that there was nothing of the New Year about them. This company looked at the frock coat and said, one after another, "Well!" — proof that they were all surprised. I too said "Well," last of all.

Eijitsu shōhin by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

About two years after I came back to Japan, a newly arrived literary magazine carried a notice that Mr. Craig had died. Two or three lines had been added to say that he was a specialist in Shakespeare. I laid the magazine down and thought: so that dictionary was never finished after all, and has come to nothing.

Eijitsu shōhin by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)
