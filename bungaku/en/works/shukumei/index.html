About this work

A collection of prose poems Hagiwara Sakutarō selected himself late in life. Beginning with a preface asking what a prose poem is, it gathers writing that moves between poetry and thought — from fantasies in the manner of "The Octopus That Would Not Die" and "Cat Town" to aphoristic fragments such as "Father," "Enemy" and "Grave." A great feature is the author's own notes at the end for each piece, in which he discloses where the ideas came from and what he was aiming at. Lyric poems from Ice Island and The Blue Cat are also included in the latter half.

Summary

In "On the Prose Poem," set at the head of the book, Sakutarō, citing Baudelaire and Turgenev, defines the prose poem as a literature which, without keeping to meter, has musical rhythm and the beauty of art, and which in content holds rather much of the essay and of philosophy. In Japan it is the Makura no Sōshi and the Hōjōki that answer to it, but since the Meiji era that tradition has been broken — and having said so, he declares that he will gather into one volume the poems of thought and the aphorisms he has gone on writing alongside his lyrics. The text begins with "Ah, breaking the hard ice," "A Woman and Rain," "On the Lawn," and there follow countless fragments: "The Octopus That Would Not Die," "The Post Office," "The Sea," "The Grave," "The Life of the Gods," "Father," "Sad Is It When a Poet Dies." In a whaler driving through the broken ice he sees the sharp point of will; upon rain as fine as feeling he lays the figure of a woman; in the octopus that eats itself up in its tank and still lives on he sees an everlasting hunger. Upon the gods enshrined in little shrines in the back alleys of the city he lays the modest prayers of poor and good people. And in the "Author's Notes on the Prose Poems" at the end he speaks frankly of the origin of each piece, of his interest in surrealism and in psychoanalysis, and of the difficulty of writing poems of thought in Japanese.

The opening (original text)

On the prose poem In place of a preface What is a prose poem? The inventor of that literature in the modern West is commonly said to be Baudelaire; and by him a prose poem means a piece of writing which, disregarding fixed laws of meter and written in a free prose form, has nevertheless a high musical rhythm throughout and a strong fragrance of the beauty of art.

Shukumei by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"All things perish with the years" — the scenes of my native place in this piece are all taken from my old work, "Poems of a View of My Native Place." "Poems of a View of My Native Place" is included in my third collection, "Pure Feelings: A Book of Little Songs"; and several pieces from it have been selected again for the lyrics in the latter half of this book.

Shukumei by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)
