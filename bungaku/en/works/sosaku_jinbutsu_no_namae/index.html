About this work

How does one name the characters in a novel? The charm of The Golden Demon, Yumeno Kyūsaku says, lies in the contrast of Kan'ichi and Omiya. An essay in which a writer of detective fiction sets out, half grumbling, the trouble of choosing names and the four conditions imposed on him in doing it.

Summary

It is something every writer of novels goes through, Yumeno Kyūsaku begins. A character in a story and his name are settled almost at the same moment. And yet choosing that name is by no means easy. Once one's nerves grow extremely sensitive to it, it starts to seem as though the whole success or failure of a work hangs on the choice of name alone. Isn't the whole charm of The Golden Demon in the contrast between Kan'ichi and Omiya? Doesn't the very life of Nami-ko live on forever in those eight characters, Kawashima Takeo and Kataoka Namiko? In detective fiction especially, a protagonist's name matters a great deal. Akechi Kogorō, Homura Sōroku, Sherlock Holmes... the mere appearance of the name on the page sets a reader's blood stirring. From here, Kyūsaku sets out four conditions. It must fit the character's personality and appearance exactly. Either the surname or the given name alone should conjure up an image of the person. It must be easy for the reader to remember. And, if a real name is used for a villain, it becomes impossible to make it right with everyone across Japan who shares that surname. He mixes in self-mockery too, of the two hours wasted opening dictionaries and encyclopaedias only to land on "potato" or "bacterium." And yet a name chosen carelessly can, by the time one has written fourteen or fifteen pages, become impossible to separate from the character, so that changing it forces one to rewrite the whole thing — the essay closes with the discovery that the author, it seems, carries the plot forward on the very feeling he gets from a name.

The opening (original text)

This is not limited to detective fiction. Anyone who writes novels, I imagine, has gone through the same thing. Whatever the writer, I think that, in any novel, the moment a protagonist, a supporting character, or a minor character is settled on, and the moment that character's name is settled on, come almost at the very same instant.

Sōsaku jinbutsu no namae ni tsuite by Yumeno Kyūsaku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

It seems I have gone and lined up nothing but contradictory statements here, but I have never once seen a review of a novel that discusses the naming of its characters. And yet, thinking it over, the naming of characters in a work of fiction does strike me as, without question, a fine art in its own right — and so, as a mere opening word on the subject, I have gone ahead and published this foolish little piece.

Sōsaku jinbutsu no namae ni tsuite by Yumeno Kyūsaku (Aozora Bunko)
