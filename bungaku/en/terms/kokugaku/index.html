What is Kokugaku (National Learning)?

Category
Other

A field of scholarship that arose in the Edo period, seeking to read the Japanese classics through rigorous, evidence-based methods and to find in them a native Japanese spirit. It is distinguished by rejecting the practice of interpreting the classics through the lens of Confucianism or Buddhism, dismissed as "karagokoro" (a Chinese-influenced mind), and insisting on a return to the text of the "Kojiki," the "Man'yōshū," and other works themselves. The monk Keichū opened a philological method for annotating the "Man'yōshū," and, through Kada no Azumamaro and Kamo no Mabuchi, it was brought to its culmination by Motoori Norinaga. Norinaga spent thirty-five years completing the "Kojikiden" in 1798, and in "Genji Monogatari Tama no Ogushi" argued that the essence of "The Tale of Genji" lay not in Confucian or Buddhist moral instruction but in knowing "mono no aware," freeing literature from being merely a vehicle for morality. Later Hirata Atsutane strengthened the Shinto dimension of the discipline, and his thought reached into the sonnō-jōi (revere the emperor, expel the barbarians) movement of the shogunate's final years. The textual editing and commentary accumulated by kokugaku became the foundation of modern scholarship on Japanese literature after the Meiji era, and its scholarly lineage flowed into the folklore studies of Yanagita Kunio and Orikuchi Shinobu as well. Norinaga's stance of seeing literature not as a tool of politics or morality but as beauty in its own right also resonates, distantly, in the modern view of literature.
