About this work

An essay Kume Masao published in two parts in Bungei Kōza in 1925, and the piece placed at the starting point of Japanese I-novel theory. He declares flatly that the I-novel is the true road and the essence of prose art, and raised a storm in literary circles with the bold claim that War and Peace, Crime and Punishment and Madame Bovary alike are no more than great popular novels. At the same time he argues for what he named the settled state of mind — a firmness of stance — as the thing an I-novel needs if it is not to sink into mere confession. The argument about a peculiarly Japanese form began with this essay.

Summary

In the literary world of the day the merits of the "state-of-mind novel" and the "orthodox novel," of the I-novel and the third-person novel, were being argued back and forth. "State-of-mind novel" is a name Kume himself gave provisionally to a novel that tries to express, rather than making its object appear as it is, the "feeling" of the moment in which it is looked at. Kume begins from his own definition of the "I-novel." It is not a translation of the German Ich-Roman. It is not a matter of the form, of whether the hero calls himself "I," but a "self-narrating" novel in which the writer has laid himself bare most directly. Yet it must not be a mere autobiography or confession; it must be a novel, that is, art. Rousseau's Confessions is not an I-novel, but Strindberg's A Madman's Defence plainly is. Sōseki's I Am a Cat he would rather not admit, because it puts one twist of the frame of a cat upon it — he loves Inside My Glass Doors better, but unhappily that was not a novel. Kikuchi Kan's Keikichi stories, though in the third person, are plainly representative I-novels; and there are Shimazaki Tōson's Shinsei, Tayama Katai's Zansetsu, Tokuda Shūsei's Kabi, and Masamune Hakuchō and Chikamatsu Shūkō and, consistent from first to last, Kasai Zenzō — looking round, every Japanese writer writes I-novels. Why is this the true road? "I cannot in any way believe that art is the creation of another life," says Kume. Art is nothing but the "reproduction" of the one life a man has walked. The moment it is entrusted to another person there comes a kind of indirectness and a sense of fabrication, and though it may be the better reading it cannot be trusted. Therefore "everything except the I-novel is popular fiction." However fine an artificial flower may be, it is not equal to one stem of a wild one. In the second part he answers misunderstandings and carries the argument further. He is not advocating or advertising the I-novel, nor laying down a rule. To the objection, "What is the good of setting down honestly the life of a dull person?" he answers that to make the life of a dull person gorgeous and complicated in the manner of the orthodox novel is just as dull. Every existence is reasonable. If everyone were blessed with the power of expression, the true thing would be for each to write one I-novel and leave it behind at his death. The question hangs on the condition "if he can express it as it is." And "as it is" does not mean the raw material as it stands. There must always be a condenser — a "state of mind" without error, which harmonizes and filters and concentrates the "I" and brings it forth whole again. This is the second stage of his argument. A state of mind is, in common speech, a firm seat, a sureness of standing ground. From there, however and wherever one looks, one can always be oneself without mistake. By the addition of this state of mind the I-novel draws a fine line between itself and confession, and receives the crown of art. An I-novel without it is a wastepaper novel of life, a pickle-mash novel, no more than boasting of one's happiness or grumbling. "This 'I-novel, state-of-mind novel' is not to be swayed by the whim of a writer or the tendency of an age; it is the home of the novel, from which one sets out and to which at last one returns" — and with that Kume closes, hoping that those who mean to write will not waste extra pains on the question of first and third person but will settle with an easy mind upon the I-novel and the state-of-mind novel.

The opening (original text)

1 Of late a part of the literary world has been advocating what is called the state-of-mind novel; and against it there are those who insist to the last upon the orthodox novel, and the two will not give way to each other; and along with that, the merits of what is called the I-novel and of the third-person novel have often been argued. What is called the state-of-mind novel is in fact a name I myself gave to it provisionally. Of its deeper purport I shall speak in another chapter; but to put it in a word here, it is a novel in which, when the author describes his object, rather than making that object appear as it is — no, it may appear as it is, but along with that — he tries chiefly to express what may plainly be called the "feeling" of the moment, or, in harder words, the reflection upon life with which he looks at it.

The "I" Novel and the "State of Mind" Novel by Kume Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Last of all I say it again: this "I-novel, state-of-mind novel" is not to be swayed by the whim of a writer or the tendency of an age; it is the home of the novel, from which one sets out and to which at last one returns. Therefore I hope that those of you who mean hereafter to write, if you have read even this rambling piece to the end, will believe at least that much, and, without spending extra pains afresh on the question of first and third person that so tormented the writers of the past, will settle with an easy mind upon the "I-novel" and the "state-of-mind novel."
