About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Kōdan Zasshi in 1947. A shabby traveller washes up at the Kashiwaya inn at Hamamatsu in Tōtōmi. Around a young man called Sōkichi and a maidservant called Otaki, the question of how a person should live rises quietly. A piece of Yamamoto's characteristic writing about human feeling, drawing a young man waking to what it means to live through his contact with the sacrifice and the sincerity of others.

Summary

At the edge of the town of Hamamatsu, in the province of Tōtōmi, there was an inn called the Kashiwaya. Once a small lodging house, it had, thanks to a cook with a curiously fine touch with the knife, gradually come to seem more like a proper restaurant. On an evening during the rainy season, a shabby traveller, however it happened, wandered in and took off his straw sandals at the Kashiwaya — and around this traveller, the story begins to move. A young man named Sōkichi, and a maidservant named Otaki. And the way of life of a man named Genojō, and of his parents, comes to shake Sōkichi's heart deeply. Genojō had not died for his own sake; his parent, though stripped of his stipend, harbours neither regret nor resentment over it — indeed, says he has nothing left to regret at all. Learning this, Sōkichi trembles. "This is how one has to live. This is how a human being has to live." The breadth of the world, the depth of the way people can live their lives, begins to come dimly into view for Sōkichi. "I'm glad I met you, Otaki. Let's do this together, from now on. This world's worth every bit of trouble it puts you through." Feeling some great power welling up and filling his whole body, he smiles quietly to himself. A piece of Yamamoto's human-feeling writing, drawing a young man's rebirth through contact with the sincerity and the way of life of others.

Characters

  • Sōkichi　A young man awakening to the meaning of life
  • Otaki　A maidservant at the Kashiwaya

The opening (original text)

At the edge of the town of Hamamatsu, in the province of Tōtōmi, there was an inn called the Kashiwaya. It had begun as a branch of the Kashiwaya Magobei, one of the leading inns in the castle town, a very small lodging house at first; but with a cook of a curiously fine touch, it drew a following among the wealthy merchants of the castle town and well-off people from the surrounding area, and naturally came to take on the shape of a proper restaurant.

Kin gojūryō by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The breadth of the world, the depth of the way people can live their lives — it seemed that Sōkichi had now begun to see these things, dimly at least. Feeling some great power welling up and filling his whole body, he smiled quietly with his eyes and murmured: "I'm glad I met you, Otaki. ...Let's do this together, from now on. This world's worth every bit of trouble it puts you through."

Kin gojūryō by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
