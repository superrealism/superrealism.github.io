About this work

Higuchi Ichiyō's first published story, printed in the first issue of the magazine Musashino in March 1892. Written under the guidance of her teacher Nakarai Tōsui, it follows Chiyo and Yoshinosuke, who grew up next door to each other, as childhood affection turns without their noticing into a love no one else can see, and Chiyo falls ill and dies without ever saying what is in her heart. In its court-style lyricism, laying a girl's life over cherry blossom, one can already see the beginnings of everything she would write.

Summary

The Nakamura and the Sonoda stand eave to eave with only a middle fence between them, and Ryōnosuke of the Sonoda and Chiyo, the only daughter of the Nakamura, have grown up like brother and sister. At sixteen, an idle remark from a friend teasing her about Ryōnosuke makes Chiyo see that her heart has gone beyond the affection of a childhood companion. Unable to speak of it, she feels it more every day; she begins to eat less and takes to her bed. Her mother, alarmed at her illness, turns to prayers; Ryōnosuke calls often to ask after her, never dreaming that hers is a sickness of love. Every visit throws her heart into confusion, and the illness only grows worse. At dusk, at the height of the cherry blossom, Chiyo knows that her time has come and asks Ryōnosuke, at her pillow, to go home — a girl's wish that he should not be troubled over her even at the last. As he steps outside the screen, a voice thinner than a thread calls him back: "My apology tomorrow." On a windless evening the cherry blossoms at the eaves scatter, and the sound of a bell rings sadly in the darkening sky.

Characters

  • Chiyo　The only daughter of the Nakamura house. A girl of sixteen who sinks into illness unable to speak of her love for her childhood companion
  • Ryōnosuke　The young man of the Sonoda house next door, who thinks of Chiyo as a sister and never notices her love
  • The mother　Who is anxious at Chiyo's illness and turns to prayers

The opening (original text)

Divided only by a middle fence of bamboo in the Kenninji style, sharing the water of the garden well they draw for each other, clear and deep at the bottom, with one plum tree at the eaves that shows the spring of both houses and whose fragrance they divide between them, there stand two houses called the Nakamura and the Sonoda. The master of the Sonoda died the year before last, and the heir is Ryōnosuke, a young man of twenty-two, a student attending some school or other.

Yamizakura by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Because it grieved her to give him trouble even at her last hour, he had gone two steps beyond the screen when a voice thinner than a thread called after him, "Ryō-san"; and when he turned to ask what it was — "My apology tomorrow." On the windless eaves the cherry blossoms fell scattering down, and in the sky of the evening dark the sound of a bell rang sadly.

Yamizakura by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)
