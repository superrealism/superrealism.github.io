About this work

What Kyōichi saw as he walked beside the railway line on a moonlit night was a great host of telegraph poles in zinc caps, marching along hopping on one leg — a piece by Miyazawa Kenji from the collection The Restaurant of Many Orders. A marching song of nonsense syllables and a puffed-up old man who is the electricity chief make a night scene at once comic and eerie.

Summary

One evening, Kyōichi, in straw sandals, was walking briskly along the flat ground beside the railway tracks. A nine-day moon hung in the sky, and scaly clouds spread across the whole heavens. Just as he came to where the red and purple lights of the station looked like some great castle, the signal post on his right let its arm drop with a clank. That alone would have been nothing strange. But what came next was another matter entirely — the row of telegraph poles that had been humming and groaning on the left side of the tracks all at once, with great swagger, set off marching north. Each wore six porcelain insulators like epaulettes and a zinc cap topped with a wire spear, hopping along on a single leg. And as they passed, they eyed Kyōichi sideways, as though mocking him outright. Before long a marching song rang out — "Dock-dedde, dock-dedde" — and there appeared a small old man who called himself the Chief of Electricity. The old man scolded the telegraph poles, directed their march, and, full of pride, explained to Kyōichi the workings of the electrical world. But then a train came. "Ah, no good — the train's coming. It'll be terrible if someone spots us," the old man cried, shouting "Whole army, close ranks — hup!" and the telegraph poles snapped to a halt and returned to their ordinary places. Seeing that the lights in the passenger car were out, the old man muttered, "Outrageous," and crawled in beneath the moving train. Just as Kyōichi tried to stop him, crying out in alarm, the windows of the car flashed bright, and a small child raised a hand and called out, laughing, "It got bright! Hooray!" as it went by.

The opening (original text)

One evening, Kyōichi, wearing straw sandals, was walking briskly along the flat ground beside the railway tracks. This was, to be sure, an offense punishable by fine. What was more, had a train come along with some long pole sticking out of its window, he would have been struck down and killed on the spot. But that evening, no track inspector came along, nor did he meet any train with a pole poking from its window. Instead, he saw something truly strange.

Tsukiyo no denshinbashira by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Look out!" Kyōichi cried, trying to stop him — but just then the windows of the passenger car flashed bright, and a small child raised a hand and called out, laughing, "It got bright! Hooray!" as the train went by. The telegraph poles hummed softly, the signal clanked up into place, and the moon slipped back in among the scaly clouds. And the train, it seemed, had already reached the station.
