About this work

A short story Kunikida Doppo published in 1902. Having lived through the upheaval of the Restoration without ever quite riding the tide, unable to become either a baron or a prefectural governor, he has withdrawn to his home country to grow old — and in that ill fortune the old Professor Tomioka lives as a stubborn, contrary oddity. Doppo draws with humour and pathos how a man left behind by his age delivered, even after his death, one unexpected rebuke to the world.

Summary

Tomioka Sensei is a celebrated eccentric old man, known to everyone in his corner of the country. Though he lived through the turmoil of the Restoration, some strange twist of fortune caused him to slip from the path of advancement; he became neither baron nor prefectural governor, and was left to retire to his native province and grow old and decrepit there. In this obscurity he lived out his years as a stubborn, willful, and arrogant "eccentric." Once possessed of both ambition and talent, yet never accepted by the world, Tomioka Sensei carried his resentment and frustration with him, clashing with those around him and spending a cantankerous old age. At last, at the end of November, Tomioka Sensei departs this world, and his native province loses another of its noted characters. Then, in two or three of the great Tokyo newspapers, there appears a large death notice, bordered in black and some twenty lines long, listing in a row the names of his disciples, relatives, and a viscount friend. Many who see it wonder in bewilderment just who this man could have been, to merit so grand an advertisement — but this, in fact, was the last great shout that the resentful Tomioka Sensei hurled out into the world before he died. Seeing this notice, which he had contrived through an acquaintance as one final release for his pent-up grievances, two or three of his countrymen who understood him were moved to tears. A character study in which Doppo, with humor and pathos, portrays the sorrow and pride of one stubborn man left behind by his age.

The opening (original text)

I can say no more than that this was once the domain of a certain duke — a domain that, as it happened, produced a good many of the new nobility, viscounts, earls, and barons alike. Yet for every one of them, there were no few men who, caught up in that same tumult of the Restoration, slipped by some strange turn from the ladder of advancement, and in the end could not so much as secure a prefectural governor's chair, let alone a barony, and were left to retire uselessly into their native province and grow old and decay there. It is precisely men of this sort who become eccentrics — stubborn, willful, arrogant — and Tomioka Sensei was assuredly one of them.

Tomioka sensei by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

At the end of November, Tomioka Sensei at last departed this world, and his native province lost one of its noted characters. In two or three of the great Tokyo newspapers there appeared a large death notice, some twenty lines long, bordered in black, in which the names of his disciple Takayama Bunsuke, his relative Hosokawa Shigeru, his friend Viscount Nogami, and others, were all set out in a row. His countrymen, seeing this notice, nodded at once and said, "So the old master has finally died" — but of the many who merely read the newspapers, some wondered in puzzlement who on earth this man could be, to warrant so large an advertisement, while others took no notice of it at all. Yet this notice was the last great shout that Tomioka Sensei ever hurled out into the world — the one small release, procured through the offices of an acquaintance, for a man who had died with his heart still full of unspent grievance. Two or three of his countrymen who understood him wept when they saw it.

Tomioka sensei by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)
