What is Shaseibun (Sketch-Based Prose)?

Category
Form / Genre

Prose that extends to prose composition the principle of "shasei" (sketching from life) that Masaoka Shiki applied to reforming haiku and tanka. Its starting point is "Jojibun" ("Narrative Prose"), which Shiki, from January 1900, published in three installments in the newspaper "Nippon," setting out the method of writing plainly and without embellishment when a scene one has seen strikes one as interesting, so that the reader too feels the same interest. That same year, a writing circle called the "Yamakai" ("Mountain Gathering") began at the bedridden Shiki's home — the name deriving from Shiki's own saying that "a piece of writing must have a mountain, a central point" — and a column for sketch-based prose submissions was also set up in "Hototogisu," gathering a diverse array of writers: teachers, students, and company employees. It was carried forward into Shiki's own "Byōshō Rokushaku" ("A Six-Foot Sickbed"), Takahama Kyoshi's "Ikaruga Monogatari" ("The Tale of Ikaruga") and "Haikaishi" ("The Haikai Master"), Nagatsuka Takashi's "Tsuchi" ("The Soil"), and the essays of Terada Torahiko, and Natsume Sōseki too stepped from this current into fiction with "Wagahai wa Neko de Aru" ("I Am a Cat"), which had been read at the Yamakai. Shimazaki Tōson's "Chikuma-gawa no Suketchi" ("Sketches of the Chikuma River") is likewise a fine example of sketch-based prose. Unlike the confessional voice of Naturalism, it brought to modern prose an objective, light-touched style that views its subject at a certain remove. Shimamura Hōgetsu, in "Ima no Shaseibun" ("Sketch-Prose Today"), while calling sketch-based prose formless and unfinished, judged it the best training ground for young people aspiring to fiction or drama.
