About this work

A representative short story by Ōsaka Keikichi, published in "Profile" in 1934. It takes the form of a tale told to a university student by a former engine driver who shares his train. In the roundhouse at H station stood D50 No. 444, which had run more than twenty people down and was known as the "funeral locomotive." After every accident the driver "Osa-Izumi" and his fireman Sugimoto would hang a wreath in the cab. Then, from one winter on, this engine begins to meet with a very strange kind of fatal accident, and to meet with it again and again. An impossible situation set down in the middle of everyday life, unraveled by pure reasoning and landing at last on a truth of great pathos: a gem of prewar orthodox detective fiction.

Summary

In the fan-shaped roundhouse at H station stood a large freight engine the staff called the "funeral locomotive": D50 No. 444. Built in 1923, it had a record of more than twenty people run down and killed. And by ill luck, on every one of those occasions the crew had been the same two men — the driver Nagata Senzō, known as "Osa-Izumi," and his fireman Sugimoto Fukutarō. From the rainy night when they ran down a madwoman of forty under the overbridge, the two had taken to hanging a cheap wreath from the cab roof for the forty-nine days after each death, to comfort the spirit of the victim, and that thoughtfulness became well known among their fellows. Then, one winter two years ago, the engine met with a string of strange accidents. On a morning of hard frost Sugimoto came back into the cab pale and took off his cap to wipe the soot from under his nose — his signal that pieces of a body had caught in the wheels. Yet when they cleaned the engine there was no trace of blood anywhere. The uncanny killings repeated themselves as if stamped from a die. The coffin shop Jippōsha in the town, where the wreaths were ordered, and the lame girl who lived shut up inside it. All of it is made clear by a single letter written on the night of the seventeenth of March.

Characters

  • Nagata Senzō (Osa-Izumi)　Driver of D50 No. 444, who hangs a wreath in the cab after every accident — the man telling the story
  • Sugimoto Fukutarō　The fireman. When he finds pieces of a body he has a habit of wiping the soot from under his nose
  • Toyo　The daughter of the coffin shop Jippōsha. Illness has swollen both her legs hideously, and she lives looking out from the inner room
  • The master of Jippōsha　Toyo's father. He made the wreaths for his daughter's sake, and went every week to the town of B

The opening (original text)

— No, you are quite right. When the season turns fine like this, traveling by train is a very easy business…. And where are you bound for, sir?… Ah, Tokyo. So your university is in Tokyo as well…. Ah, is that so. Well, very good, very good…. Eh, myself? Ah, I am only going as far as H, just up ahead. Yes, that's right. The place with the engine sheds.

The ending (original text)

Well, young man — …I think you will understand now why I came to feel like leaving the railway, and why every year on the eighteenth of March — the anniversary of the death of the girl at Jippōsha — I go out like this to the public cemetery at H to visit a grave…. Eh? Ah yes, of course… I am sure you worked it out long ago, but in fact I am "Osa-Izumi" of the "funeral locomotive" — Nagata Senzō…. Well, well, you have let me talk on a long while…. It seems we are drawing into H station…. I shall take my leave of you here.
