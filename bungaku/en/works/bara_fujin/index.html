About this work

A draft Ranpo did not publish in his lifetime. With Aoyama Kōichi, a young man of twenty-one who has stolen his uncle's money and, at the end of dissipation, grown weary of the world, for its protagonist, a story with an air of decadence and crime opens — and breaks off unfinished. It is known too because Ranpo later reused this opening and developed it into Yōkyo, a relay novel by several writers: an interesting fragment.

Summary

Aoyama Kōichi, a young man, sits on the lawn of the park that was once Hama Rikyū, gazing absently at the steamships lying at anchor. He has stolen fifty thousand yen from his uncle's private hoard and spent it all lodging from one hot spring to another; his pockets now hold barely enough for tonight's lodging. With no intention of going back to his uncle's house or his place of work, the twenty-one-year-old broods on a "world not worth the living," even thinking as far as death. Before him, in time, the shadow of a mysterious affair and mysterious people begins to creep near. The story advances toward a tense scene concerning a woman named Sumie — and there the pen breaks off. This draft, never published in Ranpo's lifetime, closes with the two characters "unfinished," leaving thick in the air the psychology of a decadent young man and a foreboding of crime. Ranpo later reused this striking opening for Yōkyo, a relay novel written together with other authors. A valuable fragment offering a glimpse behind the scenes of a writer's creative process.

The opening (original text)

Aoyama Kōichi sat on the lawn facing the sea, in the park that had once been Hama Rikyū, gazing absently at the several steamships lying at anchor there. Behind him hung a huge, deep-red sun. All around, the colors of evening were visibly gathering. Perhaps because it was a weekday, there was no sign of anyone at all, save for a young couple passing by now and then. The fifty thousand yen he had stolen from his uncle's private hoard he had spent entirely in ten days of travel. In his pockets there remained only just enough money to cover tonight's lodging.

Bara fujin by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Sumie, it's me." This time he raised his voice a little louder. (Unfinished)
