About this work

"I," reading a life of Turgenev, remembers the old garden of the house where he was born in Koishikawa. A fox is said to appear at the foot of the bluff, where an old well and an aged willow stand among the undergrowth, and on a snowy morning his father, the houseboy and the firemen smoke it out of its hole and kill it. And then, because the fox had taken a chicken, those very chickens are killed as well and a drinking party begins. An autobiographical short story of Kafū's, which tells a cruel memory of childhood quietly and ends by saying that his doubts about trials and punishments were born there.

Summary

On a winter afternoon, "I" am reading a life of Turgenev when I remember the old garden of the house where I was born in Kanatomichō, Koishikawa. At the foot of the bluff of the house, built on a site bought up from the grounds of a bannerman's mansion, there was a deep old well that had never been filled in and a decaying willow, and to me as a child the night was a thing that welled up out of the bottom of that well. Ever since a robber got in, that corner of the garden was the terror of the whole household. On an autumn morning my father, out at his archery, cries that a fox has sprung out of the bamboo grass on the bluff, and Tazaki the houseboy goes about the garden with a carrying pole, but nothing is found. Oetsu the cook is scolded for saying that killing a fox brings bad luck on a house, and my mother and my nurse quietly threw out fried bean curd to keep it in good humor. In time the commotion was forgotten, and winter came. On a snowy morning in February the henhouse has been raided, and footprints are left in the snow. My father, the houseboy, the rickshaw man, the head of the firemen and the gardener all turn out, smoke the hole with sulfur, and kill the animal with one blow of a fireman's hook as it puts out its head. Before the quarry hung up on the carrying pole, I hid my face in my mother's sleeve at the sight of the blood. That afternoon, because the fishmonger's order would not come in time, the two chickens I had fed every day were killed, and the grown-ups drank until late. The people who killed the fox because it had killed chickens had gone on to kill the chickens themselves — and my thinking so as I fell asleep may have been, I write, the seed of my doubts about trials and punishments.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, looking back on the boyhood he spent at the house in Kanatomichō, Koishikawa
  • Father　An official of the Dajōkan period. He draws his great bow every morning, and takes command of the fox hunt
  • Mother　A gentle person who takes my part
  • Tazaki　A houseboy from my father's home province, who boasts that he will club the fox to death
  • Seigorō　The head of the firemen who come and go at the house, who kills the fox with a fireman's hook

The opening (original text)

The sound of fallen leaves running over the little garden, the noise of the wind shaking the paper doors. It is the afternoon in my winter study. At a window of dim light, such as makes one think of the dusk of an autumn field where one parted from a lover years ago, I was sitting alone against the brazier, reading a life of Turgenev. Turgenev, while still a child too young to know anything, wandering in the fearfully overgrown garden of his father's estate, saw one summer evening by an old pond thick with weeds a snake and a frog locked painfully together; and with a child's heart, which could not yet tell good from evil, he had already begun to doubt the mercy of God…. Reading that, I found myself remembering, without reason, the fearful old garden of my father's house in Kanatomichō, Koishikawa, where I was born. It was thirty years ago, in the days when the water of the aqueduct at Kohinata Suidōchō ran like a field stream among the dayflowers.

The ending (original text)

As I fell asleep that night I thought: why did those people hate the fox so? Because it had killed a chicken; and the people who killed the fox, because they had killed the fox, went on and killed two more chickens. Ah, Turgenev, from the struggle of a snake and a frog, came with a child's heart to doubt the mercy of God. That I, as soon as I began to read a little, came to doubt the meaning of what the world calls trials and punishments — perhaps that too had, without my knowing it, its cause in that fox hunt long ago, and in the memories of it.
