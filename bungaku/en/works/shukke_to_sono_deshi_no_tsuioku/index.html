About this work

A short recollection Kurata Hyakuzō, then in his mid-forties, contributed to the magazine Gekijō twenty years after writing his central work. He begins by writing that the play is a monument to his youth, and looks back on leaving the First Higher School at twenty-three, nursing his illness, and, after days of devotion and reflection, writing it at twenty-seven. It is also testimony about the performance history — the première at the Yūrakuza, the staging at the Imperial Theatre, Okada Yoshiko as Kaede — and he frankly sets down both his own judgment that it is certainly sentimental and his irritation at being asked for ever about the same work. An unadorned commentary by the author of a famous work.

Summary

Kurata writes that his youth was "certainly a thing of pure, burning ardor," and that he feels no regret for it. At twenty-three he left the First Higher School and, nursing his illness, moved from place to place — to the sea, to the mountains, to his native village — and at the end of all that, at twenty-seven, he wrote The Priest and His Disciples. Into it he poured, to the very brim, the anguish, the longing, and the religious sentiment of youth. Some call it sentimental, he admits, but he insists that a firm resolve — the search for something "unbreakable even by fate" — runs through the whole of it. And yet, since he knew nothing of "the world" in those days, its sweetness is certainly sweet. Is it not, he wonders, because people who have been ground down by the world find in reading that work a chance to recover, if only for a while, the innocence they have lost, that they have come to love it so? Even as he asks himself this, he says that his own further striving pushed him rather in the direction of coming to know reality, and led him, in time, to the play Beyond Shame. The latter half turns to the stage and the screen. The premiere was at the Yūrakuza, with Murata Minoru and later Yamada Ryūya playing Yuien, and Okada Yoshiko playing Kaede. The house was packed beyond capacity, but as a piece of theater it could hardly be called a success, and the author himself was full of complaints. Since it is a dialogue-driven play with little movement, he believes it ought rather to suit talking pictures. And in closing, he states plainly that to be forever asked for more works just like it is, however well meant, an unwelcome burden.

The opening (original text)

This play is the monument of my years of youth. In many senses, it carries with it a whole flood of memories. These days, when the work I long to do goes unaccomplished, and my energy and ambition sit heavy and unspent within me, I rarely find myself indulging in reminiscence over my youth — but since I am so often asked about it, this play does sometimes lead my thoughts back to those years. My youth was certainly a thing of pure, burning ardor. I feel no regret for it. Toward life, toward truth, toward love, I believe I lived, within the life I was given and the circumstances I was placed in, with the whole of my strength. At twenty-three I left the First Higher School, and, nursing my illness, moved from the sea to the mountains, to my native village, in and out of hospitals, and passed my days in devotion and in thought. And at twenty-seven I wrote that work.

The ending (original text)

That people should read it — young people especially — can never, I still believe even now, do any harm; it will surely, I think, purify and enrich the heart, and instill a feeling that reaches straight after what it seeks. But it is unreasonable to keep asking me, forever, to write works like The Priest and His Disciples. I am pursuing truth still, further mired in the dust of the world. Ground down by that world, coming to know reality, and, especially now, living amid poverty while also taking part in a national movement — and yet I do not wish to lose, for the whole of my life, my innocence and my idealism. Those who have read The Priest and His Disciples, I hope, will by all means also read Beyond Shame (published by Kaizōsha). As for myself, to be forever spoken of only in terms of The Priest and His Disciples is, to me, an unwelcome burden. I am a man ceaselessly striving onward, from one state of being to another. And at each turning point of that transformation, I have always written a major work. A true devoted reader is one who, for the author's sake, must be exactly that. (Published in Gekijō, Vol. 2, No. 1; December 7, 1936)
