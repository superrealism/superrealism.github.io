About this work

The record of what Asano Wasaburō, a pioneer of psychical research in Japan, said were communications from the spirit world obtained with his wife Takeko as medium. Kozakurahime, said to have lived on the Miura peninsula in the age of the warring states, tells in the first person of her life and of the training she undergoes in the world of the gods after death. Whether one believes it or not, an unusual book as a systematic account of the world after death in Japanese.

Summary

"This tale is, so to speak, one of the spirit-world communications carried out within our own household, and contains not the slightest exaggeration or admixture" — so begins the preface by the author's elder brother, Asano Masayasu, and his words set the tone for the whole book. The narrator is Princess Kozakura, who says she was a princess married into the Miura clan of the Miura Peninsula in the age of the Warring States. In the first half, her upbringing, her marriage, and the course of her life — down to the loss of her husband and clan in battle against the Hōjō forces, and her own death — are related together with the fine details of daily life in that age. In the latter half, the narrative shifts to the world beyond death: the confusion immediately after leaving the body, those who come to receive her, and the spirits suffering below in the lower reaches of the spirit world, still bound by their own attachments. In time the princess begins her training under a spirit who becomes her teacher, and learns of the workings of thought, of the work of saving others, and of the hierarchy and organization of the divine realm. Why are people born, and what becomes of them when they die? What manner of beings are the gods and buddhas? The princess's answers are concrete, and at times almost naively schematic. Toward the close, it is explained that the deities enshrined at Shinto shrines fall into two lineages, nature spirits and human spirits; that however excellent a human spirit may be as the principal deity, a powerful dragon god always stands in attendance behind it; and that above all these gods stands the Imperial Grandson, and beyond him, in the deepest place of all, Amaterasu Ōmikami. The distinctions among Kannon, Fudō, and the rest, too, the princess says, are made only by the lesser retainers dwelling in the lower reaches of the spirit world — in the deepest places, all are one. It is, she says in closing, much as there being many paths up Mount Fuji.

The opening (original text)

A Word Beforehand This tale is, so to speak, one of the spirit-world communications carried out within our own household, and it contains not the slightest exaggeration or extraneous matter. But because of the very nature of the material, my late younger brother took a rather cautious approach before venturing to make it public — going so far as to investigate, in person, the sites of the shrines named in the spirit communications, as well as the places of training described therein — and only after confirming, again and again, that none of it was fanciful invention, did he at last publish it in a magazine.

Kozakurahime monogatari by Asano Wasaburō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Besides these gods, this land also holds Kannon, and Fudō, and various other such beings — but as far as I have been able to investigate here myself, these are only differences along the way... that is to say, they are distinctions drawn only by the lesser retainers dwelling in the lower reaches of the spirit world, and in the deepest places, all of them are one and the same. Even in climbing Mount Fuji, there are many different paths one may take. I believe the paths of teaching, too, must be much the same sort of thing.

Kozakurahime monogatari by Asano Wasaburō (Aozora Bunko)
