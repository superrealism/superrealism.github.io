About this work

Terada Torahiko's central science essay, published in the magazine Kaizō in 1929. He places monsters as a masterwork of the culture born from the contact of human beings with nature. That people long ago explained the inexplicable phenomena of the natural world as the work of monsters was in fact a kind of working hypothesis, and a spirit connected, however roughly, with science was at work in it. Science has advanced and the old monsters have gone, but the mind that produces them lives on in changed forms — a light-handed and sharp argument about the human imagination and the understanding of nature, taking as its clue the monster that stands between religion, science and art.

Summary

Among all the various creations that human culture has produced, the "monster," Terada Torahiko begins by declaring, must be counted among its finest masterworks. The monster, too, is the legitimate offspring born of the contact between man and nature, and the world it inhabits is at once the world of religion, the world of science, and the world of art. There is no religion whose scriptures do not give monsters a role to play; without monsters, religion itself could not exist. People of old explained inexplicable natural phenomena like thunder and earthquakes as the work of monsters, but this was itself a kind of "working hypothesis" aimed at understanding such phenomena in a unified way, and within it already lay the seed of a scientific spirit. In time science advanced, and one by one the monsters' territories were explained away by the laws of nature, the old monsters dismissed as mere superstition. And yet Terada holds that the very working of the human mind that gives rise to monsters can never perish; it goes on changing its form to suit the age while preserving, eternally, the very same inner content. It is, rather, precisely within the humble spirit that knows science is not omnipotent that the existence of new "monsters" becomes permissible — and with the supple imagination of a scientist, Terada discusses the relationship between nature and humanity with wit and humor.

The opening (original text)

Among the various works invented and created along the path of human cultural progress, the "monster" must, I think, be counted among the finest masterworks of all. The monster, too, is the legitimate offspring born of the contact between man and nature, and the world in and out of which it moves is, on one side, the world of religion, and on another, the world of science. At the same time, it is also the world of art.

Bakemono no shinka by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

These humble words of a great and venerable master strike me as both a maxim directed at those superstitious enough to believe that present-day science must be able to explain everything, and, at the same time, a kind of sanction permitting the existence of what I have been calling "monsters." If that is so, then the monsters who have long been sealed away might now be free to walk about openly, heads held high — though this too may be nothing more than my own suspicious, monster-haunted interpretation. I can only wait for wiser heads than mine to enlighten me.

Bakemono no shinka by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)
