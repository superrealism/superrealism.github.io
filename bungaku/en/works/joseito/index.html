About this work

A short story published in the magazine Bungakukai in 1939. It follows a girl from morning to night in first-person monologue, through feelings and daydreams that shift from moment to moment. It is said to be worked up from a diary a reader sent him, and its fresh rendering of the fine-grained inner life of an adolescent girl has given it a particular standing among Dazai's works.

Summary

"I," who lost my father and live alone with my mother, begin the day in the bad temper of waking. What I see of people on the train, my exchanges with classmates at school, desultory feelings about kimono and spectacles — "I" set out frankly all the thoughts that rise in my mind and go again. Amid a mixture of complicated feelings toward a friend, thoughts of my dead father, and a vague unease and irritation at becoming an adult, in the evening "I" help about the house, look after my mother, keep my diary, and so end the day. Talking to the moon while doing the washing at the window, and murmuring that I am a Cinderella with no prince, "I" fall asleep, and the record of the day closes quietly there.

Characters

  • I　A schoolgirl who has lost her father and lives with her mother. She tells in the first person the movements of her heart through a single day
  • Mother　My mother, often ill, who watches over me quietly

The opening (original text)

The feeling of waking in the morning is interesting. As in hide-and-seek, when you have crouched perfectly still in the pitch dark of a closet and Deko-chan suddenly slides the door open with a rattle and the daylight comes flooding in and she shouts, "Found you!" — the dazzle, and then the odd awkwardness, and then your heart pounding as you pull your clothes together, and coming out of the closet a little embarrassed, and then all at once feeling angry and sick — that feeling; no, not that; not that feeling either. Somehow it is even more unbearable.

Schoolgirl by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The feeling of falling asleep is a strange thing. As one pulls hard on a line for a carp or an eel, something heavy, a weight like lead, pulls my head down by a thread; and when I begin to doze off it slackens the thread a little again. And then I come to myself with a start. Again it pulls hard. I doze. Again it lets out a little line. It repeats this three or four times, and then at last it gives one great pull, and this time until morning. Good night. I am a Cinderella with no prince. Do you know where in Tokyo I am? We shall not meet again.

Famous passages

The feeling, in the morning, when I first open my eyes, is a curious one.

The opening of a work that records a girl's day in the first person. In a light tone, as if speaking directly to the reader, it moves on into a wavering interior monologue.

Happiness comes one night too late.

Words that come to mind for the girl as she collapses into bed at the end of her day. A sense that happiness always arrives one step behind hangs faintly over the whole work.

Tomorrow, too, the same kind of day will come. Happiness will never come in my whole life. I know that. But it's best, I suppose, to go to sleep believing that it will surely come, that tomorrow it will come.

The monologue of the girl as she gets into bed at the end of her day. Knowing happiness will not come, she still tries to fall asleep believing in tomorrow—a fragile yet brave heart.

Good night. I am a Cinderella with no prince. Do you know where in Tokyo I am? We shall never meet again.

The ending. The girl's monologue of a single day, addressed to the reader throughout, closes with a self-portrait as a Cinderella without a prince and a light-footed farewell.

I like taking off my glasses and looking into the distance. Everything goes hazy, like a dream, like a peep-show picture — wonderful. I can't see anything dirty at all. Only the big things, only vivid, strong colors and light come into my eyes.

The monologue of the girl as she sits at her dressing table in the morning. Her feeling that the world blurred without her glasses is “wonderful” holds all the charm of this work's fresh, lively narration.
