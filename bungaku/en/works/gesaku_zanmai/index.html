About this work

A historical story drawing one day of Kyokutei Bakin, the late Edo popular writer known for The Eight Dog Chronicles of Nansō Satomi. Vexed by an unpleasant business at the bathhouse, a vulgar caller and small domestic matters, the old writer nevertheless passes in time into the rapture of composition — the trance of art — and the process is followed exactly. Akutagawa laid his own conviction about writing over the inner life of an artist caught between fame and reality, and the piece is often taken up as a statement of his art-for-art's-sake position.

Summary

In the autumn of Tenpō 2 [1831], the old writer Kyokutei Bakin, abused by a stranger at his usual bathhouse over The Eight Dog Chronicles, returns home in an unpleasant mood. Afterward, too, one visitor after another comes calling — a bookseller bringing a scheme for profit, a young popular writer seeking instruction, a vulgar man begging for money — and each in turn sets his mind astir. Wearing his nerves thin over the world's praise and blame, wavering between doubt and pride in his own work, Bakin keeps asking himself whether what he writes will last to later generations. Encouraged by the innocent words of his grandson Tarō, he shuts himself in his study, and in time, freed of distracting thoughts, reaches a state of self-forgetting in which the brush moves of itself — the trance of art. In that rapture, fame, the hardships of life, and every word of praise or blame are all forgotten, and only the joy of writing fills the old writer. Meanwhile his family only look on at Bakin, thinking him a tiresome man once again lost in his scribbling. In the study, as in the sitting room, the crickets go on singing out the autumn just the same.

Characters

  • Kyokutei Bakin (Takizawa Bakin)　An old popular writer at work on The Eight Dog Chronicles of Nansō Satomi. Suffers under the world's judgment, yet reaches the trance of creation
  • Ohyaku　Bakin's wife. Has little understanding of her husband's work
  • Sōhaku　Bakin's sickly son
  • Oji　Sōhaku's wife. Respects and supports Bakin
  • Tarō　Bakin's grandson. His innocent words encourage Bakin

The opening (original text)

It was a certain morning in September of Tenpō 2. At the Matsu-no-yu bathhouse in Kanda Dōbōchō, there were, as always, many customers from the morning on.

Gesaku zanmai by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

So said Ohyaku, looking at her son and daughter-in-law. Sōhaku, pretending not to hear, gave no answer. Oji, too, said nothing and went on plying her needle. The crickets, here as in the study, went on singing out the autumn just the same.
