About this work

An essay in which Nishida Kitarō set down his grief at losing a small child. His old friend Fujioka Sakutarō had also lost a daughter, and the piece begins from a scene in which the two, carrying the same grief, face each other with few words. It deepens into the purity of a parent's love, the cruelty of the world's consolations to resign oneself and forget, and the thought that to solve the question of death is the great business of a life: a fine piece in which the philosopher's feeling and his intellect dissolve into one.

Summary

Nishida sets down that, while grieving together with his close friend Tōho over the death of the latter's daughter, he too lost his own second daughter, then six years old, and found himself, if anything, comforted by his friend instead. Bearing the same grief, the two men, meeting again after some time, could not bring themselves to say even a single word about the child they had each lost, and only exchanged the lightest words of condolence — true sincerity, Nishida says, lies precisely in what cannot be put into words. A parent's love holds not the slightest trace of self-interest; it is simply, for no reason at all, dear. The world's consolations — that it was a girl, or that one has other children still — bring no true comfort, and the words urging one to resign oneself and forget are, for a parent, an unbearable pain; a parent, he writes, does not wish for this grief to pass at all. His thought then arrives, before the fact that one so dearly loved can turn, in an instant, to bare bone, at the conviction that to solve the question of death is the one great business of a human life; and turning regret into confession, and giving oneself over in devotion to some vast and overwhelming power, he closes with the thought that one may thereby apologize to the dead, and save oneself as well.

The opening (original text)

In the summer of Meiji 37, when Mr. Tōho returned to his home province with his family, he had a daughter named Mitsuko. She was a lovely, lively child, but the following summer, unexpectedly, he lost this child at his lodgings in Odawara, and I, calling to mind my own younger brother who had died in battle at Port Arthur the year before, did all I could to comfort him. And yet who could have guessed that this January, I myself would lose my own second daughter, only just turned six, and find myself, instead, the one being comforted by him.

Wagako no shi by Nishida Kitarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But in the end there is nothing for it but to resign oneself to fate. Fate works not only from without but from within as well. Behind our own failings, some mysterious power seems to hold sway; the feeling of regret arises precisely because we trust too much in our own power. In such a case, when we come to know deeply our own helplessness, and, casting ourselves aside, give ourselves over in devotion to some vast and overwhelming power, regret turns into confession, and the heart, as though relieved of a heavy burden, can save itself, and can also make its apology to the dead.

Wagako no shi by Nishida Kitarō (Aozora Bunko)
