About this work

A short story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Shōjo no Tomo in 1941. Set in the castle town of Kanazawa in Kaga, it draws how Orui, a girl who is a master of the hand drum and possessed by winning, comes through a meeting with an old man to understand what art really is. An art played in order to beat other people is not art at all — the teaching of a poor, shabby old man strikes the heart of a girl grown proud. The clear note of a drum that sounds only when competition is given up. A limpid piece singing the spirit of an artistic discipline.

Summary

Oroi, daughter of the foremost silk wholesaler in Kanazawa, Kaga province, is renowned even at fifteen as a master of the small hand drum. Beautiful but headstrong and proud, she throws herself fiercely into practice, determined to win first place at the upcoming Tsuzumi Kurabe — a grand contest of skill to be held before the lord of the Maeda house. One day, Oroi comes to know a poor, ailing old man. Though fallen on hard times, he is clearly no ordinary man, and he quietly speaks to Oroi, who thinks only of winning and losing, of what art truly is. A drum struck in order to defeat another, to triumph over someone else, is no true art no matter how skillful. Art is not something to be competed over; it must spring from a clear heart and pass straight into the hearts of others. Oroi comes to suspect that the old man is none other than the legendary master Kanze Ichinojō, who cast aside fame and vanished from the world. Deeply moved by his teaching, Oroi abandons her obsession with winning the contest and withdraws from the competition. Instead, she sits properly at the bedside of the dying old man and strikes her drum for him alone. Its clear, pure tone fills the room with a sound that can only be called solemn.

Characters

  • Oroi　Daughter of the foremost silk wholesaler in Kanazawa, Kaga province. A fifteen-year-old master of the hand drum, headstrong and proud. The protagonist
  • The Old Man　A poor, ailing man who is clearly no ordinary drummer. Teaches Oroi the true heart of art. Believed to be the master who cast aside his name, Kanze Ichinojō

The opening (original text)

The garden was filled with the warm light of a mild Indian-summer day. Amid the withering hedge of chrysanthemums, where most blooms had already faded into small, shrunken things, a single flower still showed its bright white petals, modestly peeking out from among the brown, curled leaves and stems. Oroi was playing her hand drum.

Tsuzumi kurabe by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

There was now no way to confirm whether the old man had truly been Kanze Ichinojō. But Oroi believed it firmly, and even if he had not been, that old man was, to her, her true teacher. The room was already dark. ...Having warmed the drum's skin over a fire she had sent for, Oroi sat properly at the old man's pillow, and for a moment closed her eyes as if to still her heart. ... "Iyaa —" With that cry, the drum resounded through the room with a clear, even solemn tone. ...Oroi began to strike out the piece known as "Otoko-mai."

Tsuzumi kurabe by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
