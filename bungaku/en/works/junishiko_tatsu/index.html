About this work

The piece for the year of the dragon, properly titled "The Story of Tawara Tōta Entering the Dragon Palace," serialized in three parts in Taiyō in 1916. The legend of Tawara Tōta Hidesato — asked by a great serpent at the Seta bridge in Ōmi to shoot the giant centipede of Mount Mikami, and invited to the dragon palace for it — is traced from the account in the Taiheiki, and for each of its elements he hunts out matching tales from all over the world. The piece in which Kumagusu's learning about dragon and serpent cults ranges most freely.

Summary

Of the legend of Tawara Tōta Hidesato's entry into the dragon palace, Kumagusu says that so far as he knows the version in the fifteenth book of the Taiheiki is the oldest complete one, and quotes it first. It is the tale of how Hidesato, thinking nothing of the great serpent lying across the Chinese bridge at Seta, stepped over it; and how that night, at the request of a messenger of the dragon palace who had taken the form of a beautiful woman, he shot dead with an arrow the great centipede that coiled seven times about Mount Mikami, and in reward was invited to the dragon palace and given a roll of silk and a rice bale that were never used up. Kumagusu takes the tale apart element by element and sets out one parallel after another from China, India and Europe: the boldness of stepping over the great serpent, the magical practice of wetting the arrow with spittle before shooting, the opposition of centipede and dragon, the motif of the inexhaustible bag. The talk extends to the belief that makes the dragon-serpent a token of woman, to traditions that men and dragon-serpents mated and begot descendants, and even to doctrines that worshipped the dragon-serpent as an image of the male and female organs. In closing Kumagusu says that this legend is no "wholesale lie made out of nothing at all," but that every part of it has its source and its ground; and he gives notice that the story of the snake will be written for the New Year number of the coming year of the snake, and asks his readers to wait.

The opening (original text)

The text of the story This tale, so far as I know, appears in its oldest complete form in the fifteenth book of the Taiheiki; and the passage that Bakin quotes in the second book of his Mukashigatari shichiya no kura, prefacing it with "a certain book says," seems also to have been taken from there.

Jūnishikō (tatsu) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In sum, the tale of Hidesato's entry into the dragon palace is no wholesale lie made out of nothing at all: every part of it has its source and its ground, as has been said above. Among them there is a tale of the killing of a monster at Nachi in a single stride, resembling the account of how Hidesato missed with his first and second arrows and brought the centipede down with the third. And as to the story in the Ōmi yochi shiryaku that Hidesato took a dragon woman to wife, in many countries from of old the dragon-serpent has been made the token of woman, and it is handed down that men and dragon-serpents mated and begot descendants.... All that I shall bring out as "The Story of the Snake" in the New Year number of the coming year of the snake, at which the demons laugh; so let my readers close the dragon's eye and raise the snake's head and wait.

Jūnishikō (tatsu) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
