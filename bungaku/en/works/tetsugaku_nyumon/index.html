About this work

"The doors into philosophy are everywhere" — Miki Kiyoshi's introduction to philosophy, which is not a survey of it. It is written from the position that neither a classification of doctrines nor a summary of his own views will do: unless philosophy teaches us to see rightly the reality we meet every day, however profound it looks it is so many empty words. With Nishida's philosophy behind him he advances through experience, language, action, technique and human relations, and closes by preaching not an ethic of sentiment but an ethic of responsibility.

Summary

There are gates into philosophy everywhere, and each of you may find his own according to his own situation; what is shown here is only one of those many gates — so the preface begins. An introduction to philosophy is not an outline of philosophy. It is neither the classifying and ordering of the doctrines in the history of philosophy nor the summarizing of one's own system. But philosophy, as the study of ultimate principles, must have unity, and this introduction too has a unity. What is presupposed here is Nishida's philosophy, so far as the author understands it. Every study must arise from a love of truth and must call up a courage founded on truth — and what is made especially clear in this book is the practical meaning of truth. ... In the body of the book, starting from the fact that we are placed within reality, he asks in order what experience is, how knowledge comes to be, what words and logic are doing, and in what way human action and technique and social relations are bound up with "truth." Again and again Miki insists that philosophy is not a contemplation cut off from life but a way of seeing reality rightly. At the close comes the problem of responsibility. Since action goes outside consciousness, it always produces social consequences. The doctrine of motive, which holds that an act is good if only the motive is good, is subjectivism and individualism, and is an error. Ethics must not stop at an "ethics of intention" but must be an "ethics of responsibility." And yet neither may it be a doctrine of consequences, which holds that all is well if only the result is good — we must act, says Miki, foreseeing as far as we can, by knowledge, the social consequences of our acts.

The opening (original text)

Preface There are gates into philosophy everywhere. Each of you will be able to find his own gate according to the situation in which he actually stands. What is shown here is only one of the many gates into philosophy. But by whichever gate you enter, if philosophy is unknown to you, you will need a guide. This book means to be one such guide.

Tetsugaku nyūmon by Miki Kiyoshi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Since the ethics of responsibility is to bear responsibility for the consequences of one's own acts, it cannot do without knowledge. We must act, foreseeing as far as we can, by knowledge, the social consequences of our acts. The ethics of responsibility gives weight to the consequences of an act; but it must not be the so-called doctrine of consequences, which holds simply that an act is good if only the result is good.

Tetsugaku nyūmon by Miki Kiyoshi (Aozora Bunko)
