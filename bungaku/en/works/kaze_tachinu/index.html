About this work

Hori Tatsuo's central work. Taking Valéry's line "the wind rises; we must try to live" as its theme, this novella follows the narrator's inner course as he keeps company with his fiancée Setsuko at a sanatorium in the highlands, in a clear, lyrical prose. It shows two people trying to find the fullness of life precisely inside a love with death close at hand, set against the turning of the mountain seasons. Its chapters appeared between 1936 and 1938, and it is held to be one of the summits of the Japanese lyrical novel.

Summary

The narrator has been spending a summer in the highlands with Setsuko, his fiancée, who paints; but her illness grows worse, and the two enter a sanatorium at the foot of the Yatsugatake mountains. In a place full of grave cases, surrounded by the shadow of death, he tries to take from each day spent with her a brightness and a happiness in living that he had never known before. The span the doctor gives her, Setsuko weakening little by little, and the two of them growing clearer in spirit even so — guided by Rilke's Requiem, he goes on looking for the meaning of truly living precisely in this time lived next to death. After he has seen Setsuko to her end, he shuts himself up alone in a hut in a deep-snow village among the mountains and turns over his own life inside a love that is gone. Looking at the light of his own hut burning in the snow-lit valley, he comes quietly to see that he is held up, and kept alive, by far more things than he had ever supposed.

Characters

  • I　A novelist, who keeps company with his fiancée Setsuko through her treatment and looks for the meaning of life in days lived beside death
  • Setsuko　The narrator's fiancée, who paints. Ill in the chest, she is treated at a sanatorium in the highlands
  • Setsuko's father　Anxious for his daughter, he watches over the two of them
  • The director　The doctor at the sanatorium, who tells the narrator how Setsuko stands

The opening (original text)

On those summer days, while you stood in the middle of a meadow thick with pampas grass, absorbed in your painting, I would always be lying in the shade of a birch tree at your side. And when evening came and you had finished your work and came over to me, we would stay a while with our hands on each other's shoulders, looking off toward the horizon, where great billowing thunderheads with their edges tinged madder red covered the sky. As though, out of that horizon just beginning to darken, something were on the contrary being born... On the afternoon of one such day (it was close to autumn by then), we had left your unfinished picture standing on its easel and were lying in the shade of that birch, eating fruit. Clouds like sand were streaming softly across the sky. Then, out of nowhere, the wind rose. Above our heads the indigo glimpsed between the leaves stretched and shrank. At almost the same instant we heard the sound of something falling flat in the grass. It seemed to be the picture we had left there, gone over together with the easel. You started up at once to go to it, but I held you back, unwilling to lose anything of that instant, and kept you beside me. You let me have my way. The wind rises; we must try to live.

The ending (original text)

So familiar has all of it become to me, this valley that people call the valley of happiness — yes, now that I have settled here and grown used to it, I feel I might almost call it that along with them... Here alone it is truly quiet, for all that the wind is making such a stir on the other side of the valley. Now and then, it is true, something creaks faintly just behind my hut; but that will be the dead branches of a tree touching one another in the wind that has barely reached this far. And sometimes what seems to be the last of that wind moves two or three fallen leaves over the others at my feet, with a small, dry, rustling sound...

Famous passages

The wind is rising; we must try to live.

Both the title and the theme of the work in a single line, drawn from a verse of Valéry. It takes up the irreplaceable time spent with a lover facing death as an act of will — the will to go on living.

In those summer days, while you stood painting intently in the meadow overgrown with pampas grass, I would always lie stretched out in the shade of a single white birch nearby.

The opening of the “Prelude.” The peaceful time of “you,” painting in a highland summer, and “I,” resting beside her, is depicted in a transparent light that foreshadows their later parting by death.
