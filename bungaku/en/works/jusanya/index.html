About this work

One of Higuchi Ichiyō's central stories, published in the special number for women writers of Bungei Kurabu in December 1895. A woman married to a well-off official, unable to bear her husband's treatment, goes home to ask for a divorce, but out of feeling for her child changes her mind — and on the way back to her husband's house meets, unexpectedly, the boy she grew up with. The resignation forced on women by the marriage system of the day is set quietly against the moon of the thirteenth night, in one of the best-loved of her works.

Summary

O-Seki, married to the official Harada Isamu, can bear her husband's daily coldness no longer. Putting her small son Tarō to sleep, she slips out of the house and goes to her parents to ask for a letter of divorce. In tears she tells them how she has been despised for having no education and shamed again and again in front of the servants; but her father, Saitō Kazue, admonishes her that once a woman has married it is a wife's part to endure, and that she would never be allowed to see Tarō again, and talks her into going back to her husband's house for tonight at least. Beaten by her love for the boy, O-Seki gives up the divorce, makes up her mind to live on as her husband's wife, and starts back through the night. She takes a cab she finds near her parents' house, and on the way the man pulling it suddenly sets down the shafts — and it is Rokunosuke, the tobacconist's son, her childhood friend, whom she once loved and who once loved her, though difference of station kept them apart. Meeting him again, come down in the world and reduced to pulling a cab, wrings her heart; but neither says much, and after a short while as companions on the road they part, one going east and the other south.

Characters

  • O-Seki　The heroine, married to an official, who suffers under her husband's treatment and goes to her parents to ask for a divorce, only to give it up for love of her child
  • Harada Isamu　O-Seki's husband. An able official, cold at home, who despises her at every turn
  • Saitō Kazue　O-Seki's father, who feels for his daughter and yet urges her back to her husband's house
  • Kōsaka Rokunosuke　O-Seki's childhood friend. Once a tobacconist's son, now come down in the world and pulling a cab

The opening (original text)

Usually it was the smart black-lacquered carriage, and at the sound of it stopping at the gate her parents would come out to meet her — is that not our daughter? — but tonight she had sent away even the cab she jumped into at the corner, and stood dejected outside the lattice door; and inside the house her father was talking in his usual loud voice: I too am one of the fortunate, you might say; both our children are docile and gave us no trouble in the raising, and people speak well of them; so long as a man does not thirst after more than his share, there is nothing further to wish for. Well, well, how grateful we ought to be.

The ending (original text)

When they came out on Hirokōji there were cabs; and O-Seki took some notes from her paper case, wrapped them prettily in a sheet of fine kogiku paper, and said: "Roku-san, this is truly rude of me, but do please buy yourself some tissue paper at least. Meeting you after so long, there seems to be a great deal I want to say, and I cannot get it out — please understand me. Well, I shall say goodbye. Take good care of yourself and do not fall ill, and set your aunt's mind at rest soon. I shall pray for you from a distance. Do become the Roku-san you used to be, and let me see you open a fine shop again. Goodbye, then." And Rokunosuke, taking the little packet, said: "I ought to make you a proper bow, but since it comes from your hand I shall accept it thankfully and keep it for a memory. I would say I am sorry to part, but if this is a dream there is no help for it. Come, go along. I am going home too. The roads are lonely when the night grows late." And he turned away, pulling his empty cab; and the one went east and the other south, the willows along the great street stirring in the moonlight, and her lacquered clogs sounding as though all strength had gone out of them — in the upstairs room at Murata's and in the inner rooms of the Harada house alike, there is much to grieve over in this world of ours.

Famous passages

Always before, it was the sound of a smart black-lacquered carriage stopping at the gate, and her parents coming out to meet the daughter they knew was home; but tonight she sent even the rickshaw she had hailed at the corner away, and stood dejected outside the lattice door.

An opening set on the night of the thirteenth, as Oseki, resolved on divorce, returns to her parents' home. The gap between this and her once-glorious days is conveyed in a single sentence.
