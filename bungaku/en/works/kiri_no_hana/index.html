About this work

Kitahara Hakushū's first collection of tanka. With poems such as the one asking the spring bird not to sing, as the sun goes down red on the grass outside, it holds a colloquial sensibility and a modern melancholy, and brought a new wind into the tanka of the late Meiji and Taishō years. The essay placed at the head, "Paulownia Flowers and Sponge Cake," is famous as a statement of Hakushū's aesthetics. The wound of his detention over an affair with a married woman — the prisoner Tonka John — shadows the whole book.

Summary

"The season of paulownia blossoms and castella cake has come round again" — so opens the short essay placed at the head of the volume, "Paulownia Blossoms and Castella." Whenever the paulownia blooms, Hakushū writes, he always recalls the cold, plaintive note of a flute. Upstairs at a Western restaurant in May, the pale violet of paulownia blossoms flickering in a thin glass of water, set against the warm yellow of castella cake — that castella, coarse and slightly austere to the touch, is what he loves best of all: its powdery freshness, the fresh touch of it, strikes pleasantly upon the delicate senses of the modern man, he explains. "Tanka is a small, ancient jewel of green — the very spirit of the sentiment of an old age of sorrow." The body of the collection that follows holds poems only a poet of just such sensibility could have written: the melancholy and decadence of the city, the scent of paulownia blossoms and summer grasses, love and the consciousness of sin. The sequence titled "Poems of Mourning" reflects directly the wound of the affair with a married woman for which Hakushū was charged with adultery and held at the Ichigaya remand prison. In the afterword, Hakushū writes: "My whole world stands defiled; all my dreams are on the verge of ruin. Even the joys and sorrows of my youth must, in the end, fade away like the pale violet paulownia blossoms of the fifth month." He names himself "the prisoner Tonka John, already a wounded traveler of the heart." The volume closes, dated to the early winter of 1912, with the words: "And what though this collection should ever see the light of the world? How far, how inconsolable, the road that this pilgrim still must walk."

The opening (original text)

To whom shall I dedicate this pitiful little collection of lyric poems To my kin, to my friends, to those I can never forget All of it, fragments of a dream once dear to my youth Tonka John Paulownia Blossoms and Castella The season of paulownia blossoms and castella cake has come round again. Whenever the paulownia blooms, I always find myself recalling the cold, plaintive note of a flute.

The ending (original text)

My whole world stands defiled; all my dreams are on the verge of ruin. Even the joys and sorrows of my youth must, in the end, fade away like the pale violet paulownia blossoms of the fifth month. To those who know my sorrow, I can do nothing but cast out the full measure of my warmth — for the prisoner Tonka John is already a wounded traveler of the heart. And what though this collection should ever see the light of the world? How far, how inconsolable, the road that this pilgrim still must walk.

Famous passages

Sing not, birds of spring, sing not — an evening when the sun sinks red, red on the grasses outside.

A poem placed near the opening of his first tanka collection. The archaic cadence of “sing not” and the red of the setting sun together set off the pathos of youth.

On the paving stones of the morning I send you home, crunch, crunch — O snow, fall like the scent of apples.

A poem from Paulownia Flowers. On the morning he sees his lover home, he calls on the snow falling on the paving stones to fall like the scent of apples. A representative tanka of Hakushū, in which sensuality and freshness live together.
