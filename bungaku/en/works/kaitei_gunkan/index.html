About this work

The adventure novel that Oshikawa Shunrō, at twenty-four, put before the world in 1900. A party attacked by pirates in the Indian Ocean and washed up on an uninhabited island meets there the underwater fighting craft the Lightning, which Captain Sakuragi of the Japanese navy has been building. Held to be the origin of Japanese adventure and science fiction, and a book that made boys wild with excitement.

Summary

On his way home from a tour of the world, "I" run into an old friend, Hamashima Takefumi, in the port of Naples, and, together with his family and Naval Captain Matsushima, who is awaiting new orders, board the ship Genjetsu-maru bound for Japan. The voyage passes gaily enough at first, with shipboard concerts and races on deck, but everything changes once the ship enters the Indian Ocean. Genjetsu-maru is attacked by a pirate vessel equipped with a diving apparatus and a searchlight for scanning the sea, and goes down. Left adrift in a small boat, "I," together with Hamashima's only son, the boy Hideo, and a handful of sailors, drift for days on end before at last washing up on a deserted island somewhere to the south. But the island is not, in fact, uninhabited. It proves to be the very island where Naval Captain Sakuragi, long given up as missing along with the thirty-seven sailors who vanished with him, has secretly built an undersea shipyard and is constructing a fearsome new weapon: the Denkō-tei, the "Lightning Boat" — a strange craft fitted with a triple ram, the newest model of torpedo, and an air-generating apparatus of its own. The island is named Asahi-jima, and its possession is formally declared in accordance with international law. Big-game hunts, baseball matches, an escape by hot-air balloon — the story piles up one exuberant boys'-adventure episode after another as it drives on toward the final showdown with the pirates' lair. It closes with the sight of the warship Hi-no-de, flying the flag of the Imperial Navy, returning home alongside the Denkō-tei, and a direct appeal to the reader — "wave your flags, and give three cheers!" — a piece that captures, in itself, the very fever of the Meiji era.

The opening (original text)

It was now fully six years ago that I set sail from the port of Yokohama with the purpose of touring the world — crossing first to America, then over the rough waves of the Atlantic to wander through Europe, making my way through the famed sights and ancient monuments of England, France, Germany, and other lands of renown, a full twenty-odd months spent in the course of it, and a journey of some fifteen thousand ri behind me, before I came at last to Italy, and gazed my fill upon the manifold wonders of that land, long celebrated as a nation of art. And it was in order to book passage home to my beloved Japan aboard the Genjetsu-maru — a ship bound for the East that was to weigh anchor that very night at half past eleven — that I arrived at Naples, that famous port, just four years ago to this day, at around noon on a certain fine, clear day in the middle of May, when the cherry blossoms were falling.

The ending (original text)

Upon the outer conning tower of the Denkō-tei will stand Naval Captain Sakuragi, Petty Officer Takemura, the boy Hideo, and more than thirty other sailors, fierce and peerless every one. Upon the deck of the warship Hi-no-de will stand her commander, Naval Captain Matsushima, the whiskered Lieutenant Todoroki Tetsuo, Hamashima Takefumi, his wife Harue, and more than two hundred other men of her crew. Every one of them, binoculars in hand, will wave white handkerchiefs, their faces bright with joy, in gratitude for your goodwill. And I, at that moment, shall surely be standing — reverently — somewhere near the stern of the warship Hi-no-de, by the eight-inch quick-firing gun, or else at the high gangway above the waterline, and, raising my helmet-shaped cap high in my right hand, shall once more, together with all of you, give three great cheers: Long live the Empire of Great Japan! Long live the Imperial Navy!
