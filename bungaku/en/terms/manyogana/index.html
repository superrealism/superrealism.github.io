What is Man'yōgana?

Category
Style & Technique

A writing system that borrowed the sound or meaning of Chinese characters, detached from the character's own meaning, to represent the sounds of Japanese. It was used to write Japanese purely with Chinese characters in the age before kana existed. Most commonly, a single character represents a single sound, and examples already appear in inscriptions on metal and stone from around the sixth century; it was used in earnest in the songs of the "Kojiki" and "Nihon Shoki" and in the poems of the "Man'yōshū." It takes its name from its extensive use in the "Man'yōshū." This device of discarding a character's meaning and using only its sound opened a path for writing one's own language with a borrowed script. In the "Man'yōshū," notation based on sound and notation based on meaning are intermingled, and deciphering it became a major undertaking for the kokugaku scholars of the early modern period. From the ninth century, hiragana was born from cursively simplified man'yōgana forms, and katakana from fragments of Chinese characters, and kana literature — the "Kokin Wakashū" and the monogatari and diary literature — flowered from there. That ancient literature was written purely in Chinese characters, and that court kana literature grew out of it, shows clearly how inseparable the history of Japanese writing is from the history of Japanese literature.
