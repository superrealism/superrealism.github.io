Famous passages by Higuchi Ichiyō

Well-known lines from the works of Higuchi Ichiyō.

6 passages

Ōtsugomori

The well had a windlass and a rope twelve fathoms long; the kitchen faced north, and the dry December wind blew clean through it, whistling, bitterly cold.

An opening that begins with the cold of the kitchen where she is in service. In the celebrated mixed classical-colloquial cadence, it depicts a single day of the girl Omine, driven to desperation for money on New Year's Eve.

Child's Play

Go round by the road and the willow of last glances at the great gate is a long way off, and yet the lights of the three-story houses reflected in the moat seem near enough to touch, and the ceaseless coming and going of carriages tells of a prosperity beyond reckoning; the name Daionji-mae sounds of the Buddha and nothing more, but those who live there call it, of all places, a lively town.

An opening that paints the pleasure quarter by the great gate of Yoshiwara in a flowing, celebrated cadence that mixes classical and colloquial diction. The bustle and atmosphere of the town rise up to enfold the fleeting days of its boys and girls.

One frosty morning someone had slipped an artificial narcissus in through the lattice gate; there was no way to know whose doing it was, but Midori, for some reason she could not name, felt a wistful tenderness for it, and set it in a single-flower vase on her shelf, admiring its lonely, pure form.

The closing. Shinnyo, who is leaving to become a monk, departs without giving his name, and only the artificial narcissus flower he leaves behind conveys the feelings never spoken aloud.

Troubled Waters

Hey, Kimura, Shin-san, why don't you stop in? I said stop in, so what's the harm in stopping in? I bet you're planning to walk right past and go on to the Futabaya again — well, think again, because I'll come out and drag you in myself.

An opening that begins with Oriki's voice calling out to customers from the doorway of a wine shop. Ichiyō's storytelling style, in which narration and dialogue melt into one another, carries the very air of the pleasure quarter with it.

Oh, I hate it, I hate it, I hate it — if only there were some way to go somewhere quiet, so quiet, where no human voice, no sound of anything, could reach me, where my own heart and mind could go blank and free of every thought

A monologue Oriki lets slip on her way back through the night streets, after seeing off a customer. The utter lack of any way out for a woman living as a courtesan surges forth in waves of repeated words.

The Thirteenth Night

Always before, it was the sound of a smart black-lacquered carriage stopping at the gate, and her parents coming out to meet the daughter they knew was home; but tonight she sent even the rickshaw she had hailed at the corner away, and stood dejected outside the lattice door.

An opening set on the night of the thirteenth, as Oseki, resolved on divorce, returns to her parents' home. The gap between this and her once-glorious days is conveyed in a single sentence.
