About this work

A novella published in the magazine Kaizō in March 1927, roughly four months before Akutagawa's suicide, and one of the central works of his last years. It takes the form of an account of the country of the kappa given by Patient No. 23 in a mental hospital. Their childbirth, love, work, art and religion turn human society inside out, and a fundamental doubt about civilization and about life is wrapped in the laughter of satire. Compared to Swift's Gulliver's Travels, and a summing-up of Akutagawa's late thought.

Summary

Patient Number Twenty-three at a mental hospital will tell anyone who comes the story of the country of the kappa. Three summers ago, setting out from Kamikōchi to climb Mount Hotaka, he chased a kappa through the valley of the Azusa River, tumbled down a deep hole, and found himself in their country. There, before a birth, the father asks the child in the womb whether it wishes to be born, and if the child answers that it would rather not, it is simply not born. Females chase males in matters of love; workers thrown out of their jobs are eaten under the Law for the Slaughter of Workmen; art goes uncensored — kappa society is a mirror that shows the human world reversed. He grows close to Bag the fisherman, Tock the poet, Mag the philosopher and Gael the capitalist; but Tock, a poet who despairs of the world, takes his own life with a pistol, and his spirit is called up at a séance to speak of the life he led. When at last the man returns to the human world, he finds he is sick of human beings after all, and, longing to go back to the country of the kappa, waits on in a room of the mental hospital for them to visit him.

Characters

  • Number Twenty-three　The patient at the mental hospital who strayed into the country of the kappa. The teller of the tale
  • Bag　The kappa fisherman who looks after him
  • Tock　A kappa poet who despairs of the world, and dies by his own hand
  • Mag　A cool-headed kappa philosopher
  • Gael　The president of a glass company. A kappa capitalist

The opening (original text)

This is the story that a patient at a certain mental hospital — Number Twenty-three — tells to anyone at all. He must be past thirty by now, though at first sight he is a most youthful-looking madman. As for the experiences of his life so far — no, that is of no importance. He simply sat hugging his knees, glancing now and then out of the window, and went on telling this long story to Doctor S, the director, and to me.

Kappa by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But I am not as pessimistic as that poet. So long as the kappa come to see me from time to time — ah, I was forgetting. You remember Pep, the judge, who was a friend of mine. That kappa really did go mad after he lost his position. They say he is in the mental hospital in the country of the kappa now. I should like to go and visit him, if only Doctor S would agree to it....

Famous passages

This is the tale that a patient at a certain mental hospital — Patient Number Twenty-Three — will tell to anyone who will listen.

The opening of this late satirical novel. By declaring in advance that it is the tale of a madman, it opens the way to a scathing critique of human society, entrusted to the land of the kappa.

A fool always believes that everyone other than himself is a fool.

A line from “The Words of a Fool,” written by Mag, a philosopher in the land of the kappa. Using the kappa world as an inverted mirror of human society, it mocks human folly.

Think it over carefully before you answer: will you be born into this world or not?

In the scene of a birth in the land of the kappa, the words the father calls loudly into the mother's womb to the unborn child. The fantastic idea of letting the child itself choose whether to be born becomes a sharp satire on human society.
