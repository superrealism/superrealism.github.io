About this work

A detective novella of 1933 featuring the detective Norimizu Rintarō. In a splendid Catholic church on the outskirts of Tokyo where White Russians exiled by the revolution gather, Norimizu takes on a run of murders around an émigré general. Puzzle-solving that deploys pedantic knowledge of theology, liturgy and Russian history, and a Gothic setting full of foreign colour: an important early work.

Summary

On a hill in the western suburbs of Tokyo stands St. Alexei's, a great Christian church suggesting the Nikolai cathedral. White Russians who fled after their defeat in the revolution pass their days in a foreign land with this church for their support. Within that closed sanctuary, one calamity after another befalls an exiled general and those connected with him. The great detective Norimizu Rintarō, drawing on an enormous store of knowledge — the history of the church, the liturgy of the Greek Orthodox rite, the history of the homeland the exiles carry with them — makes his way into the secret lying at the bottom of a church that is itself a sealed room. Having unraveled a tangle of human ties and an ingeniously plotted crime, Norimizu deliberately keeps the truth of the case from the public and passes judgment on the criminal in his own way. An excellent early work of the Norimizu Rintarō series, full of foreign color and gothic feeling.

Characters

  • Norimizu Rintarō　The great detective of enormous learning, who meets the calamity at the church with his knowledge of theology and liturgy
  • Prosecutor Hasekura　The prosecutor who works on the investigation with Norimizu
  • General Abramov　Commander of the White army of the Far East, and leader of the Russian exiles who built St. Alexei's

The opening (original text)

St. Alexei's. This great Christian church, which the world calls simply the cathedral and which looks exactly like the Nikolai cathedral, stands on the hill of I— in the western suburbs of Tokyo, surrounded by coppice, vying in height with the clock tower of R— University; and its musical bell, ringing out clear at seven in the morning and four in the afternoon — that too, I imagine, my readers have heard.

Sei Arekisei jiin no sangeki by Oguri Mushitarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"That is to say, it is the conclusion I reached by putting my own interpretation upon how this case came about; for I judged that a mental hospital would suit the carrying out of the criminal's sentence better than a prison. If the truth is a secret belonging to me alone, then the right to judge must naturally be mine as well." Some hours later the sleeping car in which the two of them rode passed through the gate of the B— asylum, over the madder-colored tracks of the melting snow.
