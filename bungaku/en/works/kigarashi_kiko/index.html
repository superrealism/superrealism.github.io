About this work

A travel piece by the poet Wakayama Bokusui. In the late autumn of 1923 he crossed the Kagosaka pass from Gotenba, worked into the mountains of Chichibu from Kōshū, and came down through a pass on the Shinshū border into the depths of the Chichibu valley: seventeen days in the mountains. Against withered fields swept by cold wind, deep ravines and rough mountain faces, he copies the people he meets on the road and the drink he takes with them in his own even hand. The character of a poet who loved travel and drink shows strongly in a journey in early winter.

Summary

On October 28, Wakayama Bokusui throws a straw mat over his shoulders and sets out alone from Gotenba. On the steep climb of the Kagosaka pass he grows drunk on sake, and while dozing on a bed of folded-down miscanthus he is woken by a strange-looking man with burn scars — such is the scene with which the journey opens. Watching the withered fields swept by a cold wind, and the rough skirts of Fuji hidden in cloud, Bokusui makes his way from Kōshū into the mountains of Chichibu. He hears from an old man the legend of defeated warriors handed down in a deep ravine, takes lodging in the mountain village of Tochimoto, which seems to hang halfway up a cliff face, and, looking down on the upper reaches of the Arakawa, takes note of the harsh life of the people living there. On November 11, parting from the old man who had guided him, he climbs Mount Mitsumine alone, visits the place where the Nakatsu and Arakawa rivers meet, and, passing through Tokyo, finally makes his way home to Numazu on the seventeenth day. A travel account in which a poet who loved travel and drink above all else copies down, in unadorned words, the loneliness and feeling of a mountain journey at the start of winter.

The opening (original text)

— Once a year, we said, let us meet like this, and parted; and now, at last, we meet again — October 28. A horse-drawn carriage from Gotenba, myself the only passenger, and bitterly cold. Not only inside the carriage, but the withering fields and woods all around, and the whole skirt of Mount Fuji, its peak hidden in cloud with only the base showing bare, all of it lay gloomy and rough, cold to the very eye.

Kogarashi kikō by Wakayama Bokusui (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

November 11. The old man was going back over the pass through Shichiri Forest to Azusayama village. I climbed Mount Mitsumine alone. Coming down from it, wanting to see the place where the Nakatsu River, which I had missed from the ridge the day before, joins the Arakawa, I went some two ri upstream along the ravine, as far as a place called, aptly enough, Ochiai — "the meeting" — village, and lodged there for the night. The next day I came out at Tokyo, stayed one night while many of my friends laughed at my rucksack and straw mat, and on what must have been the seventeenth day, made it home to Numazu.

Kogarashi kikō by Wakayama Bokusui (Aozora Bunko)
