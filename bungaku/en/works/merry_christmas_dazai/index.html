About this work

A short story about a reunion in postwar Tokyo, published in Chūō Kōron in January 1947. The narrator, back in Tokyo from Tsugaru, runs into the daughter of a mother and daughter he used to know, outside a bookshop. Buoyed by the thought of seeing the mother, he walks with the girl to an apartment in the ruins — and then. Light and playful in tone until the final scene at a food stall, where it turns suddenly and deeply sad. One of the finest of his late stories.

Summary

Back in Tokyo after a year and three months of living away from the bombing, the narrator walks the streets of a city that seems to him quite unchanged. Early in December, stopping at a bookshop on his way home from a film, he hears his name called by a young woman in a bright red raincoat. It is Shizueko, the daughter of a mother and daughter he used to know. He buys three bags of peanuts at a street stall, says they must get her mother out and have a good drink, and walks with the girl as she leads him to a barrack apartment. As she grows a step more grown-up and less lively with every step, he flatters himself that she is jealous of her mother. When he calls out loudly at the door, the girl stands stock-still and suddenly bursts into tears. Her mother, she says, was killed in the air raid on Hiroshima. The two of them go to an eel stall near the station and order three servings of skewers and three cups of wine.

Characters

  • I (Kasai)　A writer come back to Tokyo from Tsugaru
  • Shizueko　The daughter of a mother and daughter he used to know, met again by chance at a bookshop
  • The mother　Shizueko's mother, the same age as the narrator, killed in the air raid on Hiroshima
  • The gentleman at the stall　A man drinking at the back of the eel stall, who shouts at the American soldiers in the street

The opening (original text)

Tokyo showed a sad sort of liveliness — that, I had thought, was how the first line of the piece would have to go; and yet, when I came back to Tokyo, what met my eye was nothing of the kind, but the same old "Tokyo life" as ever. Until then I had lived for a year and three months at my family's house in Tsugaru, and in the middle of November this year I moved back to Tokyo with my wife and children; and now that I was here, it felt almost exactly as though I had come home from a little trip of two or three weeks.

Merry Christmas by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The gentleman suddenly followed my eyes, and then, like me, watched the stream of people outside the stall for a while — and abruptly shouted at the top of his voice: "Hello! Merry Christmas!" An American soldier was going past. For no reason I can name, it was only at that joke of the gentleman's that I burst out laughing. The soldier who had been called to shook his head with a look of utter bewilderment and strode away. "Shall we eat this eel too?" I put my chopsticks to the dish of eel left standing in the middle. "Yes." "Half each." Tokyo is as it always was. Not the least bit different from before.
