About this work

Old Gen, a ferryman in the port town of Saiki in Kyūshū, has lost his wife and his only son one after another and is entirely alone. One day, pitying a homeless beggar boy, he takes him into his house and cherishes him as his own. For a lonely old man those days with the boy are a brief happiness — but in time the boy disappears. Doppo's central early work, drawing in the beautiful measures of classical prose the despair of a man who has lost everything he had to hold to.

Summary

A young teacher, come down from the capital to teach language in the town of Saeki, takes lodgings on the shore of the harbor called Katsura, and there, from the innkeeper and his wife, hears the story of an old man's life. The old man's name is Gen — Genta Ikeda. In his youth he lost his wife Yuri, and then his only son Kōsuke, and now, utterly alone in the world, he earns his daily bread rowing a ferry-boat. Shunned by the townspeople as taciturn and cantankerous, he is an old man who silently grips his oar and says nothing. One day, Gen catches sight of a homeless beggar boy wandering the town — Kishū — and, taking pity on him, brings him home. For the old man, who has lost his own child, the days spent living with the boy are an unlooked-for happiness, restored to him. Though their fare is poor, he shares it with the boy, fusses over him as though he were his own son, and warmth comes to dwell once more in his aged breast. But the boy — whether he could not bear the old man's affection, no one can say — one day suddenly leaves Gen, and vanishes without a trace. Having lost his wife, his son, and now the boy who had become his last anchor in the world, Gen sinks into deep despair. And on a certain night, he secretly hangs a rope from a pine branch by the shore, and takes his own life. A masterwork of Doppo's early years, rendered in a lofty classical style, that sets down an old man's solitude and the sorrow of a love that goes unrewarded.

Characters

  • Gen (Genta Ikeda)　The ferryman of the port town of Saeki. A solitary old man who has lost his wife Yuri and his only son Kōsuke
  • Kishū　A homeless beggar boy whom Gen takes in. He eventually vanishes
  • The young teacher　A young man come from the capital to teach language in Saeki. The narrator, who hears Gen's story from the innkeeper and his wife

The opening (original text)

A young teacher, come down from the capital, taught language to the young people of Saeki for very nearly a year — arriving in the middle of autumn and leaving in the middle of the following summer. At the start of that summer, disliking to live within the castle town, he moved half a league off, to the harbor shore called Katsura, and from there commuted to the schoolhouse. So it was that he stayed by the sea for a month, and in that month the people with whom he exchanged so much as a word could be counted on less than one hand. Chief among them was the innkeeper. One evening, when rain fell and the wind rose and the sound of the surf beating the shore grew somewhat rough, even the teacher, who loved solitude and spoke little, felt lonely enough at that, and came down from his room upstairs to the veranda's edge, where the innkeeper and his wife sat with their legs stretched out, cooling themselves. The couple, not troubling to light a lamp, sat talking in the half-dark, waving fans to drive off the mosquitoes; seeing the teacher, they said, how rare, and made room for him to sit. The evening wind, blowing the rain lightly, sent a drop or two brushing across their faces, and the three of them, receiving it with pleasure, fell into idle talk of this and that.

The ending (original text)

"I'll go," the young man said, and set down his lantern on the ground and ran off. Ten paces on, already he could see it. From a pine branch reaching out over the path hung some strange thing. The young man, stout of heart, went striding up and fixed his eyes upon it. The one hanged there was Gen. Not far from Katsura harbor, tucked into the fold of the hills, there is a small graveyard, facing east. The graves of Gen's wife Yuri and his only son Kōsuke both lie here. A grave-marker inscribed "The Grave of Ikeda Genta" has since been raised here as well. The three graves stand in a row, Kōsuke's between the other two, and on winter nights the sleet sometimes falls upon them; and the young teacher in the capital, out of pure pity, still imagines Gen living on alone and lonely by the shore even now, weeping as he thinks of his wife and son. As for Kishū — he is Kishū still. He is looked upon by the townspeople as a mere fixture belonging to Saeki, just as before, and wanders this old castle town at midnight like one escaped from the grave, just as before. Someone once said to him that Gen had hanged himself and died; and Kishū only stared, in silence, into that person's face.
