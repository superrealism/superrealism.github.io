About this work

An early short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the magazine Musashino in April 1892. A beautiful orphan girl, born into the family of a former Tokugawa retainer and now come down in the world and living alone at Yanaka; the older guardian who keeps faith with her; and a young nobleman who moves in next door — it draws the love and duty that arise among the three and hold each other at cross purposes. A tragic story of a triangle, written in an ornate mixture of the elegant and the colloquial.

Summary

Aoyagi Itoko, born as a descendant of Aoyagi Ukyō, a former Tokugawa retainer, lost her parents early and lives alone at nineteen in a modest dwelling surrounded by a flower garden in Yanaka, behind Ueno. Out of loyalty to his late master's house, Matsuno Yukizō, son of a former steward, now in his mid-thirties, calls on her every morning and evening without fail, rain or snow, devoting himself to finding Itoko a good husband. One summer evening, chasing fireflies, Itoko drops her fan over the hedge; it is picked up and returned by a beautiful young man — Midori, second son of Viscount Takemura, who has come to the neighboring house for the summer — and the two feel an instant bond. While Itoko falls into lovesickness, Yukizō too, in secret, has found his loyalty gradually turning into a love he knows he must not feel, and suffers for it. Midori has Itoko's background investigated and has his father the viscount propose a match. But when the messenger comes before him, Yukizō declares that Itoko already has a betrothed — himself, Matsuno Yukizō. Learning what has happened, Itoko, pitying Yukizō's single-minded devotion too much to bear, realizes that if she chooses Midori, Yukizō will surely lose himself, and finds herself unable to move between the two men. Concluding that the only way to hurt no one is to erase herself, Itoko quietly takes out her inkstone and begins to write a final letter of farewell.

Characters

  • Aoyagi Itoko　Descendant of the Aoyagi family, former retainers of the Tokugawa. A nineteen-year-old orphaned girl, fallen into poverty, living alone in Yanaka. Caught between a young nobleman and her guardian
  • Matsuno Yukizō　Son of the Aoyagi family's former steward. In his mid-thirties. Serving Itoko out of loyalty to his late master's house, he finds a secret love growing within him
  • Takemura Midori　The beautiful young second son of Viscount Takemura. Comes to the neighboring house for the summer, and feels an instant bond with Itoko

The opening (original text)

A world that should have held such charm for her she cast aside like an empty cicada shell, and now, at nineteen this year, heaven's own beauty made flesh — how bitter, that a life buried like wood beneath the earth should never see its spring — Aoyagi Itoko: even to hear the name alone is to call her graceful bearing to mind, and no wonder, for trace her lineage back and the scroll runs long — she is the third-generation descendant of Aoyagi Ukyō, who once held a seat above eighty thousand retainers, bearing the long title of Attendant of the Shogun's Personal Service, in the still waveless days of the Edo era, in the last reach of the Tokugawa line.

Tamadasuki by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And yet she cannot bring herself to follow that course either — what, then, is she to do, that Matsuno's clouded heart might clear, and her own innocence be shown to young Lord Takemura? If only there were someone she could hate, her heart would not be torn so — how fleeting a thing her own life is, she thinks, looking up to find the moon hanging clear and pure in the sky. What is it that whispers by the round window where she leans her elbow? — only the wind, rustling the silver-grass leaves against each other. Even her own shadow seems a thing to pity, a thing to be ashamed of. The flower garden spread out before her wears the night's brocade, proud beneath the moon, and the dew rolls in beautiful white pearls. When she considers it, everyone in this world must vanish in the end — if she alone should cease to be, what obstacle could that possibly cause to anyone? That she should find this weary world hateful is nothing new to her; to cast it aside has long been her wish, and this, she tells herself, is nothing to grieve over. Smiling faintly, she quietly draws out her paper and inkstone, grinds the ink, sets her brush right, and begins to let flow the letter that — into whosever hand it may fall — will be read tomorrow as a keepsake, a last, fine hand of farewell.

Tamadasuki by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)
