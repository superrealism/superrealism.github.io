About this work

Nakahara Chūya's second and posthumous collection. After his sudden death at thirty in October 1937, his friend Kobayashi Hideo saw it into print with Sōgensha in April 1938. In two parts, "Diffidence" and "Poems of Days Past," it gathers the late poems sharpened by grief at the loss of his eldest son Fumiya, and sings life, death and love for a small child in a quiet measure. It closes with the afterword Chūya wrote when he entrusted the manuscript to Kobayashi just before returning to his home province — unintentionally, his own epitaph.

Summary

The book is in two parts, "Bashfulness" and "Songs of Days Past." The first half sets out lyrics that recall in faint colours the poet's childhood and his memories of love; the second half continues with poems mourning his eldest son, who died young — "A Fairy Tale," "The Beach on a Moonlit Night" — in which the sorrow of loss is raised into transparent language. In "Bones" he looks at his own corpse as though foretelling his own death; and after "The Northern Sea" and "Nagato Gorge in Winter," which carry his longing for home, the book closes with "Frog Voices," listening to the frogs. At the end is set an afterword dated 23 September 1937, in which he tells of entrusting the manuscript to Kobayashi Hideo and of taking leave of thirteen years of life in Tokyo to withdraw to his home country: the poet's own voice, about a month before he died.

The opening (original text)

Songs of Days Past Bashfulness — Songs of Days Past — Why should the heart be so ashamed? Autumn: it was in the shade of a hill on a day of white wind, in a hollow of fallen chinquapin leaves, where the trunks stood with an air disagreeably grown-up

The ending (original text)

I am now putting the manuscript of this collection together, entrusting it to my friend Kobayashi Hideo, taking leave of thirteen years of life in Tokyo, and withdrawing to my home country. It is not that I have any new plan; but I mean at last to sink myself in the life of poetry. Well — what will become of me after this? … To think of it is to be lost in vagueness. Farewell, Tokyo! O my youth! [23 September 1937]

Famous passages

On a moonlit night, a single button lay fallen at the water's edge.

The opening of “Moonlit Beach.” Singing it over and over, unable to do anything with the button he has picked up, it leaves behind only an unaccountable tenderness.

On an autumn night, far off in the distance, there was a riverbed of nothing but small stones, and upon it the sun shone down, rustling, rustling softly as it shone.

The opening of “A Fairy Tale.” Describing sunlight falling on an empty riverbed as though it made a sound, this late, anguished poem sublimates grief for a lost child into a limpid vision.

When I think of it, I've come a long way. That evening in the winter I was twelve, the steam of the whistle that rang out over the harbor sky— where is it now?

The opening of “A Naive Song.” Recalling a steam whistle he heard as a boy, the poet looks back on the self he has become, so far removed from that time—a passage full of nostalgia.

Look, look, here are my bones— having broken through that filthy flesh so full of the troubles of when I was alive, washed white by the rain, the tips of the bones jut out.

The opening of the poem “Bones.” It begins the eccentric idea of gazing at one's own bones after death with a light “Look, look,” depicting at once the pain and the comedy of life.

At Nagato Gorge, the water was flowing on. It was a cold, cold day. I was at an inn, drinking sake. Besides me there was no other guest. The water, as if it had a soul, flowed and flowed on. Presently a setting sun like a tangerine spilled over the railing. Ah! — there was such a time, a cold, cold day.

The whole of the poem “Nagato Gorge in Winter.” It depicts drinking alone at an inn in a gorge of his native Yamaguchi, in the quiet repetitions of the literary language. Said to have been written in grief after the death of his infant son, it is filled with a deep sense of loss.
