About this work

A short story published in Chūō Kōron in October 1908, one of Oguri Fūyō's central works from the period when he leaned toward naturalism: a first-person recollection drawn from the author's own wandering as a young man. Before he is twenty, the narrator runs away from home out of youthful recklessness, and when his money and his lodging are both gone he ends up in a cheap doss-house in a port town on the coast. There he is looked after by a young drifter called the coin-diviner, who goes about selling written fortunes told with six copper coins. Saying that helping people in trouble is the traveller's proper feeling, the coin-diviner keeps the narrator, whose origins he does not know, for over a month. Also in the house is a couple who make and sell fake fountain pens, and while the husband is out the wife and the coin-diviner take up together. A realist drawing, exactly to the life of the bottom of a doss-house, of the free and easy ways of these drifters and the loneliness that hides behind their freedom.

Summary

"I," not yet twenty, on a sudden whim left home and wandered aimlessly from place to place, until at last, in a port town some two hundred ri from my native place, I ran entirely out of travel money. I could take no lodging, could not even eat, and when I asked for work, no one would take on a mere traveler. Nearly collapsing where I stood, I wandered the town's outskirts by night, and, guided by a rickshaw man, put up at Yamamoto-ya, a cheap lodging house in the slum quarter on the edge of town. After a night spent suffering under a grimy, threadbare quilt crawling with fleas and lice, I woke the next morning to find that all my travel money amounted to a single five-rin copper coin. It was a young drifter lodging in the same house, a "coin-fortune peddler," who reached out a hand to me in my helplessness. He made his easy, unattached living selling fortune-telling booklets — priced at six mon a copy — in the busy quarters of town, and, saying, "When you're out on the road, we look out for one another — it's only natural among us drifters," he supported me for more than a month. Also lodging there was a couple, the Man'nen-ya, who made and peddled fake fountain pens. The two of them had been saving money, meaning to go back to farming, but when word came that the bank holding their savings had suspended payments, the husband set off for his home village to look into it. As his absence stretched on past ten days, the wife, left in the peddler's keeping, gradually grew fond of the coin-fortune man, and word of the two of them spread among the other lodgers. When the fake-pen seller came back, he was mocked by everyone, beaten and driven out, and the wife and the coin-fortune man came together as a couple. But one morning, the coin-fortune man suddenly announced he was "crossing over to the far shore." Telling the woman that a man who drifted from journey to journey could not take a wife along, he urged her to go back to her former husband, and, giving fifty sen each to "me" and to the wife, set off from the boat landing. To "me," the coin-fortune man let slip his own loneliness — that at the end of a life spent wandering from place to place, he would end up as so much earth in some unknown ground, no better than a stray dog or cat — and urged me to go home to my parents while I still could. In time, once the fifty sen she had been given ran out, the wife went and threw herself upon the mercy of the master of the Sanshū-ya, and the reunited Man'nen-ya couple went off somewhere together. True to his word, the coin-fortune man sent "me" two yen by way of an umbrella-mender, from a town some fifteen ri further on. The next day, "I" set out on the road for home.

Characters

  • I ("the Student")　A young man not yet twenty. The narrator, who leaves home to wander and, his travel money spent, lodges at a cheap inn in a port town. The other lodgers call him "the Student"
  • The coin-fortune man　A young drifter. Makes his easy living selling fortune-telling booklets for six mon apiece. Supports the destitute "I" for over a month, but values above all his free, unattached life on the road
  • Man'nen-ya　A fellow lodger who makes and sells fake fountain pens. Saving money with his wife to return to farming, he sets off for his home village, worried over his bank's suspended payments
  • The Man'nen-ya wife　Man'nen-ya's wife. Kept by the coin-fortune man during her husband's absence, she gradually grows fond of him
  • The mistress of Yamamoto-ya　The wife who runs the cheap lodging house Yamamoto-ya. A brisk, no-nonsense proprietress

The opening (original text)

This happened when I was not yet twenty. Out of the reckless folly of youth, I once left home on a sudden whim and wandered from journey to journey with no destination at all. It was already deep into autumn, the leaves turned markedly yellow, at the end of October, and after crossing the mountains on a two-day journey I entered an old port town facing the sea at the edge of that province, my small store of travel money already entirely spent. I had reached the town, but I had no lodging money to take a room for the night — no, I could not even manage a midday meal. I had walked the whole of the night before, and that morning, at the edge of town, had bought a single sen's worth of steamed sweet potato, which at least got me through the morning. Even a rice ball would do — I longed for a bite of rice at midday — but in my purse there remained only a single two-sen copper coin.

The ending (original text)

The Man'nen-ya wife had grown utterly downcast, but once the fifty sen she had been given by the coin-fortune man ran out, she soon threw herself upon the mercy of her own husband, now at the Sanshū-ya. And so the reunited Man'nen-ya couple shouldered their bundles, wrapped in white and moss-green cloths, dividing the load between them, and went off somewhere, no one knew where. The coin-fortune man had promised to send word within two or three days, but, seven days late, he had two yen delivered to me by way of an umbrella-mender who had come over from the far shore. The far shore had proved a far more depressed place than expected, it seemed, and the coin-fortune man had already moved on to some town a further fifteen ri away, from which he had had the money sent on. The next day, I set out on the road for home.
