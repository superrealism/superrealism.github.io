About this work

George Sunbird, who teaches art history in New York, is possessed by a white jar he glimpses in a colleague's study — a piece from Yamakawa Masao's series "Close Friends." The owner says that jars like that lie about by the eaveful in the Trobriand Islands, then dies suddenly, and the jar is burnt with his coffin. Sunbird goes alone to an island in the South Pacific and reaches the hut of the old priestess who made it. A nightmarish short story in which the bottom drops out of the passion for collecting.

Summary

In Sunbird's study, from a great Chinese water jar to an ancient Egyptian scent bottle, the jars and vessels gathered to his own taste stand crowded together. That was the one thing that made his life worth living. On the night of a party of professors, in the study of Professor Beaumont the folklorist, he stands rooted before a white jar about a foot high with a saw-tooth pattern cut into it. Simple, elegant, brimming with a living force — a masterpiece. But Beaumont went pale and shut the door and insisted that it was nothing but a liquor jar used by the natives of the Trobriand Islands, of which there are heaps rolling about under every eave. "I will never let it go. When I die that jar goes into my coffin with me." — Three months later Beaumont died suddenly of heart failure, and was cremated with the jar as he had willed. What is left is a few photographs and those words. The next year Sunbird lands alone on the island of Kiriwina. The town was indeed full of white jars of the same shape, but not one of them has the lively dignity of Beaumont's. It must have been made with all his heart by some particular person. Struggling with the natives' language, following Beaumont's footsteps of twenty years before, he arrives at last at the name of an old woman potter, Ipwaigana — once the greatest sorceress of Kiriwina, and now living alone outside the town. He goes by himself to the hut to which no one will guide him. In front of the hut, set down carelessly, was the very white jar he had sought, full of dignity and vigor. "Is it only the jar you want?" laughs the plump, childlike old woman, and after offering him palm wine she says that she too has a hobby of collecting, and brings out from the back of a cupboard a thin hempen cord.

Characters

The opening (original text)

George Sunbird lectures on the history of art at a university in New York. His specialty is Greek vase painting. But in truth his interest lay not in Greek civilization or its geometric meanders and plant and animal designs, but simply in the vessels themselves. This may be one of the tastes not easily understood. But then, taste and hobby, liking and passion, have at their core, more or less, some mysterious and inexplicable personal impulse that cannot be understood or explained to others (and probably not to oneself either).

Shūshū by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Choking on the strong-smelling liquid, Sunbird cried out with all the strength he had left. "...That too. Give me that bottle as well." The old woman opened her round white eyes and peered into his face. "This too?" "Yes.... I will give you whatever you want. But along with the great jar, I beg you, give me that little bottle too.... It is splendid, a splendid masterpiece...." Something like an opaque weed began to waver over his retina, and his consciousness was going out. But Sunbird held his eyes open with all his might, and beyond the rapidly darkening, blurring curtain of his sight he saw the round-faced old woman nod deeply. Joy filled his breast. And then he closed his eyes.

Shūshū by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)
