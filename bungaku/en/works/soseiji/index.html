About this work

A short story published in Shin Seinen in 1924, in the form of a written confession: a man about to be executed tells a clergyman of one more grave crime that no one yet knows about. He killed his identical twin brother and took his property and his wife — and then a terrible coincidence involving fingerprints. An important early story in which Ranpo used the fingerprint trick to fine effect.

Summary

A man whose day of execution draws near begins to talk to the chaplain who comes to visit him, as though laying down a burden on his mind. He has been sentenced to death for killing a man and stealing thirty thousand yen from his safe; but in fact he had committed, before that, another and graver murder. He had done away with his twin brother, his very double — a brother who, unlike his own dissolute self, was rich and had a beautiful wife — and using their identical faces had passed himself off as his brother and made the property and the wife his own. That perfect crime was never discovered; why, then, has he come at this late day to be sentenced to death? In it lies a terrible irony of fingerprints. Comparing it with a real case that once occurred in Fukuoka — in which the prints at the scene were reversed, like a photographic negative, and the investigation nearly went into the labyrinth — the man reveals the mechanism of the fate that destroyed him. The reflections of a man who could not help confessing, in the face of death, the one secret he had wanted kept from his wife above all, raise a quiet shudder. A fine early short story in which Ranpo made full use of a fingerprint trick.

The opening (original text)

Sir, today at last I have made up my mind to tell you. The day of my execution too draws nearer and nearer. I want to say quickly what is on my mind, and pass the few days that are left to me, at least, in peace. Troublesome as it will be, please spare a little time for this pitiable condemned man. As you know, sir, I was sentenced to death on the charge of killing one man and stealing thirty thousand yen from that man's safe. No one suspects me of anything beyond that.

Sōseiji by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

With this I have told you the whole of what I wanted to tell. I am sorry to have taken up so much of your time with a dull business. Please, as you promised a moment ago, convey this to the judges and to my wife. I shall go up to the scaffold easy in my mind, believing that you will carry out your promise. So then — I beg you again and again to grant a condemned man's dying request.

Sōseiji by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)
