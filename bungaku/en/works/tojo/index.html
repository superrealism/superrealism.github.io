About this work

A short story Tanizaki published in Kaizō in 1920. Yukawa Katsutarō, an office worker, is stopped by a private detective he does not know and made to listen, as they walk, to a strange story: the crime of probability, in which one kills a person without leaving any evidence at all, simply by raising the probability of death little by little. It is a terrible piece of reasoning about the death of Yukawa's first wife. Famous as a forerunner of the Japanese mystery, and highly regarded by Edogawa Ranpo: an intellectual and chilling piece.

Summary

On an evening at the close of the year, Yukawa Katsutarō, a company employee and bachelor of law, is hailed by an elegantly dressed stranger on a bridge near Shinbashi. The man, who claims to be a private detective, falls into step beside Yukawa and, under cover of idle conversation, broaches a chilling subject: that besides the "crime of certainty," in which one kills leaving evidence behind, there exists a "crime of probability" for which one can never be held to account. One need only, he says, surround a frail person with dangers raised imperceptibly, bit by bit — making them more prone to catching cold, increasing their chances of infection, dulling the effect of their medicine. Not one of these acts, taken alone, amounts to a crime, but taken together, in time the victim dies of what looks like entirely natural causes. The detective traces, casually yet relentlessly, the circumstances of Yukawa's first wife's death from illness, and his subsequent plans to remarry the woman he has set his heart on. Disguised as mere small talk, the detective's words draw closer and closer, step by step, to a single point: that it was Yukawa himself who, through precisely such a "crime of probability," brought about his first wife's death. As they walk on, the color drains steadily from Yukawa's face...... A landmark work of Japanese detective fiction, in which Tanizaki renders the terror of the perfect crime — a crime that leaves no evidence at all — as an intellectual drama of conversation.

Characters

  • Yukawa Katsutarō　A company employee and bachelor of law. Having lost his first wife to illness, he is planning to remarry the woman he loves, when a private detective confronts him with a terrifying piece of reasoning
  • The private detective　Hails Yukawa and, as they walk, closes in on the truth behind his first wife's death through his account of the "crime of probability"

The opening (original text)

It was around five o'clock one evening, well into December, and Yukawa Katsutarō, a bachelor of law employed at the Tokyo T.M. Corporation, was strolling idly along the streetcar road at Kanasugi Bridge, bound for Shinbashi. "Excuse me — excuse me, but aren't you Mr. Yukawa?" Just as he had crossed rather more than half of the bridge, a voice called out to him from behind. Yukawa turned around — and there, before him, bowing politely as he removed his derby hat, stood a gentleman of fine bearing whom he had never met before in his life.

The ending (original text)

Reaching the front of the house, the detective suddenly burst into loud laughter. "Ahahahaha. It's no use now. No use hiding it any longer. You've been trembling this whole while, haven't you? Your late wife's father is waiting for you at my house tonight. There's no need to be so frightened — please, come inside." And with that he seized Yukawa's wrist and, shouldering the door open, dragged him bodily into the brightly lit house. Lit by the electric lamp, Yukawa's face was ashen white. He staggered as though he had lost all his senses, and dropped heavily into a chair standing there.
