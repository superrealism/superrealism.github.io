About this work

A conjuror newly back from abroad turns up at the office of a variety company in Asakusa Park to sell himself. He puts a rabbit and a pigeon to sleep, cracks a beer bottle with his forehead, and cuts a radish clean in two on his own arm — and watching the act, the young house dramatist, who writes chain dramas anonymously to eat, falls to thinking about his own profession. A short story published in the fourth Shinshichō. Kume's own impatience at that period shows in the brief exchange between two professionals whose only difference is the road they took.

Summary

The office of the Moon World Company, which runs shows in Asakusa park. The young house dramatist, only this year out of the university, came here simply because he had to eat, and writes chain dramas under another name. He has thrown away every artistic principle and entered the "way of the writer." As he grows used to the pleasure of seeing what he has written stir the feelings of an audience, the trade he loathed becomes gradually amusing to him, and he even comes near forgetting the purpose of his life — and noticing it he starts and scolds himself. He must surely have too much artistic gift in him to sink into a worthless dramatist like this. But when will the day come when he can write as he pleases? Will this trade not have poisoned him by then? What a wretched thing it is that a man must first of all eat.... And there a card is brought in from the desk: "Newly returned from abroad, conjuror; Jungle Jap, otherwise Emoto Shin'ichi." The director says, "We shall test him as usual," and a space is cleared in the middle of the office. The conjuror throws off his coat and looks cheerfully about him. At his cry of "Pat! Pat!" the rabbit rolls over asleep and the pigeon lies down with its neck limp. He sees through a tape measure shut into a paulownia box; encouraged by the laughter of the usherettes he cracks jokes; he strikes a dark brown beer bottle against his forehead with a "Ha!" and scatters the glass; and he cuts through a white radish laid on his brawny left arm at a stroke. The director is satisfied and asks whether he has any title that would do for the bill. "Shown before Mrs. Roosevelt, wife of the late president" — the conjuror says it as though it were nothing. As he goes off to the reception room to discuss his pay, the dramatist suddenly calls him back. The trade of a conjuror. He has taken it into his head to ask what it feels like. He went to America at first meaning to do respectable work, the conjuror answers, and when he could not eat he joined a party of conjurors and picked it up by watching, and it became his trade after all. For the radish cutting he wore an iron band and did it every day for six years. "If you put as much zeal as that into something else you would rise in the world," says the dramatist; and the conjuror says: "I think so too sometimes. But, sir, it is interesting, you see. Once a man sets foot in this way there is no getting out into the world again." The band strikes up, and the clappers sound. In the theater the first chain drama is about to begin. Ah, trade, trade. The dramatist stands gazing after the conjuror; and hearing "two beats," he hurries away into the dim backstage.

Characters

The opening (original text)

At the office of the Moon World Company, which runs two or three shows in Asakusa park, the director came in with a cheerful face at a quarter to ten as usual. It was a beautifully clear autumn morning, and looking out of the window, nearly twenty people were already waiting for the Mikado-za next door to open. The director looked them over and gazed up at the sky with satisfaction.

Tejinashi by Kume Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"That is it. Whatever your age, it is amusing when the audience roars. That is what I keep on for. If there were any honest trade for me I should like nothing better than to take it. Well, well, I have talked too much. Excuse me...." And so saying he went off toward the reception room. The band strikes up noisily for a while. And through it comes the sound of the clappers. In the theater the first chain drama must be beginning now. The usherettes had gone. The clerks had sat down at their desks. But this young dramatist alone stood gazing after the conjuror, lost in thought. Ah, trade, trade. For today at least he must stay here as he is. Hearing "two beats," he hurried away into the dim backstage.
