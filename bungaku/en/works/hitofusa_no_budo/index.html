About this work

A children's story published in 1920 in Akai Tori, the magazine edited by Suzuki Miekichi. The narrator, a boy who loves to draw, attends a school in the foreign quarter of Yokohama and, wanting his classmate Jim's imported paints, steals them on impulse; found out, he breaks down in tears. But his Western teacher, learning of it, does not scold him. She shares out a bunch of grapes and quietly gathers him in. The pain of a child who has done wrong, and the gaze of one who forgives it gently, in a clear recollective prose. Arishima's best-known work for children, and long a school text.

Summary

The narrator, a boy who loves to draw and goes to a school in the Bluff quarter of Yokohama, cannot stop wanting the foreign paints belonging to Jim, a Western boy in his class — above all two beautiful colors, indigo and carmine. Unable to put the thought aside, he slips into the empty classroom during the noon recess and quietly takes the two colors from Jim's paint box. But after school his manner gives him away; the other boys press him, Jim seizes his hands and searches his pockets, the paints are found, and as he bursts into tears he is taken to their teacher, a Western woman. She does not scold him. She lets him rest quietly in the upstairs room and picks a bunch of grapes for him to take home. The next day, when he hangs back from going to school, it is Jim who comes to fetch him, and the two are friends again. Smiling, the teacher greets them, picks another bunch of grapes, cuts it in two on her pure white palm, and gives half to Jim and half to him. That teacher, who wrapped a child's wrongdoing in kindness instead of punishing it, and the beauty of the purple grapes lying on that white hand, he can still see clearly now that he is grown — and in that quiet remembering, the pain and the forgiveness of a childhood day are set down.

Characters

  • I　A boy at a school on the Bluff in Yokohama, fond of drawing, who gives way to a sudden impulse and steals Jim's paints
  • Jim　A Western boy in the narrator's class, owner of the fine foreign paints. The next day he comes to fetch the narrator and they are friends again
  • The teacher　The Western woman in charge of the class, who does not scold the boy for stealing but shares her grapes with him and takes him in with kindness

The opening (original text)

When I was small I loved to draw. The school I went to was in a place in Yokohama called the Bluff, a quarter where none but Westerners lived, and at my school too the teachers were all Westerners. Going to school and coming home again, I always passed along the street by the sea, where the hotels and the foreign companies stood in a row. Standing on the seaward side of the street, you saw warships and merchant ships drawn up in numbers on the deep blue water, some with smoke rising from their funnels, some with the flags of all nations strung from mast to mast, so beautiful that it hurt the eyes. I would often stand on the shore and look out over that view, and when I came home I would try to draw as much of it as I remembered, as beautifully as I could. But that transparent indigo of the sea, and the carmine painted near the waterline of the white sailing ships, I could never get right with the paints I had. However much I drew and drew, I could not bring out the colors as they were in the real scene.

The ending (original text)

So saying, the teacher leaned her body, wrapped in its pure white linen, out from the window, picked a bunch of grapes, laid the powdery purple cluster on her white left hand, and with a long, narrow pair of silver scissors snipped it in two through the middle and gave the halves to Jim and to me. The beauty of those purple grapes lying heaped on that pure white palm I can still remember clearly now. From that time, I think, I became a slightly better boy than before, and a little less shy. And yet — where has she gone, that dear teacher I loved so much? Knowing that I shall never meet her again, I still find myself wishing she were here. Every autumn the clusters of grapes ripen purple and take on their beautiful bloom, but the white and lovely hand, like marble, that held them out to me is nowhere to be found.

Famous passages

When I was small, I loved to draw.

It opens with a recollection of a boyhood spent loving to draw. A story of the sin of theft and of forgiveness, it has long been a favorite in school textbooks.

Every autumn the bunches of grapes still turn a beautiful purple, bloomed with their fine white powder, but nowhere can I find again those beautiful white hands, like marble, that once received them.

The closing. The white hands of the teacher who forgave his theft and shared his grapes with him are recalled each autumn, together with the grapes that turn color once more.
