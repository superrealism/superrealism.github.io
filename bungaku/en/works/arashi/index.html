About this work

A short story Shimazaki Tōson published in the magazine Kaizō in 1926. It draws the quiet days in Tokyo of the narrator, who has lost his wife and is raising four children alone. Watching them grow by the marks of their heights on a pillar, the father thinks of the parting that must come. A work showing the ripeness Tōson reached late in life, and loved as a quiet study of a father's love for his children.

Summary

"I," having lost my wife, live in a house in Tokyo with four children — Tarō, Jirō, Saburō and Sueko. At the end of the year the children gather by the pillar in the sitting room to measure their heights against one another, and make a great noise marking them in pencil and reckoning how much each has grown. In the lines of those measurings cut into the pillar their father reads quietly the growth of his children and the passing of the years without their mother. Each with a nature of his own, the children are beginning one by one to set out on their own roads. On the night when Jirō leaves for the old country, watching the cart with his baggage go up the slope, "I" taste with deep feeling the fate of a parent who must one day part from the children he has raised. A father who has come through days like a "storm" since he lost his wife, and who still watches quietly over his children, is drawn with feeling in a restrained style.

Characters

  • I　A father who has lost his wife and brings up four children with his own hands. The narrator, who suggests the author himself
  • Tarō　The eldest son, left in the care of relations in the old country, who joins the family only at the end of the year
  • Jirō　The second son, who has grown tall and at last leaves the house
  • Saburō and Sueko　The third son and the youngest daughter, small children brought up at their father's side

The opening (original text)

The children gathered in the sitting room where the old clock hangs, and went to the pillar there to measure their heights against one another. I am astonished at how tall Jirō has grown. His head, the tallest in the house, looks as if it would reach the lintel with another inch and a half to go. Jirō has grown a good deal taller than Tarō, who comes up from the old country at the end of every year for the New Year and is with us only then.

Arashi by Shimazaki Tōson (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Jirō, who is impatient by nature, could not wait for the hour of departure, and went once round the house he had grown up in and came back. "I am going, then." We heard the words about the time the old clock in the sitting room struck nine. Up the Ueki slope the cart with Jirō's baggage had already moved off ahead. And before we knew it Jirō too was making the sound of his shoes on the lane outside the house, and going quietly away from us.
