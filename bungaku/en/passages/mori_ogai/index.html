Famous passages by Mori Ōgai

Well-known lines from the works of Mori Ōgai.

14 passages

The Dancing Girl

The loading of the coal is now at last finished.

This celebrated opening line is written aboard ship, at a port of call on the voyage home. From the stillness of this single sentence begins Toyotarō's remorseful confession, having left his lover behind in a foreign land.

Ah, a good friend such as Aizawa Kenkichi one shall surely never find again in this world. And yet, to this day, a single point of hatred for him remains lodged in my mind.

The final line. A poignant close that places, at the very end of this confession, a hatred for the friend who was his benefactor and yet also tore him from his lover.

What brilliance is this that strikes my eyes! What color is this that would bewilder my heart!

The scene in which Ōta Toyotarō, newly arrived in Berlin, is dazzled by the splendor of Unter den Linden. The paired phrases in elegant classical style convey the astonishment of a Meiji youth standing in a great Western city.

Vita Sexualis

Kanai Shizuka's profession is philosophy.

A spare opening that introduces its protagonist in a single line. The entry point into a controversial work, banned from sale, that sets down the memory of sexual desire with unwavering composure.

The Wild Geese

It is an old story. I happen to remember that it took place in the thirteenth year of Meiji.

An opening that draws the story out as if reeling in a distant memory. It tells of a love between Otama, the mistress of a moneylender, and the student Okada, that ends without ever quite meeting.

“There are unlucky geese in this world, it seems.”

Okada's muttering after the stone he threw strikes a goose on Shinobazu Pond. This small chance ends up making his path and that of O-Tama, who was waiting for him, pass each other by.

The Abe Family

Hosokawa Tadatoshi, Junior Fourth Rank, Lower Grade, Minor Captain of the Left Palace Guards and Governor of Ecchū, left behind, in the spring of the eighteenth year of Kan'ei, the flowers of his domain of Higo, which bloomed earlier than anywhere else, and set out flanked before and behind by the resplendent procession befitting a lord of five hundred and forty thousand koku

An opening that begins with a weighty sentence stringing together court ranks and titles. It is a representative historical work of Ōgai's, moving toward the destruction of the Abe family, who were denied permission to follow their lord in death.

Faust

For whatever one has written down in black and white one can carry home with an easy mind.

The student's line to Mephistopheles, disguised as the professor, about taking notes at lectures. The phrase about having something “in black and white” — so that it is safe to carry home — is widely used as an idiom in the original German as well.

Sanshō the Steward

I long for Anju, hōyareho. I long for Zushō, hōyareho.

The song hummed by the mother, sold into Sado, chasing birds away with her blinded eyes. This repeated refrain gathers into itself all the pathos of a brother and sister torn apart.

Saigo no ikku

“Surely there can be no mistake in what the authorities do.”

The single remark added by the girl Ichi, who has petitioned to die in place of her father, after answering the magistrate's question. These words, concealing a piercing irony toward authority, are the “last phrase” of the title.

The Boat on the River Takase

The takasebune is a small boat that plies up and down the Takase River in Kyoto.

A quiet opening that begins with an explanation of a small boat on a Kyoto canal. It sets the frame for a short story that asks what crime is, and what mercy killing is.

That he had killed him was surely a crime. And yet, thinking that he had done it to free his brother from suffering, a doubt arose that could not, however he turned it over, be resolved.

Shōbei's reflection after hearing the full circumstances of a brother's killing. It hands the question of mercy killing to the reader, unresolved.

Thinking on like this, from one thing to the next, there was no knowing how far a man might go before he could stop. And it was this Kisuke, Shōbei realized, who was now showing him, before his very eyes, what it was to stop.

Shōbei's reflection after hearing the story of the unacquisitive Kisuke. Contrasting human nature, which extends its desires without end, with Kisuke, who knows contentment, it shows the work's other theme.

Kanzan jittoku

As they fled, Hanshan could be heard to say, “Fenggan has been talking.”

The final lines, in which Hanshan and Shide laugh and run away from Lü Qiuyin, who has come to worship them on hearing that they are incarnations of Mañjuśrī and Samantabhadra. The story closes by lightly brushing aside a reverence that leans on authority and hearsay.
