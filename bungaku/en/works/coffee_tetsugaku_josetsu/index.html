About this work

An essay Terada Torahiko published in Keizai Ōrai in 1933. Terada, physicist and fine essayist, traces lightly his own dealings with coffee — from the memory of first drinking it with milk as a boy, as medicine, through the cafés of his student years in Germany, to a daily cup. In time the talk reaches the lift of body and mind a single cup brings and its relation to creation in science and in art. A piece full of the peculiar flavour of Terada's essays.

Summary

At eight or nine years old, on a doctor's orders, I was made to drink milk for the very first time. In those days milk was not a thing of pleasure but chiefly a medicine for the weak and sickly. Since I found the smell of milk so unbearable that I could not get it down, the doctor never failed to mix in a small measure of coffee with it. The powdered coffee was put into a little bag of bleached cotton cloth and steeped in the hot milk — and the flavor of the coffee I tasted for the very first time in this way utterly enraptured this country-raised boy. To a child's heart, drawn to everything exotic, that fragrance, at once tropical and Western, felt like a fair wind blowing in across some distant ocean from an unknown paradise. In time, through his years at higher school, his study abroad in Germany at thirty-two, the fine coffee brewed for him by the old landlady of his lodgings in Berlin, a cup taken at a café — "I" traces, with fine and tender memory, the whole course of his own dealings with coffee. And from there the talk turns, lightly, to the bracing lift a single cup of coffee gives to body and mind alike, to its bearing on a scientist's research and on artistic creation, and to the "philosophy" hidden within the most ordinary of daily habits. "What began as idle jottings on coffee has, before I knew it, turned into something like an introduction to a philosophy of coffee" — a delightful essay by Terada Torahiko, the physicist and celebrated essayist.

The opening (original text)

At eight or nine years old, on a doctor's orders, I was made to drink something called milk for the very first time. In those days, milk was still, it seems, neither a thing of pleasure for the general public nor a staple item of nutrition, but chiefly a medicine for the weak and sickly. And it was an age in which many people of the old-fashioned sort found milk, and the so-called "sop" made from it, so unbearably foul-smelling that they could not drink it at all, certain to bring on vomiting or looseness of the bowels if they tried. The milk I drank for the first time was, it seems, likewise nothing more than a hard-to-swallow "medicine." To make it easier to drink, the doctor never failed to mix in a small measure of coffee with it. In any case, the flavor of the coffee I tasted for the very first time in this way utterly enraptured this country-raised boy that I was.

The ending (original text)

The delicious dish that is art, too, can at times intoxicate a person, and among the intoxicating elements at work in it, it seems, are the wine already mentioned, along with nicotine, atropine, cocaine, morphine, and any number of other things. Perhaps art could even be classified by these very elements. It is a sad thing that there is so much cocaine-art and morphine-literature about. These idle jottings on coffee have, before I knew it, turned into something like an introduction to a philosophy of coffee. This too may be nothing more than the intoxicating effect of the very cup of coffee I have just now finished drinking.
