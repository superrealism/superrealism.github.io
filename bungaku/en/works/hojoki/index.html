About this work

An essay written by Kamo no Chōmei in 1212, in the early Kamakura period. With Sei Shōnagon's Pillow Book and Yoshida Kenkō's Essays in Idleness, it is one of the three great Japanese essays. It opens with the famous line "The flow of the river never ceases, and the water is never the same," and argues that everything in this world passes, that neither people nor their dwellings are fixed. Chōmei sets down what he witnessed of five disasters — fire, whirlwind, the moving of the capital, famine and earthquake — before withdrawing from the world to a ten-foot-square hut on Mount Hino. He describes the ease of that plain, unencumbered life, then turns on himself at the last, judging even his attachment to the hut a kind of clinging. Celebrated as well for the clarity and dignity of its mixed Japanese-Chinese prose, it is the summit of Japanese writing on impermanence.

Summary

The flow of the river never ceases, yet neither the water nor the foam upon it is ever the same. So too with the houses ranked in the capital and the people who live in them: they change without pause, and nothing endures. With this account of the world's frailty Chōmei begins. He goes on to relate five great disasters he witnessed in the capital: the Angen fire, which burned two-thirds of the city; the Jishō whirlwind, which broke the houses; the sudden removal of the capital to Fukuhara, which threw people into confusion; the Yōwa famine, in which the dead of starvation filled the roads; and the Genryaku earthquake, which brought down mountains and houses. Contemplating these calamities and the frailty of human affairs, Chōmei gives up name and gain and takes the tonsure past the age of fifty. In a ten-foot-square hut built deep in the hills of Hino he finds the pleasures of a free and solitary life, with the way of the Buddha, the biwa and poetry, and the four seasons of the mountain for company. Yet at the close he asks himself whether even his attachment to that hut is not a clinging that goes against the Buddha's teaching, and, finding no answer, lays down his brush with the invocation on his lips.

The opening (original text)

The flow of the river never ceases, and the water is never the same water. The foam that floats upon its stillness now vanishes, now forms, and never stays long. So it is with the people of this world and with their dwellings. In the jewelled capital, where the ridgepoles stand in ranks and the roof tiles vie with one another, the houses of the high and the low seem to pass down the generations and never come to an end — but ask whether it is truly so, and you find that the houses of former days are few.

An Account of My Hut by Kamo no Chōmei (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In the quiet before dawn I follow this reasoning out and ask myself: you fled the world and came among these hills and woods in order to govern your heart and to practise the way. And yet your appearance is that of a holy man while your heart is steeped in impurity. Your dwelling presumes upon the example of the layman Vimalakīrti, but what you keep of the practice does not come up even to Cūḍapanthaka's. Is it the recompense of poverty and low estate that torments you? Or has a deluded mind at last driven you mad? At that my heart made no further answer. I did no more than borrow my tongue for two or three invocations of the Buddha's name, unasked, and had done. In the second year of Kenryaku, about the last day of the third month, written by the monk Ren'in in his hut on the outer hills.

Famous passages

The flowing river never stops, and yet the water never stays the same. The bubbles that drift on its pooling eddies now vanish, now form again, but never endure for long.

The opening line entrusts the flux of all things to the image of a ceaselessly flowing river and the bubbles on its surface. It is the emblematic passage of Kamo no Chōmei's literature of seclusion, a lifelong meditation on impermanence.

I do not know: those who are born and die — from where do they come, and to where do they go?

A passage following the opening image of flowing water. Layering the question of where people come from and where they go, it deepens still further the sense of impermanence.

The old capital had already fallen into ruin, and the new capital was not yet built. Everyone, one and all, felt like a drifting cloud.

A passage recording the move of the capital to Fukuhara in 1180. The old capital lay in ruins and the new one was not yet built: the unease of people who had lost their footing is expressed as “the feeling of a drifting cloud.”

Now, the Three Worlds are nothing but a single mind. If the mind is not at peace, oxen and horses and the seven treasures are of no use, and palaces and towers are nothing to wish for.

A passage near the end, describing life in his ten-foot-square hut. All worlds lie in the mind alone, and without peace of mind wealth and palaces mean nothing: the state Chōmei finally reached.
