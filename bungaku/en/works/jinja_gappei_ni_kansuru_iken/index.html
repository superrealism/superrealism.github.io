About this work

The written opinion in which Minakata Kumagusu attacked severely the policy of merging shrines that the government pushed at the end of Meiji. Setting out concrete harms, he argues that a policy of consolidating many shrines to a standard of one per town or village destroys the sacred groves and damages the faith and the livelihood of the people, and precious nature besides. Arguing the wrongness of the mergers from every angle — learning, faith, economics and the protection of nature — this piece is known as a forerunner of conservation thought and ecology in Japan.

Summary

Minakata first makes clear, following the actual text of the law, that the mergers ordinance of 1906, which set one shrine as the standard for each town or village and imposed excessive endowment requirements even on shrines of long standing, was a scheme that in practice destroyed a great many shrines. He exposes, with concrete examples from Wakayama, Osaka and elsewhere, how prefectural governors and county heads competed with one another over mergers in pursuit of credit, and how Shinto priests and officials threatened parishioners or wheedled them into forcibly signing petitions. Mergers, he argues, damage the people's reverence for the gods, cut down the sacred groves that protect the shrines and destroy the ancient landscape and ecology that go with them, and rob a region of the seat of faith bound up with its history. Citing even the cases of the Roman Empire and Russia, which alienated their people's hearts by suppressing ancient rites, Minakata argues that mergers corrupt the people within and invite the mockery of other nations without. And he appeals strongly to those in power that the hasty mergers be halted at once, that the restoration of the abandoned sites be permitted, and that the shrines and their sacred groves be protected, taking whatever time is needed.

The ending (original text)

The above may read like a petition, but I have no intention of submitting so long-winded a petition as this. I only hope that even one man of patriotic goodwill will read it, draw out its essentials, and speak on it in a way that will have some real effect. As they say that brevity comes from breadth, I have set down here, holding nothing back, everything that is in my mind.
