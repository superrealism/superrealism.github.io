About this work

A children's story with a streak of black humour rare in Miyazawa Kenji: the police chief who cracks down hardest on poison-fishing turns out to be — . Set in an invented country called Puhara, it gives away the culprit with a deflating ease, and the last words spoken just before the execution are so vivid that this very short piece is hard to forget.

Summary

Four valley streams rising from the glaciers of Mount Karakon come together to make one great river in the country of Puhara. The pools in the riverbed are full of fish, and loaches and catfish especially have multiplied. Article One of this country runs: "You shall not take birds with gunpowder; you shall not take fish by poison-mashing." Poison-mashing is a way of fishing in which the bark of the sanshō tree and the ash of maple wood are mixed, put into a bag, and mashed out in the water so that the fish float up. To keep it down was the most important duty of the police. One summer a new superintendent arrives at the police station of this town, somehow resembling an otter, with a red beard sticking straight out. With his fine mustache and his silver false teeth, he becomes a favorite of the townspeople, and when he is invited to their houses he behaves most agreeably. But from about that time, fish keep dying and floating up in the river. Among the children a rumor begins that it is the superintendent's doing. When the mayor conveys the rumor to him diffidently, the superintendent jumps up from his chair: "That is a serious matter. It touches my honor as well. I shall arrest the criminal at once." The evidence, he assures him, is already in. "Do you know who it is?" "I know perfectly well. In fact, it is I who do the poison-mashing." Calmly the superintendent struck the bell, called in a first-class detective, and had himself arrested. He was sentenced at his trial to death; and as his head was struck off with a great curved sword he said, laughing: "Ah, that was good fun. When it comes to poison-mashing, I am simply mad about it. Now at last — shall I do some poison-mashing in hell?" Everyone was thoroughly impressed.

Characters

  • The superintendent　The chief of police, resembling an otter, who comes to put down poison-mashing
  • The mayor　Who conveys the rumor of the poison-mashing to the superintendent
  • Richiki　A poor barber who owns only two razors

The opening (original text)

Four cold valley streams came out of the glaciers of Mount Karakon and went roaring, spitting white foam, into the country of Puhara. The four rivers came together at the town of Puhara to make one great, quiet river. Ordinarily the water of the river was clear and the clouds and the shadows of the trees were reflected in its pools; but once it flooded, the broad riverbed a thousand yards across, where the willows grew, was filled to the brim with terribly roaring water.

The ending (original text)

And so the superintendent was bound and brought to trial, and it was settled that he should be put to death. When at last his head was to be struck off with the great curved sword, the superintendent said, laughing, "Ah, that was good fun. When it comes to poison-mashing, I am simply mad about it. Now at last — shall I do some poison-mashing in hell?" Everyone was thoroughly impressed.
