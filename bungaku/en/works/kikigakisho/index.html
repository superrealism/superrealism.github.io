About this work

A historical novel Tanizaki serialized in the Osaka Mainichi and Tokyo Nichinichi in 1935, subtitled "A Second Blind Man's Tale." A companion to A Blind Man's Tale, it takes the elaborate form of documentary work on an old manuscript, and draws the horror under Hideyoshi's rule in the purge of the family of the regent Toyotomi Hidetsugu, and the life of Junkei, a blind grave-keeper who went on privately loving Ichi no Tsubone, the principal wife who died at the Sanjō riverbed. The narration, weaving real histories together with invented sources, shows the high point of the line of "records" that began with A Portrait of Shunkin.

Summary

Following the publication of "Mōmoku monogatari," the author receives from a reader in Nagahama, in Ōmi, an old manuscript titled "The Asaka Gendayū Papers." It tells the strange and fated life of a samurai who once served Ishida Mitsunari, later lost his sight for some particular reason, and fell at last into keeping the "tomb of great wickedness" where the Toyotomi Hidetsugu clan lay buried. After the defeat at Sekigahara, Mitsunari's orphaned daughter and her wet nurse, fleeing in secret to the capital, come upon a blind ascetic named Junkei, who has built himself a hermitage at the Sanjō riverbed. Junkei, once a samurai who went by the name Yabuhara, had in his youth attended upon Lady Ichi-no-Dai, principal wife of the regent Hidetsugu, and, knowing full well that she was a being beyond his reach, had secretly cherished her noble image in his heart. In time Hidetsugu incurred the wrath of the Taikō Hideyoshi and was made to take his own life at Mount Kōya, and Lady Ichi-no-Dai, together with more than thirty of his wives, concubines, and children, was put to the sword, one after another, at the Sanjō riverbed, and buried together in a single mound. Junkei, casting aside both bow and biwa, kept, even in his blinded eyes, the vision of that lady, and built his hut beside her grave, praying morning and evening for the repose of her soul ever after. The author weaves historical research into the first half of this told tale, and, noting that the continuation may one day be set down in a further volume, "The Later Papers," lays down his brush there.

Characters

  • Junkei　Once a samurai named Yabuhara who served Ishida Mitsunari. Loses his sight for some particular reason and lives on as keeper of the Hidetsugu clan's grave
  • Lady Ichi-no-Dai　Principal wife of the regent Hidetsugu. A noble lady of the Kikutei family, put to death at the Sanjō riverbed
  • Toyotomi Hidetsugu　Nephew of the Taikō Hideyoshi. Inherits the office of regent, but is suspected of treason and forced to take his own life
  • Mitsunari's daughter　The orphaned daughter of Ishida Mitsunari, who hides in the capital with her wet nurse after the defeat at Sekigahara
  • Asaka Gendayū　A samurai of the early Edo period, said to be the author of the "Papers"

The opening (original text)

In a work called the "Hōnaiki," found in a supplementary volume of miscellaneous records within the revised Historical Documents Collection — also known by the alternate title "A Record of Hideyori's Affairs" — said to have been compiled by Kuwabara Kyūtoku from the account of Takagi Ni'emon Nyūdō Sōmu, who lived to witness the fall of Osaka, there appears, in one passage of the first volume, a postscript concerning Hayato-no-shō Shigeie, the eldest son of Ishida Mitsunari.

Kikigakishō by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The verses each of them composed as they went to their deaths, the sight of their robes — mournful, and yet with some flash of splendor still about them even in that desolation — the words recorded of that day: "How piteous, how sorrowful it was; had they only known beforehand how wretched a thing this would be, they would never have gone out to watch it, said the many voices, weeping in a thousand regrets; and when more than twenty had been cut down, one upon another, even the river's water changed its color" — how gorgeous and terrible a picture of hell that riverbed unfolded on that day — this I have truly wished to set down on paper, recreating the scene from Junkei's own testimony as it is preserved in the "Papers," and to record in fuller detail, besides, how Junkei, having cast aside not only his bow and arrows but his biwa as well, moving between regret, and anger, and enlightenment, and madness by turns, could never, to the end of his life, banish that lady's image from his blinded eyes, and, wandering ever deeper into delusion, came at last to keep her grave. But these matters I must leave for some other day, under the title "The Later Papers," when I may again take up my brush and inkstone to carry the tale forward. What has been related here is, after all, no more than the first half of Gendayū's "Papers."

Kikigakishō by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)
