About this work

An early short story, and the work that first drew attention to Izumi Kyōka as a writer of the novel of ideas, published in Bungei Kurabu in April 1895. Through a policeman so faithful to his duty that he has no regard for human feeling, it draws the collision of rules and humanity. With "The Surgery Room" of the same year, the work that made his name known in literary circles.

Summary

Hatta Yoshinobu, a policeman patrolling Kōjimachi late at night, is a man strict to the rules — he berates an old rickshaw man shivering without even a pair of drawers, and drives off without mercy a poor mother sleeping in the cold with her infant in her arms. Just then, on the bank of the moat, a drunken old man walking with his niece O-Kō is telling her of the obstinate feeling in his breast, which will not allow a match between her and Hatta, out of a grudge over the woman who was taken from him long ago — O-Kō's mother. Hatta is the very man O-Kō loves, and the words carry to his ears where he is patrolling a little way off. Unable to bear it, O-Kō tries to throw herself from the embankment, and in the movement the old man, running after her, falls into the moat by accident. Hatta runs up and O-Kō clings to him; but pleading his duty he pushes her away, and though he cannot swim he throws himself into the freezing moat to save the old man — and loses his life with the old man's, leaving O-Kō behind.

Characters

  • Hatta Yoshinobu　A policeman strict to the rules. The hero, who loves O-Kō and yet throws his life away in the name of duty
  • O-Kō　A young woman in love with Hatta, whose uncle keeps them apart
  • The uncle　O-Kō's blind old guardian, who will not allow the match because of his lingering feeling for O-Kō's mother, who was taken from him
  • The old rickshaw man　A poor rickshaw man sternly reproved by Hatta late at night for a trifling fault

The opening (original text)

"Here, old man, where do you come from?" said a young fellow of the artisan sort, turning to the old rickshaw man beside him. The old rickshaw man had already passed fifty, and one would think there was not much between him and sixty. In a weak voice, perhaps from hunger, and trembling besides with the cold, he said, "I beg you, sir, forgive me; I shall certainly take care hereafter. Yes, yes" — and was flustered and confused.

Yakō junsa by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Let go!" — and shaking her off resolutely, so that her strength failed and her hand fell away, the policeman in that instant leapt and threw himself in as though casting himself away. O-Kō fell into a faint. Alas, Hatta, in order to wipe out the debt he bore toward society as an officer, and to save the devil whose death he had all this while desired — nay, whom he had wished to kill — threw away his love along with his life, at freezing point, at midnight when the water froze, and he unable to swim. Afterward society in general called Hatta a man of benevolence. Ah, was he indeed a man of benevolence? And how is it that no one praises the devoted labor of that same one man in punishing, cruelly and harshly, an old rickshaw man who deserved forgiveness, and in sternly rebuking a pitiable mother and child?
