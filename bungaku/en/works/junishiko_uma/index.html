About this work

The piece for the year of the horse — the folklore and legends of the horse — serialized irregularly in Taiyō in 1918, and one of the longest in the series. Since the horse and the ass spread over the whole world from Asia, Europe and Africa, he says, the legends about them cannot be counted; and beginning from the wise horse of the Buddhist scriptures, he writes down horse stories of all ages and countries as the horse of his own mind takes him. At the end he argues why the white horse is called the blue horse, from the history of colour perception itself.

Summary

"The colt that runs past the crack in the wall is quick of foot, and today or tomorrow we shall greet the year of the horse" — so Kumagusu begins. Among the animals assigned to the twelve branches there can be no better and worse, but in the amount of legend attached to them there is a marked difference. Since sheep have not been many in Japan, one hears almost no Japanese sheep tales; and since monkeys, though plentiful in the East, are not produced in Europe, the old traditions of that quarter are scanty. The horse, on the contrary, is native to Asia and Europe, and the ass, which may be called its younger brother, has Africa for its home, and from there they have spread over the whole world, so that tales of them are past counting. Fearing to be told "that story again" if he sets out the commonplace anecdotes of Japan and China, Kumagusu first summarizes, in place of a New Year's greeting, the most auspicious tale of the wise horse from the Vinaya in Chinese translation, and then declares that he will let the horse of the mind run and talk at random as things occur to him. The names of the horse, the legends of famous horses, tales of horses and marriage, sacrifices of horses, the ghosts of horses. In closing he takes up the Aouma Banquet on the seventh of the first month and asks why the white horse should be read "blue horse." The perception of color cannot be measured exactly like temperature or electric current, he says; in old times all the pale colors were called white; and in China deep purple was named "snow blue" because shadows on the snow look purple. It must be, he concludes, that white looks blue according to the light.

The opening (original text)

Legend I The colt that runs past the crack in the wall is quick of foot, and today or tomorrow we shall greet the year of the horse. Truly, among the animals assigned to the twelve branches there can be no better and worse; but in the amount of legend attached to them there is a marked difference. For instance, since sheep have until now not been many in Japan, one hears almost no Japanese sheep tales. Tales of the monkey are not few in the East, but since the animal is not produced in Europe, the old traditions of that quarter are scanty.

Jūnishikō (uma) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Color, unlike temperature or electric power and the rest, cannot be measured exactly by degrees; and accordingly not only ordinary people but scholars too see it very differently.... In old times the distinguishing of colors was extremely rough, and all the pale colors were called white.... Someone has explained that because on a fine day the shadows of things on the snow of a high mountain look purple, the Chinese named deep purple "snow blue" — a name that confuses purple with blue. That white should look blue according to the light there are frequent instances of among the women of the capital who put on much white powder; and it must be for such reasons that the white horse came to be called the blue horse.

Jūnishikō (uma) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
