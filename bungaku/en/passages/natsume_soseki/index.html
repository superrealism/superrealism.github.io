Famous passages by Natsume Sōseki

Well-known lines from the works of Natsume Sōseki.

62 passages

I Am a Cat

I am a cat. I have no name yet.

One of the most famous opening lines in Japanese literature. In these two short sentences lies the whole conceit of the novel: human society observed through the eyes of a nameless cat.

Where I was born I have not the faintest idea. All I remember is that I was mewling in some dim, damp place.

The passage that follows the opening line. The history of a cat with neither origin nor name begins, told in an easy, unhurried voice.

I am going to die. In death I shall find this great peace. This peace cannot be found without dying. Namu Amida Butsu, Namu Amida Butsu. How grateful, how grateful I am.

The final scene, in which the cat, tipsy on beer, falls into a water jar and slowly loses his strength. It closes a long, easygoing novel with a quiet resignation and a Buddhist invocation.

Even people who seem carefree give off a sad sound somewhere if you tap at the bottom of their hearts.

The cat's reflection in the final chapter, after the guests gathered at Kushami's house have gone home. Pinpointing the loneliness beneath these comic people, it prepares the sorrow of an ending that closes with the cat's death.

Rondon tō

Pass through this gate, you who would go to the land of sorrow. Pass through this gate, you who would meet eternal torment. Pass through this gate, you who would join the lost. Justice moved my high Maker; divine power, highest wisdom, and primal love made me. Before me there was nothing but the eternal, and eternal I endure. Abandon all hope, you who would pass through this gate.

The inscription on the gate of Hell from Dante's Divine Comedy, which rises in the narrator's mind as he passes through the gate of the Tower of London. Sōseki depicts the Tower he visited while studying in London as a fantastical entrance into a bloodstained history.

Botchan

Ever since I was a child, I have been getting myself into trouble through the reckless streak I inherited from my parents.

One of the most famous opening lines in Japan, in which the narrator introduces himself through his inherited recklessness. In a single breath, the Edokko-spirited protagonist springs to life.

And that is why Kiyo's grave is at Yōgenji Temple in Kobinata.

The final line. A narrative voice that has pushed through in true Edokko fashion closes quietly, leaving behind only his affection for Kiyo, the old family maid.

“You have a straight and honest nature.”

The words with which Kiyo, the family's maid, praises the young narrator, whom his family dislikes. Kiyo's steadfast faith in a boy no one else appreciates becomes the warmth running through the whole story.

When I was in primary school, I jumped from the second floor of the school building and couldn't stand up for a week. Some may ask why I did such a reckless thing. There was no deep reason. I was sticking my head out of a second-floor window of the new building when one of my classmates said as a joke, “You can brag all you like, but you can't jump down from there. Coward!”

The episode that follows the opening line about the recklessness Botchan inherited from his parents. The boyhood leap from a second-floor window, made because he was teased, tells his straightforward character in a single breath.

Kusamakura

Approach everything rationally, and you grow rigid. Pole your boat along the stream of human feeling, and you are swept away. Insist on having your own way, and you find yourself confined. However you look at it, the human world is a hard place to live.

The opening monologue of the painter climbing a mountain path. Through a cascade of parallel clauses on the hardship of living in the human world, it stands as one of Sōseki's finest passages of prose.

When the difficulty of living grows too great, one wants to move somewhere easier. It is when one realizes that no place, however far one moves, is any easier to live in, that poetry is born, and paintings come into being.

A passage following the celebrated opening. It states the novel's theme: that art exists to make an unlivable world just a little more bearable.

Going up a mountain path, I fell to thinking.

One of the best-known opening lines in modern Japanese literature. After this short sentence flows the painter's meditation that begins, “Approach everything rationally, and you grow rigid.”

It was neither gods nor demons who made the human world. It was, after all, just ordinary people—the ones you glimpse in the houses across the way and next door.

A continuation of the opening meditation. It declares that the hard-to-live-in world was made not by special beings but by ordinary people close at hand, and moves on to the resignation that there is therefore no escape.

If the world one cannot leave is hard to live in, one must ease that hard place as much as one can, and make one's fleeting life livable, if only for a fleeting while. Here the vocation of the poet arises; here the mission of the painter descends.

The heart of the theory of art set out at the opening. It holds that the task of poets and painters lies in making a world one cannot escape at least a little more livable.

If the twentieth century has need of sleep, then to the twentieth century this unworldly flavor of poetry is precious.

A passage praising the realm of Eastern poetry, free of gain and loss. It argues that in the busy modern age above all, poetry that transcends the world is needed, much as sleep is.

“That's it! That's it! If that comes out, it will make a painting!”

What the painter cries at the end, the moment “pity” appears on Nami's face as she watches the train carry her former husband off toward Manchuria. Announcing the completion of the painting he has been seeking, it closes the story.

Gubijinsō

Being in earnest, you see, means fighting in dead seriousness. It means striving to overcome. It means being unable to rest until you have overcome. It means the whole of a person thrown into action.

Near the novel's end, Munechika Ichi presses the wavering Ono to change his ways. Hammering home what it means to live sincerely, this is the most widely quoted line in the book.

Tragedy is greater than comedy.

The opening of Kōno Kingo's diary, placed at the novel's close. Written in the wake of Fujio's death, it is celebrated as the line that declares the theme of the entire work.

Those who forget death grow extravagant.

A line from the same diary. It argues that a life which forgets death grows arrogant, and in time tramples on morality — the view of life that runs through Fujio's tragedy.

“It's quite a long way, isn't it? Where are we supposed to climb up from, anyway?”

The opening, which begins with Kōno and Munechika climbing Mount Hiei. From the talk of these two contrasting young men, the drama surrounding Fujio begins.

She was a woman like a single deep drop of purple, rising above spring itself, let fall vividly upon a sleeping heaven and earth at the height of a noon wrapped in the crimson of the third month.

The passage introducing the heroine, Fujio. In ornate prose strung with Chinese-derived words, it paints a proud and bewitching woman through the color purple—a passage emblematic of the novel's style.

Ants gather to sweetness; people gather to novelty. The people of civilization languish in boredom amid a fierce struggle for existence.

Criticism of civilization placed at the start of Chapter Eleven, which depicts the bustle of the Industrial Exhibition. In paired phrases it sharply pins down people who swarm to novelty and the modern person suffering boredom amid busyness.

“Here, only comedies are in fashion.”

The final line of the novel. Munechika's reply from London to Kōno's diary entry, “Tragedy is greater than comedy,” it brings down the curtain with irony toward a world that tends to forget tragedy.

Kōfu

People speak of something bringing them to tears, but if the tears come, you can rest easy. As long as tears come, you are sure to be able to laugh as well.

A passage in which a young man who ran away from home and nearly became a miner looks back on his cornered state of mind. A paradox from one who has known the depths of hardship: while you can still cry, you still have some room left.

Ten Nights of Dream

I had a dream like this.

The short phrase that opens each of the ten nights. Offering no reason or explanation, it draws the reader straight into the logic of dream — the emblematic opening of Sōseki's experimental sequence of stories.

“Wait for me — a hundred years.”

A promise left behind by a dying woman on the First Night. Because of this single line, the ending — a white lily blooming a hundred years later — quietly holds together within the logic of the dream.

“So the hundred years had already come,” I realized then for the first time.

The close of the First Night. The moment the dreamer, who has waited beside the woman's grave, kisses a white lily that has bloomed there, he realizes that the promised hundred years had already passed.

In the end I realized that no guardian kings were buried in the wood of Meiji. And with that, I also more or less understood why Unkei was still alive today.

The close of the Sixth Night. Having watched Unkei carve guardian kings in the Meiji era, the dreamer tries carving wood himself and comes to this realization, tinged with bitter criticism of modern Japan.

Sanshirō

“But surely Japan will go on developing from here,” he protested. Whereupon the man, quite unruffled, said, “It will perish.”

A remark from the man (Professor Hirota) sharing his railway carriage on the way to Tokyo. This single dampening word, cast against the exhilaration following the Russo-Japanese War, casts a shadow over young Sanshirō's future and over the novel as a whole.

Even then, he was not alone. Stray sheep. Stray sheep. The clouds are shaped like sheep.

A phrase repeated near the novel's end, when Sanshirō learns of Mineko's marriage. “Stray sheep” translates the English “stray sheep,” and points to Sanshirō himself, unable to decide where he is headed.

“The inside of your head is bigger than Japan,” he said. “Don't let yourself be held captive. However much you think of Japan's good, you'll only end up harming it with your partiality.”

Words of Professor Hirota, who shares Sanshirō's train carriage on the way to Tokyo. Hearing them, Sanshirō realizes how narrow he was in Kumamoto, and feels that he has truly left home.

Pity's just another way of saying you've fallen for someone.

Yojirō's rendering of the English phrase “Pity's akin to love.” Professor Hirota scolds him for its vulgarity, but it is remembered as a translated phrase that overlaps with Sanshirō's feelings for Mineko.

“For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me.”

A verse from the Psalms that Mineko speaks as they part. The scene is celebrated for the way she faintly confesses, through words of repentance, her feelings for Sanshirō and her own choice.

And Then

At the sound of someone's hurried footsteps running past the gate, in Daisuke's mind a huge wooden clog hung suspended from the sky.

An opening written from the drowsy edge of waking consciousness. It begins a novel in which Daisuke, who lives without working, steps toward his feelings for a friend's wife.

Daisuke resolved to ride the streetcar until his own head burned itself to ash.

The final line. As Daisuke sets out to look for work, the whole world appears red before his eyes — a celebrated ending in the history of the modern Japanese novel.

My existence needs you. It needs you, absolutely.

The scene in which Daisuke confesses his feelings to Michiyo. Spoken without sweet words, in a simple and solemn tone, this confession becomes the decision that cuts him off from society.

“Ah, it's moving. The world is moving.”

Words Daisuke speaks aloud on the streetcar near the end, having rushed out to look for work. The beginning of the famous, near-delirious finale in which red things leap one after another into his head.

The Gate

For some time now Sōsuke had brought a cushion out to the veranda and sat cross-legged in a spot that looked sunny, but before long he tossed aside the magazine in his hand and lay down flat.

A quiet opening on a veranda on a fine autumn day. The daily life of a couple who carry a past with them and live at the edge of society is distilled into this one peaceful moment.

He was not a man who could pass through the gate. Nor was he a man who could get by without passing through it. In short, he was one of those unfortunate men fated to stand frozen beneath the gate, waiting for the day to end.

A line describing Sōsuke as he comes down the mountain, having given up on his Zen retreat. It names his position — neither reaching salvation nor able to return to daily life — by layering it onto the title's image of a gate.

What heals all wounds is time.

A maxim Sōsuke has engraved on his heart from his own experience. It is recalled in the scene where, on hearing news of Yasui, the wound of the past that time should have healed opens again.

“It's truly a blessing, isn't it? Spring at last,” she said, her brows lifting brightly. Sōsuke, out on the veranda cutting his overgrown nails, answered, “Yes, but it'll be winter again soon enough,” and went on working the scissors with his head bowed.

The ending. Oyone rejoices at the coming of spring; Sōsuke replies that winter will soon come again. The uneasy daily life of a couple for whom nothing has truly been resolved closes with a quiet exchange.

Omoidasu koto nado

Throw in every chrysanthemum there is — into the coffin.

A haiku of mourning written from his sickbed after his grave illness at Shuzenji, on hearing of the death of Ōtsuka Kusuoko. The fierce command to throw every chrysanthemum there is into the coffin carries the strength of his grief.

Gendai nihon no kaika

The civilization of the West (that is, civilization in general) is internally generated, while the civilization of present-day Japan is externally generated.

The central contrast of this lecture given in Wakayama. It distinguishes the civilization of the West, opening naturally from within, from that of Japan, forced into shape by outside pressure.

To put it in a word, it comes down to this: the civilization of present-day Japan is a superficial civilization that merely skims along the surface.

A sentence widely quoted as the lecture's conclusion. While calling rapid modernization a mere skimming of the surface, it does not tell people to stop, but faces it as an unavoidable reality.

Higan sugimade

Each time I compare myself with Chiyoko, I feel the urge to repeat the words “the woman who does not fear and the man who fears.”

Words spoken by Sunaga in “Sunaga's Story,” comparing himself with his cousin Chiyoko. The contrast between a woman who acts without hesitation and a man who thinks too much to take a step hints at a theme the late Sōseki pursued to the end.

Kōjin

“Death, madness, or else religion. Before me lie only these three.”

Ichirō's confession of anguish, recorded in the long letter of his friend H. A celebrated passage that shows, by laying out three paths, the dead end of an intellectual cornered by the loneliness of the self.

Kokoro

I always called him Sensei.

An opening that quietly begins with a remembrance of the man he called Sensei. This is the tone that carries through the whole novel, one of Sōseki's late masterworks on human solitude and egoism.

I began by flatly declaring, “Anyone with no ambition to better himself spiritually is a fool.” These were the very words K had once used to me, while the two of us were traveling together in Bōshū.

A scene in which he throws K's own words straight back at him, now that K has become his rival in love. This single remark drives his friend to the brink, and becomes Sensei's lifelong burden of guilt.

“Ma'am — please give me your daughter's hand.”

The scene in which Sensei proposes marriage to his landlady. Spoken without a word to K, who loved the same woman, this single line summons his friend's death and a lifetime of regret.

At that moment I felt as though the spirit of Meiji had begun with the Emperor and ended with the Emperor.

Sensei's reflection on the death of the Meiji Emperor. Together with the news of General Nogi's ritual suicide that follows, he comes to layer his own death over the ending of an entire era.

“I am a lonely man.”

Words Sensei lets slip after asking the narrator why he keeps coming to visit. The loneliness of a man who keeps others at a distance even as he longs for them seeps into this brief confession.

But you know, love is a sin. Do you understand?

A cryptic remark Sensei makes about love on the way back from viewing the cherry blossoms. The past with his friend K, revealed later in his testament, gives these words their full weight.

We who are born into this modern age, so full of freedom, independence, and the self, must all taste this loneliness as the price we pay for it.

A passage in which Sensei speaks of loneliness as the fate of modern people. The question that runs through the whole novel—the loneliness born in exchange for individual freedom—is voiced here.

Ordinarily, everyone is good. Or at least, everyone is an ordinary person. That is what makes it so frightening—that at the decisive moment, they suddenly turn bad.

Sensei's answer when talk turns to whether there are bad people in the world. It sounds like a forewarning both of his uncle's swindling of his inheritance and of the betrayal Sensei himself will later commit.

I should have died much sooner—why have I lived until now?

Words K added at the very end of his suicide note. In a letter without a single word of reproach for Sensei, this one line leaves a wound in his heart that never heals.

Before I die, I want to trust someone—even just one person—and die having trusted them. Can you be that one person? Will you be? Are you serious, from the bottom of your heart?

Sensei's reply when the narrator presses him to tell of his past. Having doubted people all his life, Sensei chooses the narrator as the one person he will trust—leading to the testament that follows.

My Individualism

Once I had this phrase, “self-centeredness,” firmly in my own grasp, I became a much stronger man.

A line from a lecture given at the Peers' School. Sōseki, who had reached an impasse measuring literature by Western standards, speaks of the “self-centeredness” he grasped only after long anguish while studying abroad. It is the very core of a lecture that expounds individualism in modern Japan.

First, if you wish to achieve the development of your own individuality, you must at the same time respect the individuality of others.

The first of three principles summarizing the lecture. By joining the establishment of the self to respect for others, these words distinguish Sōseki's individualism from selfishness.

Garasudo no uchi

Looking out from behind the glass doors, my eye is at once caught by a banana plant wrapped against the frost, the branches of a winterberry hung with red fruit, a telegraph pole standing bolt upright without the least reserve—but beyond these, hardly anything worth counting comes into view.

The opening of an essay collection from Sōseki's last years. Starting from the narrow, monotonous view through the glass doors of his study after an illness, his pen quietly traces memories and the events around him.

Michikusa

Hardly anything in this world ever gets settled. Once a thing has happened, it goes on forever. It's just that it changes into all sorts of shapes, so that neither other people nor you yourself can recognize it anymore.

Kenzō's reply at the end of the novel to his wife, who is relieved that the matter with his former foster father is settled. The bitter view of life in this autobiographical novel, drawn from Sōseki's own experience, is distilled into this remark.

Light and Darkness

After the doctor had finished probing, he let Tsuda down from the operating table.

The opening of Sōseki's last novel, left unfinished at his death. Beginning with the raw scene of an examination for an anal fistula, it opens a story that layers bodily unease upon the unfathomability of the human heart.

At any moment this body of mine might meet with some change or other. Not only that: some change may be taking place within it even now, and I know nothing about it. What a frightening thing.

A passage in which Tsuda, after his examination, reflects on a crowded streetcar. It is followed at once by “The world of the spirit is exactly the same,” as unease about changes in the body leads into a fear of the shifting of the human heart.
