About this work

The story of a brother and sister, Pemperu and Neri, as told by a stuffed hummingbird in a glass case in a museum. Miyazawa Kenji draws in a quiet hand what was done to the two when they held out at the gate of a circus the yellow tomatoes they had grown in their field, believing them to be gold coins. Few children's stories have written so piercingly the moment when a child's innocence is hurt by the world's measure.

Summary

In the big glass cabinet at the museum in my town, there are four stuffed hummingbirds. One morning, when I was small, the one perched on the very topmost branch spoke to me in a thin, silvery voice: "Good morning. That boy named Pempel really was such a good child — what a pity it all was." Setting my bag down on the floor and sitting upon it, I settled in to listen to the hummingbird's tale. Pempel and his little sister Neri had once played every day beside their working parents. In time the two of them were left alone, and came to live by tilling a field beside a house in the woods. One year, among the tomatoes they had grown, there was one that ripened a deep, brilliant yellow, gleaming like a jewel. The two of them treasured it and put it carefully away. One day a circus came to town. Hearing that money was needed at the gate, the two children, believing that very yellow tomato to be gold, carried it there in both hands, cradled with care. But seeing what they held out to him, the gatekeeper shouted, "What's this, you brats — don't make a fool of me," and drove them off. Neri burst into tears, and before long Pempel too began to cry, loud and sudden. "You surely don't know a sadness like that." The two of them walked home in silence, choking back sobs now and again. Pempel clenched his fists, and Neri swallowed hard from time to time, as the two of them crossed the pitch-black little hill and made their way home. Having finished the tale, the hummingbird's beak closed once more. I stepped out into the corridor, and, struck by the sheer brightness of the light and by pity for the two poor siblings, wept great streaming tears.

Characters

  • Hummingbird　A stuffed specimen in the museum. Tells the story of the siblings
  • Pempel　The elder brother who grew the yellow tomatoes
  • Neri　His younger sister

The opening (original text)

In the big glass cabinet at the museum in my town, there are four hummingbirds, stuffed specimens now. When alive, they were the small, dear hummingbirds that cry mī-mī and sip nectar from flowers like butterflies. Of the four, I was especially fond of the one perched on the topmost branch, its wings half spread, looking as though it might take flight into the deep blue sky at any moment.

Kiiro no tomato by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I too grew terribly sad, and said, "Well then, hummingbird. Goodbye. I'll come again. But if there's ever anything more you want to say, please say it. Goodbye, and thank you. Thank you, hummingbird" — and, quietly picking up my bag, I stepped out of that room, which seemed like the inside of some brown glass shard, and went quietly out into the corridor. And all at once, struck by the sheer brightness of the light and by pity for those poor siblings, my eyes stung sharply, and the tears came spilling down.
