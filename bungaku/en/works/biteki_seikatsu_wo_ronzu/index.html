About this work

Takayama Chogyū's central essay, published in the magazine Taiyō in 1901, which set off the argument about the aesthetic life that drew in Mori Ōgai and others from the moment it appeared. Chogyū argues here that morality and knowledge are in the end no more than means serving the satisfaction of human instinct, and that true happiness lies in satisfying the original demands of human nature. The aesthetic life means serving life and the body themselves, which stand above food and clothing — a declaration of instinctivism and individualism, recognizing supreme value in the satisfaction of instinct. A piece compressing the thought of Chogyū's last years, when he was deep in Nietzsche, and one that sent great ripples through the intellectual history of Meiji.

Summary

"A man cannot serve both God and mammon" — quoting the words of the ancients, Chogyū poses his question: What is the aesthetic life? It is, he answers, service to "life and body" themselves, which surpass in worth mere food and clothing. As he sees it, moral judgment, which distinguishes good from evil, and knowledge, which pursues truth from falsehood, are not ends in themselves, but ultimately nothing more than necessary expedients toward the satisfaction of human instinct. And yet the people of this world hold fast to these cumbersome expedients as though they were of the utmost importance, parading their morality and boasting of their learning, and in doing so lose sight of the true happiness proper to human life. Is it not folly to spend one's brief span of fifty years on the roundabout procedures of morality and knowledge, and feel no regret for it? To be pitied is not the man who hungers, but the man who knows no sustenance beyond bread; to be grieved for is not the man who is poor, but the man who understands no value beyond wealth and rank — so Chogyū presses on, arguing that wherever the innate demands of human nature are satisfied, there exists, even in the life of a beggar, a paradise an emperor might envy. Let the poor not grieve, let those who have lost all hope not sorrow — the kingdom is always within your own breast, and it is the aesthetic life alone that makes you understand this gospel, he concludes. An essay that places supreme value on the satisfaction of instinct, ringing out Chogyū's loud proclamation of individualism and instinctivism.

The opening (original text)

One: Prefatory Words The men of old have said: a man cannot serve both God and mammon. Take no thought, then, for what you shall eat or drink to sustain your life, nor for what you shall wear to clothe your body. Is not life itself worth more than food, and the body more than raiment? Should anyone, before I set forth my own argument, ask what the aesthetic life is, I would answer: it is service to life and body themselves, which surpass in worth both food and raiment.

On the Aesthetic Life by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Ah, to be pitied is not the man who hungers, but only the man who knows no sustenance beyond bread. Wherever the innate demands of human nature are satisfied, there exists, even in the life of a beggar, a paradise an emperor might envy. To be grieved for is not the poor man, but only the man who understands no value beyond wealth and rank. I cannot believe that there is great value in the life of one who dies without ever understanding love. To be pained over is only the man who thinks of bread and not of life, who worries over raiment and not over the body. Such a man is born without ever knowing what he ought to do. Now, as the affairs of the world grow more turbulent by the day and leave men no leisure for quiet thought, yet still — let the poor not grieve. Let those who have lost all hope not sorrow. The kingdom is always within your own breast, and it is the aesthetic life alone that makes you understand this gospel.
