About this work

Reminiscences Sōseki dictated for Hototogisu before the seventh anniversary of Shiki's death. Shiki installing himself uninvited in his lodgings at Matsuyama, eating grilled eel with a great deal of noise and going home telling him to settle the bill — it begins with what Sōseki calls Shiki's greed for food, and goes on to their student days, when Shiki flourished Chinese poetry and philosophy at him, to his high self-regard, and even to his politician's ambition. He does not prettify his friend, and in the tone in which he says he simply humoured him one senses, all the more, how deep the friendship went.

Summary

Masaoka's greed for food? — Sōseki begins to laugh. When I was at Matsuyama, Shiki came back from China and, without going either to his own house or to any relation's, settled himself in my lodging before I had agreed to it. People warned me that consumption was catching and told me to refuse him, but I let him stay. And then all the haiku people of Matsuyama began coming every day, so that I had no time even to read, and could not help making haiku myself. At noon Shiki would send for grilled eel and eat it with a smacking of the lips. And he ordered it for himself without consulting me. And when he went back to Tokyo he said calmly, "You pay for it, will you," and went home, and after that asked me to lend him money besides. In a letter from Nara he wrote that the sum he had borrowed had been duly spent up in that place. ... Though before that, to be sure, it was always I who was treated by him — and the talk goes back to their student days. Shiki, who never went to lectures, would summon me before the examinations, listen carelessly to what was in my notes, and swallow it whole with "Good, good, I have it." He would carry a brazier into the privy, and on that brazier boil beef and eat it. He showed Tsuki no Miyako to Rohan and was very proud of it, and said that lately he had come to understand the haiku as well, and spoke as if there were no one to be afraid of. In Chinese verse Shiki knew the tones better than I, and when he waved books of philosophy about I was the one who gave way. He was oddly proud and hardly ever kept company with anyone, and yet, for some reason, with me alone he did. "In short, it was I who was the better-natured of the two." He was a man who would not be satisfied unless he was the general in everything, and even when the two of us walked along a road I was always dragged about exactly as he pleased.

Characters

  • I (Sōseki)　The narrator, who kept up the friendship though he was ordered about by Shiki
  • Masaoka (Masaoka Shiki)　A friend of the same age, greedy for food and oddly proud

The opening (original text)

Masaoka's greed for food? Ha, ha, ha. Well now. It was when I was at Matsuyama; Shiki came back from China and turned up at my place. I thought he was going to his own house, but he went neither to his own house nor to any relation's, and said he would stay here. He settled it all by himself before I had agreed to it.

Masaoka shiki by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

At one time Masaoka said he would cast my fortune, and though I had not asked him for that either, he did it. He came with something written on a roll of paper about the length of one mat. Something was written about my becoming an educator and doing this or that, and something was written about a woman besides. That was by way of teasing. Masaoka was a man who wrote letters without limit, and I sent as many back in proportion. They are not left now, but there is no doubt that all of them were foolish things.

Masaoka shiki by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)
