Famous passages by Sakaguchi Ango

Well-known lines from the works of Sakaguchi Ango.

14 passages

Bungaku no furusato

Just as the very absence of morality is itself a morality, the very absence of salvation is itself a salvation.

A passage from an essay that discusses the roots of literature through fairy tales and legends that offer no salvation. It says that it is precisely in stories that push us away that the true face of humanity appears.

Here I see the birthplace of literature—or the birthplace of humanity. Literature begins here—that too is what I think.

The passage in which the phrase that gives the essay its title appears. It says that where a story without salvation pushes the reader away lies the starting point of literature, and of humanity.

Nihon bunka shikan

It wouldn't trouble me in the least if Hōryūji and Byōdōin burned down. If necessary, tear down Hōryūji and build a railway station. The glorious culture and traditions of our people would never perish because of it.

The most provocative passage of this essay on Japanese culture, written during the war. Rejecting a view of tradition that venerates old buildings, it holds that the essence of culture lies in what arises from the necessities of life.

Discourse on Decadence

We must fall all the way down the road of falling, and in doing so discover and save ourselves.

The core of this essay, written for the Japanese people just after their defeat in the war. Rather than returning to the borrowed morality of bushidō or the emperor, it argues, one must find oneself only after falling all the way down.

Human beings do not change. They have only returned to being human. Human beings fall into decadence. Even loyal retainers, even saints, fall into decadence.

A passage that looks steadily at the state of things after the defeat. It is the core argument of the essay: accept decadence as human nature itself.

The war is over. Have the heroes of the Special Attack Corps not already become black marketeers, and do the widows' hearts not already swell with new faces?

A passage confronting the state of society less than a year after the defeat. It looks squarely at the collapse of beautifully told wartime ideals and at how easily people change in order to live.

Human beings live, and human beings fall. There is no convenient shortcut to saving humanity other than that.

A sentence placed after the argument that people cannot be saved by preventing their fall. The pivot of the essay: accept living and falling as one and the same.

It is not because we lost the war that we fall. We fall because we are human; we fall simply because we are alive.

A passage rejecting the view that blames decadence on the defeat. Declaring that decadence is not a product of the times but of human nature, it urges postwar people to look at themselves anew.

The Idiot

In that house lived humans, pigs, dogs, chickens, and ducks, and, truth be told, the buildings they lived in and the food they ate were scarcely different from one another.

A coldly detached opening that sets humans and livestock on the same level. A representative postwar work of Ango's, depicting a man fleeing through a city under air raid together with a feeble-minded woman.

Zoku darakuron

Decadence itself is always a paltry thing, nothing more than evil; yet among its qualities there sternly exists that great reality of human beings called solitude.

A passage from the sequel to “Discourse on Decadence,” probing the value hidden within decadence. Those who fall bear a solitude in which they can rely on no one—and there, it argues, lies the true face of humanity.

In the Forest, Under Cherries in Full Bloom

When the cherry blossoms bloom, people carry bottles of sake and eat dumplings and stroll beneath the flowers, giddy with talk of a magnificent view and a splendid spring — but this is a lie.

An opening that declares a lie the common belief that all is beautiful beneath the blossoms in full bloom. It is Ango's representative work, depicting the void and the terror lurking beneath the flowers.

Beneath the blossoms, though there was no wind at all, it felt as though a great wind were roaring.

The sensation of the mountain bandit as he steps beneath the cherry trees in full bloom. The contradiction of a wind roaring where there is no wind at all names the terror lying beneath the blossoms.

Even now, no one knows the secret beneath the forest of cherry trees in full bloom. Perhaps it was the thing called “solitude.”

The nature of the forest's terror, revealed by the narrator near the end. It suggests that what the man feared all along may have been none other than “solitude.”

Only the petals were left, and a cold emptiness stretched taut.

The final sentence. Beneath the cherry trees in full bloom, both the woman and the man vanish, leaving only petals and a cold void—a beautiful, fantastical ending.
