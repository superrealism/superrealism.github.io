About this work

A piece Futabatei Shimei translated from Turgenev's A Sportsman's Sketches and published in Kokumin no Tomo in 1888 at Tokutomi Sohō's request. "About the middle of September one day I was sitting in a birch wood" — the description of that birch wood, beginning from such an opening, astonished the young literary men of the time, who had not known that nature could be described so in colloquial Japanese. Because Kunikida Doppo, Tayama Katai and Shimazaki Tōson all handed down the shock they felt, it survives in literary history as a translation that changed modern Japanese prose itself.

Summary

In the middle of September, in autumn, "I" had sat down to rest in a birch grove while out with my dog. Under a changeable sky in which fine rain and clear intervals took turns, the faint whispering of the leaves, the light glittering on the wet twigs, the gold of the fallen leaves spread over the ground — the shifting look of the wood is set down in a hushed style. Dozing off and waking, twenty paces away a peasant girl was sitting with her head bent and a bunch of wild flowers on her lap. Her long lashes were wet, and on her cheek the track of tears shone in the evening sun. At every sound she raises her face, and then begins again to sort her flowers. Presently, parting the bushes, there appears a young man of the sort of pampered valet kept in a landowner's house. With an indifferent yawn he says, "Have you been waiting?" and tells her that he leaves tomorrow. The girl, Akulina, begs him again and again not to forget her, to say at least one word to her. But the man only shows off his eyeglass and holds forth with satisfaction on the enlightenment of Petersburg, and sniffs without interest at the flowers she has gathered and lets them drop on the grass. To her plea for "just one word," he throws out, "Enough of this!" and looking at her back as she falls weeping, shrugs his shoulders and walks calmly away. The girl springs up to follow him and cannot stand. Unable to bear it, "I" burst out from behind the tree, and she cries out under her breath and runs off among the trees and is gone. Only the flowers were left behind. "I" pick up the bunch and come out of the wood. The sun hangs low, a fierce wind carries the fallen leaves, and flocks of birds scatter over the fields — ah, it is autumn. Even after I came home the girl's figure was long before my eyes; and the bunch of flowers I brought back is withered, and I keep it still.

The opening (original text)

This "Tryst" is a short piece by Turgenev, a novelist famous in Russia, who died some years ago in France. I have tried translating it at Mr. Tokutomi's request. My translation is, even to me, oddly — well, whatever it is; but the original at any rate is extremely interesting. I was sitting in a birch grove one day in autumn, about the middle of September. Since early morning a fine rain had been falling, giving way at times to warm sunshine; the weather was unsettled. The sky would now be overcast with soft white clouds, and now suddenly clear for an instant in places, and from behind the parted clouds blue sky would appear, bright and gentle as a beautiful eye. I sat and looked about me and listened.

The ending (original text)

I stopped.... A forlorn feeling came over me; through the cheerful though chilly look of things there seemed to show already the melancholy of approaching winter, with nothing of interest and nothing of amusement in it. A wary raven flew high over me, beating the air heavily with its wings; it turned its head and looked at me sidelong, then soared up and disappeared beyond the wood, cawing in broken cries. A great flock of pigeons came sweeping up from the threshing floor and, all at once wheeling into a column, scattered over the field — ah, autumn! Someone was driving over the bare hill, and the empty cart rattled loudly.... I went home; but the figure of the poor Akulina was long before my eyes and would not leave me. Her flowers, long since withered, I keep to this day...

Famous passages

I was sitting in a birch-wood in autumn, about the middle of September.

The opening of Futabatei Shimei's translation of a story from Turgenev's A Sportsman's Sketches. Rendering the shifting moods of nature in fine detail in the new colloquial style, the translation had a great influence on the nature writing of Meiji authors, beginning with Kunikida Doppo's Musashino.
