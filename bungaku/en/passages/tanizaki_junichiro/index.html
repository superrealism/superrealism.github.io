Famous passages by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō

Well-known lines from the works of Tanizaki Jun'ichirō.

11 passages

The Tattooer

It was in the days when people still possessed that precious virtue called folly, and the world had not yet come to grate against itself as harshly as it does now.

A celebrated opening that announces Tanizaki's aesthetic world. Out of a longing for an age when folly could still be a precious virtue, an uncanny story begins to take shape.

Master, I have thrown away, once and for all, that timid heart I used to carry. — And you, it seems, were the very first to become my fertilizer.

The closing words the girl, now tattooed on her back with a golden orb-weaver spider, hurls at the tattooist Seikichi. A single instant in which the one who tattoos and the one who is tattooed change places.

Naomi

I intend, in what follows, to set down as honestly and frankly as I can, just as it happened, the story of a marriage between my wife and myself that I believe has few parallels in the world.

An opening confession in the form of a memoir. It foretells the course by which a man who set out to raise the girl Naomi into his ideal woman ends up her slave.

With this I will end the record of us as husband and wife. Those who find it foolish, please laugh. Those who think it a lesson, please take it as a warning. As for me, I am in love with Naomi, so I cannot help what anyone thinks. Naomi is twenty-three this year, and I will be thirty-six.

The close of the memoir. The confession of Jōji, who though toyed with by Naomi shrugs that he cannot help it since he loves her, ends on a flat line stating their ages.

A Portrait of Shunkin

Shunkin — her true name was Mozuya Koto — was born to a wholesale druggist's family on Dōshōmachi in Osaka; she died on the fourteenth of October, 1886, and her grave lies in a certain Jōdo-sect temple in Shitadera-machi, in that city.

An opening that lines up bare facts as though reading from a gravestone inscription. In a voice like an old document, it dispassionately opens the tale of a blind shamisen master and the extraordinary devotion of her disciple Sasuke.

Early one morning Sasuke secretly brought a maid's dressing mirror and sewing needle from the servants' quarters, sat upright on his bedding, and, gazing into the mirror, drove the needle into his own eyes

The scene in which Sasuke blinds himself so as never to see Shunkin's disfigured face. A style stripped of punctuation tells this extraordinary act of devotion in a single unbroken breath.

In Praise of Shadows

I recall that Master Sōseki, in “Kusamakura,” once praised the color of yōkan; and now that I think of it, is that color not itself somehow meditative?

A celebrated line about the color of a sweet bean paste confection. In this essay, which argues that Japanese beauty shows to best advantage in dim light, the very passage embodies its own theme — that shadow gives birth to beauty.

I have come to believe that beauty lies not in any object itself, but in the pattern of shadows, the play of light and dark, that objects create between one another.

The sentence in which the book's argument is most concentrated. It holds that beauty resides not in things themselves but in the shadow born of light and darkness.

We Orientals create beauty by bringing shadows into being in places that are in themselves nothing at all.

A passage arguing that, in contrast to Western brightness, Japanese beauty is born in dimness. It pins down the core of the Japanese aesthetic: creating shadows where there is nothing, and making them beautiful.

The Makioka Sisters

“Koisan, please, would you...”

The opening of a novel depicting four sisters of Semba, Osaka. This single call to the youngest sister, in the Semba dialect, conveys both the sisters' closeness and a premonition of the Kansai way of life soon to be lost.

The diarrhea did not stop that day either, and it was still going on after she boarded the train.

The last sentence of this long novel. With Yukiko's marriage at last arranged and the train carrying her to Tokyo for the wedding, the narrative comes to rest on a bodily ailment far removed from any festivity — a famously unexpected final curtain.
