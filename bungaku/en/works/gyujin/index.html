About this work

A short story Nakajima Atsushi published in Seikai Ōrai in 1942, one of his Chinese pieces, drawn from the Zuo Commentary. Shuniu, the son born to Shusun Bao of the great house of Lu by a woman with whom he spent one night on a journey in his youth. Because the boy is the exact double of the black, ox-like man who helped him in a dream, Shusun takes him into service. But in time Shuniu, by his cunning, works the household as he pleases and drives Shusun at last to death by starvation — the terror of an inescapable fate, drawn in Nakajima's dignified Chinese-flavoured style.

Summary

Shukuson Hyō, born into a distinguished house of the state of Ro, fled as a young man from civil unrest toward the state of Sei, and along the way, at a place called Kōsō, took a woman for a single night's union. Once he had made his way in Sei, married a wife of proper standing, and fathered two sons, he thought no more of that fleeting encounter by the roadside. It was around this time that Shukuson had an ominous dream: the ceiling above him slowly descended, and just as its unbearable weight was about to crush him, an ugly, black-skinned man, uncannily like an ox, appeared and bore up the weight in his place. Before long, a young man claiming to be the son the woman of Kōsō had borne came to call on Shukuson — and his face was the very image of the ox-like man from that dream. Shukuson named him Jugyū and took him into his household as a servant close at hand. But Jugyū, wielding a bottomless cunning, brought down Shukuson's rightful heirs one after another, and came in time to control the household entirely as he pleased. When Shukuson, now old and fallen ill, took to his bed, Jugyū cut off his food and sealed him away from the outside world, driving him steadily toward death amid hunger and isolation. The ox-man who had once, in that dream, come to his rescue now did nothing but look coldly down upon him. Before the world's unfathomable malice, Shukuson at last lost even the will to resist. Three days later, the celebrated grandee of Ro, Shukuson Hyō, died of starvation. A tale of Chinese antiquity in which Nakajima, in a stately, classical style, depicts the terror of an inescapable fate.

Characters

  • Shukuson Hyō　A grandee of a distinguished house of Ro. Takes into his service Jugyū, the son born of a single night's union in his youth, only to be driven to death by starvation through the boy's cunning
  • Jugyū　The son the woman of Kōsō bore to Shukuson. The living image of the ox-like man who appeared in Shukuson's dream, he wields his cunning to control the household and drives Shukuson to his death

The opening (original text)

When Shukuson Hyō of Ro was still a young man, he once fled to the state of Sei for a time to escape civil disorder. Along the way, at a place called Kōsō on the northern border of Ro, he caught sight of a beautiful woman. The two grew intimate at once, spent a single night together, and parted the following morning as he went on into Sei. Once settled in Sei, he married the daughter of the grandee Kokushi and fathered two sons — and by then he had entirely forgotten that fleeting union by the roadside.

Gyūjin by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Whatever anger had remained in him until just now was overwhelmed entirely by a fatalistic dread. He no longer had the will even to defy this man. Three days later, the celebrated grandee of Ro, Shukuson Hyō, died of starvation.
