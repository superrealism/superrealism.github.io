About this work

An essay serialized in the magazine Keizai Ōrai from 1933. Against a Western culture that has pursued light, Tanizaki argues that traditional Japanese beauty has always lived in half-darkness, and works out an aesthetics of shadow through houses and privies, lacquerware and sweets, nō and bunraku, and the way women were dressed. Between light complaints about modern life under electric bulbs, it sets out what he takes to be the substance of Japanese taste. A classic of writing on Japanese culture, and widely read abroad, especially in architecture and design.

Summary

Beginning with the difficulty of bringing electrical fittings into harmony with a house built in the pure Japanese style, Tanizaki argues through a wealth of examples that Japanese beauty cannot be separated from shadow. He finds the height of elegance in Japanese architecture in a privy set among the trees; he takes up the beauty of lacquerware, which comes alive only in candlelight, the part played by gold-ground sliding doors and screens in gathering light in the depths of a dim room, the color of yōkan tasted in the dark, the glow of an actor's face on the Noh stage, the white faces of the women of former times who lived in the shadows — and so shows how the Japanese senses were refined in darkness and half-light. Then, lamenting the excess of illumination in a modern age that uses electric lights without stint, he ends by saying that he would like to call back the world of shadows we are losing, if only into the realm of literature; and that we might try, by way of experiment, turning off the lights.

The opening (original text)

When a man with a taste for building sets out these days to put up a house in the pure Japanese style and live in it, he takes great pains over how the electricity, the gas and the water are to be fitted, and contrives in every way to make these installations harmonize with Japanese rooms — as anyone will have noticed on entering the rooms of a teahouse, a restaurant or an inn, even if he has never built a house himself. It would be one thing for some self-satisfied devotee of tea to ignore the blessings of scientific civilization and set up a thatched hermitage off in the country somewhere; but if a man has a family of any size and is living in a city, then however Japanese he wishes his house to be, he cannot reject the heating, the lighting and the sanitary arrangements that modern life requires.

In Praise of Shadows by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

My purpose in writing all this, though, is that I wonder whether in some quarter — in literature and the arts, say — a way may not still be left to make up the loss. I should like to call back, if only into the realm of literature, the world of shadows we are already losing. I should like to deepen the eaves of the mansion called literature, darken its walls, push what is too plainly visible into the shadows, and strip away its needless interior decoration. I do not ask that it be done to every house on the street; one such house would be enough. Well — to see how it turns out, let us try switching off the lights.

Famous passages

I recall that Master Sōseki, in “Kusamakura,” once praised the color of yōkan; and now that I think of it, is that color not itself somehow meditative?

A celebrated line about the color of a sweet bean paste confection. In this essay, which argues that Japanese beauty shows to best advantage in dim light, the very passage embodies its own theme — that shadow gives birth to beauty.

I have come to believe that beauty lies not in any object itself, but in the pattern of shadows, the play of light and dark, that objects create between one another.

The sentence in which the book's argument is most concentrated. It holds that beauty resides not in things themselves but in the shadow born of light and darkness.

We Orientals create beauty by bringing shadows into being in places that are in themselves nothing at all.

A passage arguing that, in contrast to Western brightness, Japanese beauty is born in dimness. It pins down the core of the Japanese aesthetic: creating shadows where there is nothing, and making them beautiful.
