About this work

Kitamura Tōkoku's essay on love, famous for the sentence that love is the secret key of human life. Drawing on the lives of Western poets such as Goethe and Byron, it argues why a poet who probes the depths of life should suffer over love and make his wife unhappy. Finding the meaning of love in the conflict between the world of the imagination and the world of fact, it breathed the ideal of love above all into the young men of Meiji: a landmark of Japanese romanticism.

Summary

"Love is the secret key of life" — so Tōkoku begins, with this ringing declaration. Love alone opens the door to life; strip it away, and life holds no savor at all. And yet it is precisely the poet — who above all others should plumb the deepest mysteries of life — who suffers most from love, and commits the greatest sins in its name. Why is it that so many of the West's great poets — Goethe, Byron, Shelley, Milton, Carlyle — met with discord and ruin in their marriages? Tōkoku argues that human beings are born to live within an "ideal world" of their own conception, and in time collide with the harsh "real world." Love is the last citadel to which the defeated general of that ideal world retreats — not mere longing for the opposite sex, but a fortress of the soul, built to defend its ideals. Yet for the pessimist-poet who seeks in love the very incarnation of his ideal, a woman of flesh and blood becomes, in time, a burden, and ardent love cools into quiet affection. The woman, once the representative of all that is graceful and sublime, is then shunned by the poet as the spokeswoman of a vulgar world, and lives out her life in tears. Tōkoku, gazing steadily at this fated tragedy between poet and woman, raises love to the highest value in human life. Confronting love head-on as a matter of the spirit, this essay struck Meiji youth with profound force, and opened up a new, modern Japanese view of love.

The opening (original text)

Love is the secret key of life; only with love does life truly begin; strip love away, and what savor could life possibly hold? And yet that monstrous creature called the poet — who, more than any other, contemplates life, and more than any other, plumbs the deepest mysteries of life — is the very one who, more than any other, commits sin through love. What reason can there be for this? Since ancient times to the present, poets who have failed in love are too numerous to count, so many that women have come to be warned against ever marrying and becoming a poet's wife. Can it be that poets are creatures without feeling? No — indeed, their depth of feeling far exceeds that of ordinary men. Why, then, is it so rare for them to see their bonds through to a happy end? Even Goethe, for all his genius, was made by posterity to be called a man whose head was gold but whose heart was lead — and this was because his fidelity in love was never whole.

The Pessimist-Poet and Womanhood by Kitamura Tōkoku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Ah, how unfortunate is woman — before the pessimist-poet she represents all that is graceful and sublime, and at the same time becomes the spokeswoman of a foul and vulgar world, the object of his scorn, the object of his cold neglect, and to the end of her days, swallowing her tears, dreaming of her beloved both sleeping and waking, resenting him even as she longs for him, she is worn down at last into her grave — how mournful, how mournful this is. "When lovers part in ruin," Byron confessed, "it is as though each were given an eternal night of winter."

Famous passages

Love is the secret key to human life. First there is love, and then there is human life. Take love away, and what color would life have?

The opening sentence of this essay, which struck the youth of the Meiji era like a thunderbolt. Praising love as the key that unlocks the secrets of life, it loudly proclaimed a modern view of love.
