About this work

A posthumous autobiographical work written until just before Akutagawa's death. Only the first chapter, "Raincoat," appeared in his lifetime, in June 1927; the whole was published after he died. Half-transparent cogwheels turning in his field of vision, a ghost in a raincoat, ominous coincidences everywhere he turns: with terrible clarity it records the process by which the narrator, his nerves gone, is driven toward madness and the certainty of death while writing in a Tokyo hotel. The furthest edge of Akutagawa's work.

Summary

On his way to Tokyo for the wedding reception of an acquaintance, the narrator hears in the car a story about a ghost in a raincoat. From then on everything begins to close around him with an ominous meaning — men in raincoats, coincidences of black and white, the words "the god of vengeance." Half-transparent cogwheels begin to turn in the right half of his field of vision and then vanish, leaving a headache behind. Working on a story in a hotel room, he wanders through bookshops and cafés, thinks about the Bible, and remembers that his sister's husband threw himself under a train. His brother-in-law had been wearing a raincoat just before he died. The sense that he is being constantly watched, and about to be punished, never leaves him; outside his hotel room he hears the sound of slippers when there is no one there, and a vision like wings burns itself into his eyes. At last he escapes Tokyo for the house where his wife and children are waiting, and there is no peace there either. "I no longer have the strength to go on writing this" — and the record breaks off with the cry: is there no one who will quietly strangle me while I sleep?

Characters

  • I　A writer, ill in his nerves, driven into a corner by ominous coincidences and visions. The keeper of the record
  • The old man　An old man living in the hotel attic, who urges religion on the narrator
  • My wife　Who is anxious for the narrator, and afraid of the sense that her husband is going to die

The opening (original text)

Carrying a single bag, I had a car race me from a summer resort in the hills down to a station on the Tōkaidō, so that I might attend the wedding reception of an acquaintance. Along both sides of the road the car ran on, there was scarcely anything but pine growing thick. Whether I should be in time for the up train was decidedly doubtful.

Haguruma by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"It was nothing in particular, really. Only I had a feeling somehow that Father was going to die...." It was the most terrible experience of my whole life. — I no longer have the strength to go on writing this. To live on with such a feeling inside one is a suffering beyond words. Is there no one who will quietly strangle me while I sleep?

Haguruma by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

To live on in such a state of mind is an agony beyond words. Is there no one who will quietly strangle me in my sleep?

The closing of this manuscript, written in the year of his death and published posthumously. Placed at the end of a record of nerve-wracking hallucinations, it is the single most famous last line in modern Japanese literature.
