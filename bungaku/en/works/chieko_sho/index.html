About this work

The collection Takamura Kōtarō published in 1941. Written over thirty years, from the end of Meiji into the Shōwa era, its poems, tanka and prose trace his life with Chieko — a woman who had meant to be a painter and married him instead — from their meeting through her descent into madness to her death. It holds "An Artless Tale," in which Chieko says there is no sky over Tokyo, and "Lemon Elegy," on the dying Chieko biting into a lemon: love poems of startling clarity and urgency. Long read as a record of devotion, and one of the central books of modern Japanese poetry.

Summary

Chieko's Sky is made of poems, tanka and prose in which Takamura Kōtarō set down, over some thirty years, his love for his wife Chieko. The opening poem, "To Someone," is a poem of his youth, addressed before they were joined to a woman about to marry another man: "I hate it — that you should go away." In time the two entered a marriage poor but pure, and Kōtarō sang, "You grow more beautiful all the while," of a wife whose beauty grew clearer as she put ornament aside. In "An Artless Tale" he catches the heart of a woman who could not settle to the city: Chieko laments that there is no sky over Tokyo, and says that the blue sky above Mount Atatara at home is the real sky. But Chieko's mind gave way, and her spirit came apart. Even through the days at her sickbed the love between them stayed clear. And then the great elegy, "Lemon Elegy": in the instant when the dying Chieko took the lemon from Kōtarō's hand and bit into it with a crunch, her clouded consciousness came back to itself, and "Chieko became the Chieko she had been / and poured the love of a lifetime into a single moment." At the end stands the prose piece "Chieko's Life," which follows their meeting through to her death and records even the paper cut-outs in which her gift flowered at her sickbed. No other collection has crystallized so purely the love of one woman and the grief of losing her.

Famous passages

Chieko says there is no sky in Tokyo, she says she wants to see the real sky.

The opening of “An Artless Tale.” The gaze of a husband who simply receives and sets down his failing wife's murmured words is distilled into these two limpid lines.

So much had you been waiting for a lemon there on your sad, white, bright bed of death you took the single lemon from my hand and your lovely teeth bit into it, crisp and clear

The opening of “Lemon Elegy,” sung at the death of his wife Chieko. With nothing more than the scent of a lemon and the sound of teeth biting into it, it vividly captures the single moment her sanity returned.

Chieko sees what cannot be seen, hears what cannot be heard.

The opening of “Priceless Chieko.” Reimagining his wife, who suffered from mental illness, as one who sees the invisible, the poet sings of her with deep love and reverence.

Having quit the business of being human without a second thought, Chieko has already gone off beyond nature— I see her back, small and alone.

A passage from “Chieko Playing with Plovers.” In his wife playing with the plovers on the beach at Kujūkuri, the poet sees both the loneliness of her having left the human world and a kind of pure beauty.
