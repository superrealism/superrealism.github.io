About this work

The group of poems Miyazawa Kenji wrote from his sickbed. The title means in the midst of illness, and the consciousness of a man coughing blood with death before him is set down directly as words. "Said with the Eyes" in particular — in which, spitting blood, he looks up at the blue sky and says what a fine wind it is — is among the most limpid things in his work. It closes with the question of what on earth this thing called himself is, and with a taking of refuge in the Lotus Sutra.

Summary

"They say the wind is blowing / on the plume poppies" — this cluster of poems begins from a sickbed where the poet can only hear of the outside scenery secondhand. In the poem that follows, "Said with the Eyes," a man who has coughed up blood and can no longer speak addresses his doctor with his eyes alone: "No good, is it / it won't stop / it's welling up, gushing and gushing," "it seems I'll be dead before long." And yet the man says — "but what a fine wind it is / the clear weather of early spring is near now / and such a beautiful wind comes welling up, brimming, out of the blue sky like that." To your eyes it may look like a wretched scene, but what I see is, after all, only a lovely blue sky and a clear, transparent wind. From there, in short poetic forms, the days of fever and pain are set down one after another: "When it comes round to three in the afternoon," "Though fever rises and my chest grows dark," "My chest now a pale, sickly blue." "And so I suppose I will die before long / what, in the end, is this thing called I" — after that question, turned over and read through again and again without ever finding an answer, Kenji goes on to reduce his own body to molecules and atoms, and further still to a vacuum, and writes that what remains there is only a single law. He gives that fundamental law the name Myōhō Renge Kyō, the Lotus Sutra of the Wonderful Law. "Life too is the life of the Wonderful Law / death too is the death of the Wonderful Law" — the poems close with these quiet words of devotion.

The opening (original text)

Sickbed On the plume poppies they say the wind is blowing On the plume-poppy thicket too they say the wind is blowing Said with the Eyes No good, is it it won't stop it's welling up, gushing and gushing since I haven't slept since last night and the blood keeps coming everywhere around is blue and hushed and deep it seems I'll be dead before long but what a fine wind it is

The ending (original text)

And so I suppose I will die before long what, in the end, is this thing called I however many times I reconsider it, read and search however much I am taught it is this way or that in the end it still has never become clear what this thing called I I devote my life to the Sutra of the Lotus of the Wonderful Law Life, too, is the life of the Wonderful Law Death, too, is the death of the Wonderful Law From this body now, until I attain the body of Buddhahood, may I uphold it well

Famous passages

It's no good, is it — it won't stop — it keeps welling up and welling up — I haven't slept since last night, the blood keeps coming, so — everything around is blue and deep and still — it seems I'll be dying soon. But what a lovely wind this is! The festival of Clear and Bright is near, and so, as if surging up and welling out of the blue sky, such a clean wind comes. Over the young maple buds and the downy blossoms it raises waves like autumn grasses, and the rush mat with its scorch marks is blue as well. Whether you are on your way back from a medical meeting or what, I don't know, but you wear a black frock coat and take such earnest care of me, so I have no complaint even if I die now. Though I am bleeding, that I am so easy and feel no pain — has half my soul left my body, I wonder? Only, because of the blood, I can't say so, and that is hard. From where you stand it must be a terrible sight, but what I can see is only the beautiful blue sky after all, and the wind, clear and transparent.

The whole of “Speaking with My Eyes,” one of the sickbed poems of Shicchū. What the narrator, coughing up blood as he looks up at the doctor, sees is only the blue sky and a clear wind — a limpid gaze in the face of death that moves the reader deeply.
