About this work

A historical story published posthumously in Chūō Kōron in February 1943, two months after the author's sudden death. Zilu, a swaggering swordsman who comes to humiliate Confucius, is struck at once by the man himself and becomes his disciple; single-minded and unbending, he reveres his teacher and finally dies in the coup in Wei. Feeling between master and pupil, the relation of knowledge to action, and the fate of a man who lives by justice, cut in dignified prose. With "Li Ling," the central work of Nakajima's last months.

Summary

Zhong You, styled Zilu, a knight-errant of Lu, sets out in fierce array to humiliate the teacher Kong Qiu, whose reputation as a sage is on everyone's lips. But in the course of their exchange he is struck at once by the greatness of the man, and takes him for his master on the spot. Straightforward beyond compare and a soldier by temperament, Zilu never takes easily to the study of poetry and ritual; yet he reveres his master more purely than anyone, and Confucius in turn knows the qualities of his rough disciple better than anyone and loves him. When the party wanders from state to state and goes hungry in the wilds between Chen and Cai, Zilu is angry but never leaves his master's side. In time he serves the state of Wey and is praised by Confucius for his administration; and then Kuaikui, the heir apparent, raises a coup in the Wey court. Refusing his fellow students' advice to keep out of danger, saying that a man who eats a lord's stipend does not avoid that lord's peril, Zilu rushes alone into the fighting, and under the enemy's blade he ties back the cut cord of his cap and cries, "A gentleman dies with his cap set straight!" — and is cut down. When the news reaches Confucius he weeps aloud; and hearing that Zilu's body had been made into pickled meat, he never again allowed pickled meat to be set on his table.

Characters

  • Zilu (Zhong You)　The most soldierly of Confucius's disciples. He loves his master purely and single-heartedly, and dies in the upheaval in Wey
  • Confucius　Zilu's master, who knows the qualities of his rough disciple better than anyone
  • Zigong　A brilliant fellow disciple, gifted in speech

The opening (original text)

Zhong You, styled Zilu, a knight-errant of Bian in the state of Lu, took it into his head to humiliate Kong Qiu of Zou, the teacher whose reputation as a sage was so much talked of lately. What is a sham sage worth? — and so, with his hair wild and his side locks jutting, his cap hanging loose and his coat cut short behind, a cock in his left hand and a boar pig in his right, he set off in high spirits for Kong Qiu's house.

Deshi by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Confucius, far away in Lu, heard of the upheaval in Wey and said at once, "Chai will come home. You will die." When he learned that it had fallen out exactly as he had said, the old sage stood a long while with his eyes closed, and then the tears ran down. And on hearing that Zilu's body had been made into pickled meat, he had every pickled thing in the house thrown away, and from that time never allowed pickled meat to be set on his table.

Famous passages

Zhong You, styled Zilu, a swaggering man of Bian in the state of Lu, took it into his head to humiliate a certain scholar of Zou, one Kong Qiu, whose reputation as a sage had lately grown loud.

It opens with the entrance of Zilu, who marches in to teach Confucius a lesson. Nakajima Atsushi's powerful work goes on to depict the life of a disciple who serves his master faithfully to the very end.

Their greatness was merely that they were useful for this or that. With Confucius it was entirely different. It was enough simply that there existed a human being named Confucius.

A passage depicting how Zilu, having met Confucius, is won over by the man. Zilu learns that there are people whose greatness lies not in their usefulness but in their very existence.

Of good men winning final victory—whatever may have happened in ancient times, in the present world one hardly even hears of such a thing. Why? Why?

A passage in which Zilu rages at a reality in which the righteous go unrewarded. His straightforward anger, demanding to know what Heaven is watching, lends deep shadow to this story of master and disciple.
