About this work

One of Tsuboi Sakae's central children's stories. It draws the days of Fumie and Yōichi, a sister and brother in a house with one great persimmon tree behind it, through the seasons of the tree. The tree their grandfather was proud of — there was not another like it in the village — is a witness to the family's history, and a source of joy that everyone shares when it fruits. Family affection as a child sees it, and the small events of ordinary living, set down in plain words. Collected in the 1944 volume Umi no Tamashii and published after the war as a book of its own.

Summary

Behind the house of Fumie and Yōichi stands one great persimmon tree, as thick round as a child can hold. Though persimmons are said to bear well only every other year, this tree bore splendidly every year, and it was the boast of their grandfather while he lived. As for how big and how good the fruit was, Uncle Santarō, for one, comes every year with the face of a child to ask for some when they ripen. He says he will have a branch grafted next spring, and every spring he forgets. Under that persimmon tree the elder sister Fumie and her younger brother Yōichi pass their days. Helping in the house, going back and forth with the neighbors, looking after the baby — in each small event as the children see it there lives the affection of a family and the feel of daily life. In time the season comes round when the persimmon leaves turn color and come fluttering down. When the baby, full of milk, shakes his whole body and laughs, the persimmon leaves too seem to be laughing, as though drawn on by that laugh.

Characters

  • Fumie　The elder sister of the house with the persimmon tree
  • Yōichi　Fumie's younger brother
  • Uncle Santarō　A neighbor who comes every year to ask for the fruit when the persimmons ripen

The opening (original text)

I Behind the house of Fumie and Yōichi there is one great persimmon tree. It was a splendid tree, as thick round as a child can hold. There were a number of small trees, but only this one was outstandingly large. A persimmon is said to bear well only every other year, but since this one bore every year, it was their grandfather's boast while he was alive. For there was not another persimmon like it in the village.

Kaki no ki no aru ie by Tsuboi Sakae (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

So she dandled him. Having had his milk from Fumie, the baby was in high spirits with his belly full, and shook his whole body for joy. "It's red — re-e-ed." When Fumie answered him, he laughed again, kya, kya. Two or three persimmon leaves came fluttering down again. They looked truly as though they were laughing, drawn on by the baby's laugh.
