About this work

Nishida Kitarō's first book and his major work, published by Kōdōkan in 1911 and generally taken as the first genuinely original work of philosophy by a Japanese. It treats "pure experience," prior to any division of subject and object, as the sole reality, and attempts to derive knowledge, reality, the good and religion from it. The starting point of the "Nishida philosophy," digesting Western thought on its own terms and fusing it with an Eastern intuition, and a landmark of modern Japanese thought.

Summary

The book is in four parts. In the first, "Pure Experience," Nishida argues that the only reality is "pure experience" — the instant of seeing a color or hearing a sound, in which there is as yet neither subject nor object and the knower and the known are one. In the second, "Reality," he takes that pure experience as his ground and tries to grasp in a single view the whole of what is real, including mind and body, nature and God. In the third, "The Good," he takes up will and action and finds the essence of the good in realizing the true demand within the self and completing one's personality. And in the fourth, "Religion," he places religion, as the conclusion of philosophy, in the state in which the finite self becomes one with the infinite God. In "Knowledge and Love," added at the end, he holds that knowing and loving are alike acts in which subject and object become one — that both are the working by which the self is made one with its object; and he concludes that the more selfless one becomes, the deeper and larger love grows, until it reaches even to the birds and beasts and grass and trees.

The opening (original text)

To experience means to know a fact just as it is. It is to abandon entirely all contrivance of one's own and to know by following the fact. "Pure" means that, since what is ordinarily called experience is in reality mixed with thought of some kind, this is the state of experience just as it is, with not the least deliberation or discrimination added. For example, it means the instant of seeing a color or hearing a sound, before there is any thought that this is the working of an external thing or that I am feeling it, and before even the judgment is added of what this color or this sound may be. Pure experience is therefore the same as direct experience. When one experiences one's own state of consciousness immediately, there is as yet no subject and no object; knowledge and its object are entirely one. This is the purest form of experience.

The ending (original text)

The more we cast away the private self and become purely objective — that is, selfless — the greater and deeper love grows. It advances from the love of parent and child and of husband and wife to the love of friends, and from the love of friends to the love of humankind. The love of the Buddha reached even to the birds and beasts and the grass and trees.

Famous passages

To explain everything by taking pure experience as the sole reality was an idea I had held for a long time.

A passage from the preface. Setting out the plan of taking “pure experience” — direct experience before the division of subject and object — as the sole reality, it marks the starting point of a book often called Japan's first original work of philosophy.
