About this work

A work Hori Tatsuo published in Kaizō in 1937. Taking as material the mid-Heian woman's diary Kagerō Nikki, it retells in modern language the inner life of its author, the mother of Fujiwara no Michitsuna, who set down her days from her marriage to Fujiwara no Kaneie. It opens with the passage from the original — that thinking how insubstantial things are, she feels scarcely present, and might call this the diary of a mayfly — and catches in Hori's own delicate colloquial prose the waiting, jealousy and resignation of a woman in an age when husbands only visited. The starting point of the court pieces that lead on to "The Mayfly Diary" and "Ubasute."

Summary

"Still, when I think how fleeting all things are, it may be called the diary of a mayfly, of a thing that seems scarcely to be there at all" — with these words of the original the story begins. The narrator, "I," was noticed by that gentleman, one of the foremost nobles of the capital; they exchanged poems, and in time she became one of his wives. But by the custom of visiting marriage his coming grows less frequent, and the signs that he goes to other women cannot be hidden. Night after night she waits; and even knowing he will not come she listens, and hears in her ears a knocking at the gate that is not there. In time a child is born, and still her heart is not filled. If she shows her jealousy she becomes ugly in her own eyes; if she puts on a calm face she dries up inside. At times that gentleman treats her roughly; and doing so, "I" perceive, it is rather he who suffers. But he himself will not perceive it. Letting everything be done as he will, "I" find at some point that it is I who am watching his back with a tightening in my breast as he goes briskly away. The places of the one awaited and the one who waits had, at some time, changed about.

Characters

  • I　One of that gentleman's wives, a woman who sets down in a diary her days of waiting
  • That gentleman　One of the foremost nobles of the capital, whose visits grow rare under the custom of visiting marriage

The ending (original text)

And after that, on that night, that gentleman began to talk with me with an affection he had not shown before. As for me, I no longer thought even that a thing to be glad of, but simply let everything be done as he would. And when the next morning came, and he had gone back to his usual briskness of bearing and was about to leave and go home, it was I whose turn it had become, at some point, to gaze suddenly at his back with a tightening in my breast....
