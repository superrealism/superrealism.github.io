About this work

One of Akutagawa's best-known stories, published in the magazine Shinchō in January 1922. Taking its material from the Konjaku Monogatarishū, it is built entirely from the irreconcilable testimony of those involved in the killing of a samurai in the mountains — a woodcutter, a travelling priest, the captured bandit, the dead man's wife, and the victim himself speaking through a medium. The accounts contradict one another and the truth is never disclosed. A work that questions whether anything people say can be trusted, it is one of the sources for Kurosawa Akira's film Rashōmon, and in Japanese "in a grove" has since become an idiom for a case that cannot be got to the bottom of.

Summary

The body of Kanazawa no Takehiro, a samurai of Wakasa, is found in the mountains of Yamashina. Before the police magistrate a woodcutter testifies how he came upon the body, and a travelling priest tells of having seen Takehiro alive, passing by on horseback with his wife Masago. The captured bandit Tajōmaru admits that he killed Takehiro: dazzled by Masago's beauty, he says, he tricked the husband and bound him and violated her, and afterwards, at her own wish, killed Takehiro in a fair fight with swords; where the woman went he does not know. Masago, come to Kiyomizu Temple to confess, says in tears that she despaired when, after her violation, her husband looked at her with cold contempt, and that she drove a dagger into his breast meaning that they should die together — but that she alone could not manage to die. Then Takehiro's own spirit, speaking through a medium, declares that he despaired when his wife, urged on by the bandit, cried out to him to kill her husband, and that after she had fled he pierced his own breast with the dagger. Three confessions, each at odds with the others, and not one thing about the truth is ever made clear.

Characters

  • Tajōmaru　A bandit notorious in the capital. He confesses to killing Takehiro, but his account of the motive and the course of events contradicts the other testimony
  • Masago　Takehiro's wife. Confessing at Kiyomizu Temple, she says that after her violation she killed her husband with her own hand
  • Kanazawa no Takehiro　A samurai of the provincial office of Wakasa, found dead. He tells of his own end through the mouth of a medium
  • The woodcutter, the travelling priest, the constable and the old woman　People on the edges of the case, summoned before the police magistrate to give their fragments of testimony

The opening (original text)

The Testimony of a Woodcutter, Questioned by the Police Magistrate Yes, sir. It was certainly I who found that body. This morning I went as usual to cut cedar on the hill behind. And there in a thicket in the shadow of the hill was the body. Where it was, sir? It would be four or five hundred yards off the Yamashina highroad. A deserted place, with scrub cedar growing among the bamboo.

The ending (original text)

At last I raised my exhausted body from the foot of the cedar. Before me the small sword my wife had dropped lay shining. I took it up and drove it in a single thrust into my breast. Something like a lump of blood welled up into my mouth. But there was no pain at all. Only, as my breast grew cold, everything about me went the more still. Ah, what silence. In the sky above this thicket in the hill's shadow not one bird came to sing. Only a lonely light hung in the tops of the cedars and the bamboo. The light — and even that was fading. Now I could no longer see the cedars or the bamboo. I lay there wrapped in a deep stillness. Then someone came stealthily to my side. I tried to look that way. But by then a faint darkness had gathered round me. Someone — and that someone, with an unseen hand, softly drew the sword from my breast. At the same moment blood welled up once more into my mouth. And with that I sank forever into the darkness between the worlds. …

Famous passages

Yes, that is so. It was indeed I who found the body.

It opens with the words of a woodcutter answering an imperial examiner's questions. Its structure, in which the testimonies never agree and the truth is never reached, left the phrase “in a grove” in the Japanese language as an idiom for the unknowable.

Someone — that someone, with an invisible hand, quietly drew the small sword from my breast. At the same moment, blood welled up once more in my mouth. And with that I sank forever into the darkness between death and rebirth.

The close of the tale told by the dead man, Kanazawa no Takehiro, through the mouth of a medium. Who drew the sword from his breast is never revealed; the testimony sinks into darkness, and the truth is left to the reader.
