What is Gikoten-shugi (Pseudo-Classicism)?

Category
Literary Movement

A literary current of the 1890s (roughly 1887–1896) that, as a reaction against rapid Westernization, turned toward Japan's own classics — above all a reevaluation of Edo-period literature such as Ihara Saikaku's. It is distinguished by inheriting the realist principles championed by Tsubouchi Shōyō and others while seeking its model not in the West but in the classics of its own country. Ozaki Kōyō, writing in a mixed classical-colloquial style learned from Saikaku, depicted manners and the subtleties of human feeling in "Ninin Bikuni Iro Zange" ("Two Nuns' Confessions of Passion") and "Konjiki Yasha" ("The Golden Demon") and pushed the Ken'yūsha into a dominant position in the literary world; Kōda Rohan stood alongside him with "Fūryūbutsu" ("The Elegant Buddha") and "Gojū no Tō" ("The Five-Storied Pagoda"), works underpinned by Eastern ideals and learning, and the two men's heyday together came to be called the "Kōro era." Higuchi Ichiyō's "Takekurabe" ("Growing Up"), which depicted the joys and sorrows of the common people in elegant prose under Saikaku's influence, is also often discussed in connection with this current. It declined rapidly once Romanticism arose after the First Sino-Japanese War and Naturalism then rose to prominence, but its significance in literary history, as an attempt to fuse the tradition of Edo literature with the modern novel, remains considerable.
