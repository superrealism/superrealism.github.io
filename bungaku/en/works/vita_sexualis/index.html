About this work

A novel published in the magazine Subaru in July 1909. The philosopher Kanai Shizuka, thinking it might serve as material for his son's education, writes out the history of his own sexual life from the age of six to twenty-one, plainly and without heat. Intellectually it is a criticism of the naturalism then in fashion, but the subject matter got the issue banned from sale. A provocative work of Meiji literature that treats sex as an object of scientific attention, and one with a strong autobiographical strain.

Summary

Kanai Shizuka, a philosopher, watches the vogue for studies of sexual desire and the way the naturalist novels write of sex as though it were the one great matter of human life, and takes it into his head to write the history of his own desire. At six, the memory of being shown erotic prints on the way home from the temple school. At ten, the picture books he saw at the lord's mansion. At thirteen, boarding at the German-language school, singled out as a good-looking boy by rough older students, and the nights he slept with an old clog beside his pillow to defend himself. At seventeen, his first experience at Yoshiwara, where he was taken by his hearty friend Koga. Kanai sets these memories down with a scientist's exactness, with a wry smile here and there. It is the record of a life lived without anything that could be called a love affair, in which desire was kept under control like a tamed tiger. Reading the finished manuscript over, Kanai decides that it cannot be shown to the world, and that he would not want his son to read it either. And so he writes across the cover, large, in Latin, VITA SEXUALIS, and tosses it into the document box.

The opening (original text)

Kanai Shizuka is a philosopher by profession. The notion of a philosopher carries with it the writing of books of some sort. Kanai, philosopher by profession though he is, has written no books at all. On graduating from the college of letters he is said to have written something distinctly odd, on a comparative study of heterodox philosophy and pre-Socratic Greek philosophy. Since then he has written nothing.

The ending (original text)

But what effect this would have on the mind of the child who read it cannot be measured in advance. What if the child who read it turned out like his father? Would that be his good fortune or his bad? That too is not to be known. There is a line in a poem by Dehmel: "Do not obey him, do not obey him." No — he does not want his own son to read it either. Kanai took up his pen and wrote across the cover, large, in Latin: VITA SEXUALIS And he tossed it into the document box.
