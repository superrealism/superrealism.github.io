About this work

A short story covering a single evening, in which Councillor Watanabe, an official back from Germany, meets again at the Seiyōken Hotel in Kobikichō, which is in the middle of rebuilding, a foreign singer with whom he had an affair during his student years abroad. The two exchange the old banter, but Watanabe keeps drawing a line with his repeated "this is Japan," and is unmoved by her invitation. The half-built hotel, where the hammering never stops, is laid over the Japan of Meiji on its way to modernity, and it ends with the carriage carrying the woman driving off through the Ginza at night. A piece that captures vividly, within one short scene, the cooled mind of a man who has known the West and must live in Japan.

Summary

Councillor Watanabe walks through Kobikichō after the rain and finds the small signboard of the Seiyōken Hotel. Inside, the place is in the middle of rebuilding, and the sound of nails being driven and of the adze echoes through it. Shown up to the salon on the second floor, Watanabe smokes a cigar in a frame of mind so cool that it surprises even him — as though it hardly mattered who it was he was waiting for. At five the workmen go home, and into the quiet comes a woman in a big straw hat. He knows those large brown eyes. The person who says in German that she has kept him waiting is the woman he loved when he was in Germany, now a singer who has drifted here from Vladivostok. She tells him without concealment that she is traveling with a Polish accompanist, and that she means to go on to America rather than count on Japan. Watanabe says that Japan is still under construction, and laughs at himself for having made himself into a thorough philistine. They move to the dining room, and the meal goes on amid meaningless talk with three waiters standing about them. When she says she might give him a kiss, Watanabe only repeats that this is Japan. And when she says at last, meaning it for a joke but hearing her own voice turn serious, "You are not the least bit jealous, are you," Watanabe lifts his glass higher than the flowers on the table and drinks to the name of the man she travels with. The woman's hand, as she smiled a smile that seemed set hard, trembled so that no one could see it.

Characters

  • Councillor Watanabe　A man back from studying in Germany, now serving as an official
  • The woman　A singer who was Watanabe's lover in his German days. She has come to Japan by way of Russia and means to go on to America
  • Kosinski　The Polish accompanist who travels with her

The opening (original text)

Councillor Watanabe got off the streetcar in front of the Kabukiza. Avoiding the puddles still standing here and there in the road after the rain, he walked along the Kobikichō bank toward the Ministry of Communications, thinking that he was sure he had seen a signboard somewhere about this corner. There were not many people about. He passed five or six men in Western clothes, apparently on their way home from an office, talking noisily together. Then a woman in a kimono with a half-collar, who looked like the mistress of a teahouse and seemed to have come out on some errand in the neighborhood, went by at a trot. A rickshaw with its hood still up came up from behind and ran past him. Sure enough, he found a rather small signboard with "Seiyōken Hotel" written across it.

The ending (original text)

Suddenly the woman said, "You are not the least bit jealous, are you." Even while they talked of meaningless things she could not help remembering the old days when, after the Central Theater had let out, they had sat facing each other exactly like this at a table in the restaurant at the top of the Brühl Terrace, and quarreled, and made it up again. She had meant to say it as a joke, and it came out in earnest without her intending it, and that left her feeling vexed. Sitting as he was, Watanabe raised his champagne glass higher than the flowers on the table and said in a clear voice: "Kosinski soll leben!" The woman's hand, as she showed on her face a smile that seemed set hard and silently raised her own glass, trembled so slightly that no one could have seen it. It was still only about half past eight. Crossing the Ginza, a sea of lights, one lonely carriage carrying the woman with her face deep in her veil drove away toward Shiba.
