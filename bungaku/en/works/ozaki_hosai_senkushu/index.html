About this work

"I cough, and even then I am alone"; "I walk round to the back of the grave." A selection from Ozaki Hōsai, a central poet of free-form haiku, arranged in order from his middle-school years to his last days on Shōdoshima. Hōsai gave up an elite career of his own accord and passed his last years as a temple caretaker with nothing at all, and the selection is put together, with short notes for each period, so that his life can be followed through it. A single pared-down line, with neither season word nor the five-seven-five, becomes the shape of solitude itself.

Summary

Middle school, First Higher School, university, Tokyo, Keijō and Changchun, Ittōen, Sumadera, Obama, Kyoto, Shōdoshima — the poems are arranged in ten chapters that follow Ozaki Hōsai's life. The earliest are shapely poems in fixed form with a season word: "The string of a broken kite has caught — a branch of plum," "A little house of bush clover heavy with dew — the edge of town." Having come out of the First Higher School and the Tokyo Imperial University and risen as far as a directorship in an insurance company, he drowned himself in drink and lost his position and his family, and after he threw everything away and entered the Ittōen in Kyoto his poems gradually shed their form. Moving from temple to temple at Sumadera, at Obama, in Kyoto, and at last in the Nangō hermitage on Shōdoshima where he came to rest, he pared his language down to the bone. "There is nothing to put it in, I receive it in both hands." "I walk round to the back of the grave." "Even when I cough, alone." Supported neither by five-seven-five nor by a season word, a single naked line offers a life with nothing in it and a bottomless loneliness just as they are. "Tomorrow New Year's Day comes — the Buddha and I." "The flesh grows thin — it is a thick bone." And with the poems "Become altogether a sick man — the willow threads are blown" and "From behind the spring mountain smoke has begun to rise," Hōsai closed a life of forty-one years.

Famous passages

Even coughing, alone

This anguished cry of free-verse haiku, in a mere handful of syllables, distills to its utmost extreme the solitude of one who is sick and alone.

No vessel to hold it — I receive it in both hands

A verse composed of nothing but two hands held out empty. Hōsai's life, stripped of all possessions, takes shape directly as this haiku.

Such a fine moon — I watch it alone, and go to sleep.

A haiku of gazing at the moon from his solitary hermitage. The loneliness of having no one to share such a moon with and a stillness that is nonetheless content melt together in a few words.

I go round to the back of the grave.

A free-verse haiku of only seven syllables. Presenting nothing but the casual act of walking round to the back of a grave, it leaves to the reader the stirrings of a heart facing the dead.

The flesh wasting away — how thick the bone is.

A haiku that gazes at his own body wasting with illness. In the matter-of-fact observation that the thickness of the bone stands out as the flesh falls away lies the calm gaze of Hōsai near death.
