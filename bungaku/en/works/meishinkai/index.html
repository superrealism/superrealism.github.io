About this work

A popular course of lectures in which Inoue Enryō, the professor of monsters, explained superstition for schoolchildren and their families. Following the chapter on superstition in the newly compiled national ethics reader for elementary schools, he takes up one by one the familiar superstitions — foxes and badgers, tengu, ghosts, lucky and unlucky directions and days. He orders them by his own scheme, which divides the uncanny into the false, the mistaken, the apparent and the true, and concludes that superstition is believing what is not truly uncanny to be so. Told plainly enough for children to read.

Summary

Enryō begins by setting out as the purpose of this book the detailed expansion of the lesson on superstition in the Ministry of Education's state-authorized elementary textbook of morals, which he finds too brief and in places hard for elementary pupils to understand. Taking up one superstition after another as they are handed down in the world — the fox that bewitches people, the badger that drums on its belly, the spiriting away by the tengu, the appearance of ghosts, the good and bad luck of directions and days, charms and divination — he shows for each what arises from a mistaking of facts, what is the contrivance of men, and what can be explained by the ordinary reason of nature. And he divides monsters into four kinds: false marvels, which men have made up in deceit; mistaken marvels, which come of seeing or hearing wrongly; apparent marvels, which the reason of nature can explain; and true marvels, which are genuinely past understanding. That people believe false, mistaken and apparent marvels to be true marvels — that is precisely superstition. Citing Arai Hakuga and Nishimura Tōri, Enryō says that all things in heaven and earth are wonderful, and that the most wonderful of all is the human mind: the mind is "the mother of monsters" and "the chief of monsters." In closing he counsels the young to polish their knowledge, to search out reason, not to fall into superstition, and to act so as not to be ashamed before heaven.

The opening (original text)

Introductory remarks Reading the state-authorized elementary textbook of morals lately compiled by the Ministry of Education, I find that there is in it a lesson on superstition, and that although careful warnings concerning superstition are given, the text is brief and there are places not easily understood by elementary pupils. Accordingly I have given these lectures on the basis of that textbook, expanding and explaining in detail each of the points indicated in it, with the aim of enabling children at school and at home to understand the various kinds of monsters and to see through superstition at a single reading.

Meishinkai by Inoue Enryō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Having explained monsters thus far, if one asks where superstition lies, then it must be said that for people to believe things that are not true marvels — false marvels, mistaken marvels and apparent marvels — as though they were true marvels, is all of it superstition. In an age like antiquity, when human knowledge had not yet advanced, it might be forgiven; but today, when education has spread and learning has opened, and when our country is called by others, and declares itself, the first civilized country of the East, then for its people to be still sunk in the abyss of superstition must be said truly to soil the honor of the state and to injure the good name of the people.

Meishinkai by Inoue Enryō (Aozora Bunko)
