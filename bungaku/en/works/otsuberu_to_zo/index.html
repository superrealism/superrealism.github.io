About this work

A children's story published in the first issue of the magazine Getsuyō in 1926, one of the few Miyazawa Kenji printed in his lifetime. Within the frame of "a certain cowherd tells this," the greedy landowner Otsuberu talks a white elephant into working itself to skin and bone, and is trampled by the herd that comes at the elephant's call — all in a telling full of Kenji's own sounds and rhythms. The end of the exploiter is satisfying enough, yet a shadow falls across the close, where the rescued elephant "laughs sadly." One of his central stories, carrying a parable of labour and exploitation.

Summary

A story told by a certain cowherd. To the place of Otsuberu, a rich farmer doing very well for himself with six rice-threshing machines set up, there comes plodding a white elephant, who settles in. At first the elephant innocently enjoys the heavy work; but the sharp-witted Otsuberu, with clever talk, gets a heavy clock and a chain and weights fastened onto him, and works him cruelly while giving him hardly any straw to eat. Growing thin and weak, the white elephant at last cries up to the red moon, "I am in pain. Santa Maria," and writes a letter to his fellow elephants in the forest asking for help. The herd, when the letter reaches them, fly into a rage and come pouring down on Otsuberu's place in a cloud of dust. From behind his palisade of logs Otsuberu sets his dogs on them and fires his gun, but he cannot hold back the flood of elephants, and in the end he is crushed flat. His companions take off the chain and the weights, and the white elephant says, "Ah, thank you. I really have been saved" — and smiles, for some reason, a lonely smile. It is a fable of what comes to those who exploit others, and of an ending that is not quite bright even in rescue, told in the sounds and rhythms that belong to Kenji alone.

Characters

  • The white elephant　The gentle elephant who settles at Otsuberu's and is set to cruel labor. Growing thin and weak, he asks his companions for help
  • Otsuberu　The greedy rich farmer with the threshing machines, who talks the elephant into working and drives him with a chain and weights
  • The herd of elephants　The white elephant's companions, who receive his letter and come down in a rage on Otsuberu's place
  • The cowherd　The teller of the story

The opening (original text)

...told by a certain cowherd First Sunday Otsuberu is really something. He has six rice-threshing machines set up, and keeps them going with a tremendous racket — non-non-non-non-non-non. Sixteen farmhands, their faces gone completely red, work the treadles and turn the machines, threshing away at rice piled up like a little hill. The straw is flung out behind them and makes a new hill again. The whole place is a strange dim yellow with the fine dust that rises from the husks and the straw, like the smoke of a desert. And through that dim workplace Otsuberu strolls back and forth with his hands clasped behind him, a big amber pipe in his mouth, his eyes narrowed and careful not to let the ash fall on the straw.

Ozbel and the Elephant by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Well, thank goodness. How thin you've grown." They came quietly to his side and took off the chain and the weights. "Ah, thank you. I really have been saved," the white elephant said, and smiled a lonely smile.

Famous passages

Now, Ozbel — he really is something.

An opening narrated by a cowherd. A remark poised between exasperation and admiration establishes, all at once, both the sheer scale of the exploiter and the view of the common people looking up at him from below.

“Oh, thank you. I really have been saved.” The white elephant smiled forlornly as he said it. Hey, [one character illegible], I've told you not to go into the river.

The white elephant's words after his companions rescue him, followed by the last line of the story. This enigmatic ending, in which the narrator suddenly calls out to someone, has stirred readers' imaginations — all the more because one character of the manuscript cannot be read.
