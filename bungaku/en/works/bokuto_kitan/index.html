About this work

Nagai Kafū's best-known novella, serialized in the Tokyo Asahi Shimbun from April to June 1937. The narrator, an old writer, forms a brief attachment with a woman in the unlicensed quarter of Tamanoi, told in an autobiographical voice that suggests Kafū himself. It has a double structure, with the draft of a novel he is planning, "Disappearance," worked in, and it also serves as an elegy for a shabby pleasure district being erased by development. The summation of Kafū's work.

Summary

The narrator, an aging writer, takes to walking in Tamanoi, the quarter of unlicensed prostitutes on the east bank of the Sumida, in search of material for a novel called "The Disappearance," whose hero is a middle-aged man who vanishes from his life. One rainy night, sheltering under the eaves, he falls into talk with O-Yuki, a woman who lives in a house by the ditch, and from then on he visits her without telling her anything of himself. Between the two there passes a strange affection that has nothing to do with money; but the narrator, thinking of his own age past fifty, and with the policing of morals in the quarter growing stricter, keeps himself from going in too deep. Alongside this, the story of Taneda Junpei — the imagined hero who abandons his family and disappears — takes shape. One day late in the autumn the narrator hears from someone that O-Yuki has fallen ill and gone into hospital, and takes the occasion to lay down his pen on this tale from east of the river. The two part without ever knowing each other's real name or where the other lives, and never meet again.

Characters

  • I　A novelist entering old age, walking the streets to gather material for a novel set in Tamanoi. The narrator
  • O-Yuki　A girl working in the unlicensed quarter of Tamanoi, who comes to feel affection for a narrator who tells her nothing of himself
  • Taneda Junpei　The hero of "The Disappearance," the novel the narrator is planning. An English teacher at a private middle school who abandons his family and vanishes

The ending (original text)

Those who were close to me have gone, one after another. And I know that the time when I too must follow them, as they went, is no longer far off. In today's clear weather I shall go and sweep their graves. The fallen leaves must be burying their graves as they bury my own garden. Completed in the eleventh month of the eleventh year of Shōwa, the year of the fire rat

Famous passages

I have almost never gone to see a moving picture.

An opening that begins with the offhand remark of a man who dislikes the cinema. It goes on to tell, in an unhurried voice, of a relationship confined to the rainy season, with a woman named O-Yuki in Tamanoi.
