About this work

Lives of great men are all of Napoleon and Alexander and almost never of scholars — so the physicist Aichi Keiichi lamented, and wrote this biography, following closely the surviving notebooks and laboratory diaries, of Michael Faraday, who rose from a bookbinder's apprentice to build electromagnetism. The author's wish — to show that in the world of learning neither birth nor favour counts, and that a person can get by on ability alone — runs through the whole.

Summary

When one says the life of a great man, it is always Napoleon or Alexander or Gladstone, and of scholars there is next to nothing. To be sure, the lives of heroes are grand and magnificent; but a Napoleon does not appear in every age, and though they may do to read as tales of an age when civilization had not advanced, they are no material for self-improvement. Scientists and philosophers and artists, on the contrary, may exist ten or twenty at a time in a single age, and the more of them there are the further civilization advances. Science in particular, since it uses few words, is common to the whole world, and a discovery in Japan is at once a discovery for the world — so the author writes in his preface, saying that for those who would be scientists a life of a great scientist is wanted. ... Of all the scientists he chose Faraday for two reasons. First, that he had no university education and rose from being simply an apprentice boy. It will be made plain, says the author, that in the world of learning family and favor count for nothing and that a man can make his way by his own arm alone. Second, that the books he bound, the notebooks he wrote, the diaries of his laboratory, the apparatus he used at the moment of his discoveries, and the laboratory itself, all remain to this day as they were. The text follows the boy born the son of a poor blacksmith who was apprenticed to a bookbinder, woke to science by reading the books he bound, and got a post at the Royal Institution by writing out Davy's lectures and sending them to him; and it traces, from the records of the time, the road of his discoveries — electromagnetic rotation, electromagnetic induction, the laws of electrolysis, and the idea of lines of force. A detailed index of names and places is appended at the end.

Characters

  • Michael Faraday　The English scientist who rose from an apprentice at a bookbinder's
  • Humphry Davy　The chemist who brought Faraday into the Royal Institution

The ending (original text)

The reason I chose Faraday in particular among the scientists is, first, that he was a man who had no university education and rose from being simply an apprentice boy. It will be made plain, I think, that in the world of learning nothing depends on the power of family or of favor, and that a man can make his way by his own arm alone. It may also be called a story of rising in the world.
