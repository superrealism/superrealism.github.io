About this work

San'yūtei Enchō's tale of human feeling and worldly success, drawing how Shiobara Tasuke, driven out of Numata in Jōshū and gone down to Edo to serve at a charcoal dealer's in Honjo, builds a great fortune in one generation on nothing but honesty and industry. From the tearful parting with his beloved horse Ao onward, Tasuke's holding to his principles against every hardship was praised as a model for the Meiji ethics readers and transferred to the kabuki stage. It is drawn from the life of a real merchant, of whom it was sung that Honjo had two things too good for it: the Tsugaru lord and Shiobara the charcoal dealer.

Summary

Tasuke, raised as an adopted son in the Shiobara family of Numata in Jōshū, is orphaned when his foster father Kakuemon dies, and finds himself despised — and even marked for death — by the villain Hara Tanji and his confederates, who scheme to seize the family's estate for themselves. Saved from mortal danger by his beloved horse Ao, Tasuke parts from the animal in tears, and, with only six hundred mon in his pocket, abandons his native village and sets out for Edo. Taken on at the charcoal shop Yamaguchiya in Honjo, Tasuke endures every hardship without complaint, working on in silence with nothing but honesty and diligence as his only capital. In time his master comes to trust him and grants him leave to set up his own branch shop, and through scrupulously honest dealing he builds up his fortune, little by little. Never once forsaking his integrity through all his trials, Tasuke at last comes to own as many as twenty-four parcels of land, and grows so prosperous that all Edo sings of him: "Two things too grand for Honjo — a Tsugaru daimyo, and Shiobara the charcoal man." He even restores, in splendid fashion, his ruined family home back in Numata. Though he had no learning, the honesty and filial devotion bred into him carry a single charcoal peddler up to become the great merchant of his age — a tale of a self-made fortune.

Characters

  • Shiobara Tasuke　Adopted into the Shiobara family of Numata in Jōshū. Builds a great fortune in Edo on nothing but honesty and diligence.
  • Kakuemon　Tasuke's foster father. His birth father, by coincidence, bore the very same name.
  • Hara Tanji　A villain who schemes to seize the Shiobara family estate. Marks Tasuke for death.
  • Ao　Tasuke's beloved horse. Saves Tasuke when his life is threatened, and shares a tearful parting with him on the mountain pass.
  • Yamaguchiya　The charcoal shop in Honjo where Tasuke serves. Its master, trusting Tasuke's honesty, grants him leave to set up his own branch.

The opening (original text)

Now, the tale I am about to tell you is called The Life of Shiobara Tasuke — the story of a man who dealt in firewood and charcoal at Aioi-chō, second block, in Honjo, whose trade carried on flourishing right down to the Tenpō era, and who came, in time, to own as many as twenty-four parcels of land. The root of this fortune was a mere six hundred mon that he brought with him from Shimo-Shinden in Numata, in Jōshū. In those days there was even a satirical verse made about him: "Two things too grand for Honjo — a Tsugaru daimyo, and Shiobara the charcoal man" — likening him, in his greatness, to a lord of a hundred thousand koku. And this fortune, whose beginnings lay in a scattering of small coins, was carried on down through five generations.

Shiobara tasuke ichidaiki by San'yūtei Enchō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

After that, husband and wife worked together, side by side, never once growing slack, and their fortune quickly grew, so that Tasuke, just as he had long wished, restored the family home in Numata in splendid fashion, and set up a branch house besides — and to this day the Shiobara family continues on unbroken. In Edo, too, Tasuke applied himself diligently to his trade, and at last built up a great fortune, buying up land parcel by parcel until he owned as many as twenty-four, and was counted among the wealthy of whom it was said, "Two things too grand for Honjo — a Tsugaru daimyo, and Shiobara the charcoal man" — and his house grew ever more prosperous. But his only capital, in all of this, was nothing more than honesty and diligence, and so I beg you all to take this story truly to heart, and not simply hear it out as an ordinary tale of human feeling.

Shiobara tasuke ichidaiki by San'yūtei Enchō (Aozora Bunko)
