About this work

Twenty-three reflections the philosopher Miki Kiyoshi serialized in Bungakukai. Death, happiness, doubt, habit, vanity, the love of fame, solitude, jealousy, success, travel — he takes up the ordinary subjects of life one by one and thinks them through in short, aphoristic fragments. Written under the oppressive air of wartime and yet holding the light of a wholly clear reason: a classic of the philosophical essay, still read.

Summary

"Of late I have come not to think of death as so very terrible. It must be my age" — so the opening chapter, "On Death," begins quietly. As the deaths of those he loves and those close to him grow more numerous, the terror of death rather thins away. He has come to feel himself nearer to those who have died than to those who are being born. After that, subjects close at hand are taken up one after another: on happiness, on doubt, on habit, on vanity, on the love of fame, on anger, on the human condition, on solitude, on jealousy, on success, on meditation, on rumor, on egoism, on health, on order, on sentimentality, on hypothesis, on hypocrisy, on amusement, on hope, on travel, on individuality. Miki explains that happiness, like virtue, is personality itself; declares that jealousy is the most devilish of qualities because it seeks not to raise oneself but to pull the other down; and writes, "Solitude is not in the mountains but in the streets. It is not in one human being; it is in the space between many human beings." Rather than lining up definitions, he starts his words from felt experience, and the chain of fragments lays itself directly over the scenes of each reader's own life. In the afterword he notes that it is not a thing that ought to end here.

The opening (original text)

On Death Of late I have come not to think of death as so very terrible. It must be my age — though I was once a man who thought and wrote so much about the fear of death. I too have reached an age at which more and more of the letters that come unexpectedly are edged in black. In these few years I have more than once been present at the death of a near relation. And I have witnessed that to even the most suffering invalid, peace comes at the moment of death.

Jinseiron nōto by Miki Kiyoshi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This book, from its nature, will need no preface. It is enough to add a short note about how it came to be. These notes, with the exception of the single piece "On Travel," have appeared in Bungakukai since June of the thirteenth year of Shōwa. Of course it is not a thing that ought to end here; it is only that, following the publisher's wish, what has been done so far has been gathered into one volume.

Jinseiron nōto by Miki Kiyoshi (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Happiness is character. The happiest person is one who can at any time cast off other kinds of happiness as easily as taking off an overcoat.

A passage from “On Happiness.” Tying happiness not to mood or possessions but to character itself, it argues that true happiness is the kind that cannot be taken off like an overcoat.

Solitude is found not in the mountains but in the town. It lies not in a single person, but in the “between” of many people.

A passage from “On Solitude.” These words, declaring that solitude lies not in being alone but among many people, are still often quoted today.
