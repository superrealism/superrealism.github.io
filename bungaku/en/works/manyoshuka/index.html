About this work

From the more than forty-five hundred poems of the Man'yōshū, the tanka poet Saitō Mokichi selects about four hundred and adds a short commentary to each. A celebrated introduction that opened for ordinary readers an anthology often avoided as difficult, explaining the movement of a poem, and the work of a single particle, with the eye of sketching from life. Written for the Iwanami Shinsho series, and widely read ever since. A central work of Mokichi's prose.

Summary

"The Man'yōshū is a precious anthology of our country, and anyone may well read it" — beginning so, Saitō Mokichi picks out of the vast sea of the Man'yōshū, volume by volume, the fine poems the nation ought to know, and adds his comment to each of the roughly one tenth of the short poems he selects. He reads the famous poems of Nukata no Ōkimi, Kakinomoto no Hitomaro, Yamanoue no Okura and Ōtomo no Yakamochi not through broad talk about the so-called "Man'yō spirit" or the national character, but through particulars: the working of a single particle or verb, the cadence of a third line or of a closing line. Mokichi's position throughout is that the poem is the master and the commentary the servant, and he asks his readers first of all to read the poems themselves over and over. From the opening "On the great moor of Uchi, setting the horses side by side" to Yakamochi's closing "On this first spring day of a new year's beginning, may good things pile up like the falling snow," he sets the taut cadence of the early Man'yō against the refinement of the later period and tells the whole appeal of the anthology in plain words. It is a book of appreciation, brief and to the point, such as only Mokichi — poet and psychiatrist both — could have written.

The opening (original text)

The Man'yōshū is a precious anthology of our country, and I think anyone may well read it; but there are more than four thousand five hundred poems in it, and to work through them all by consulting a commentary on each is no ordinary undertaking. One method, then, is to make a selection and grow familiar with the poems that way, and that is the method this book takes. My principle in selecting has generally been to pick out the finest poems volume by volume; I aimed at roughly a tenth of the whole, and since I have left out the long poems and taken only the short ones, if the short poems of the Man'yō come to a little under four thousand two hundred, this amounts to about a tenth of them. Such has been my standard; but it comes of having selected, as far as possible, those poems of the Man'yōshū that the people at large ought without fail to know, so that the intention of a book for everyone is carried out in it of itself. A specialist would gradually raise the standard; and readers are free to make out of this book their own selections of three hundred, or two hundred, or a hundred, or even fifty poems. I have kept that much room in it for them.

Man'yōshūka by Saitō Mokichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Such are the poems of the later Man'yō; but if one compares them with the tens of thousands of poems written since the Kokinshū, there is simply no comparison at all. So good are the poems of the Man'yōshū. This poem of Yakamochi's is the last in the anthology; and the Daishōki says of it: "Now this collection begins with the poems in which the emperors Yūryaku and Jomei showed their compassion for the people and their joy at the world being well governed, and sets them down in order; and since it ends with the present poem, blessing the whole work, the two halves answer each other like the lid and the box of a jeweled casket — a sign that what it holds will pass through the ages and never be lost."

Man'yōshūka by Saitō Mokichi (Aozora Bunko)
