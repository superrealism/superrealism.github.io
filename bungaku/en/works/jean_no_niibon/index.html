About this work

Taught the thought of nothingness in a Paris café, the young Frenchman Jean swallowed cyanide while chanting a garbled invocation, and now waits in the clouds on the far shore to be examined for his first Bon festival — a piece from Yamakawa Masao's series "Close Friends." The gate closes at midnight tonight. Sent down to earth by the Buddha's mercy, what he sees instead of Bon lanterns are banners for a midsummer clearance sale flapping under every eave. Postwar Japan seen from a country he had longed for, in light satire.

Summary

The trouble of Jean, born in a country where God is dead, was where the "individual" was going and that there was nothing to hold it up. Taught the thought of "nothingness" by a Japanese in a café at Saint-Germain-des-Prés, and learning that there is no God, that nothingness is the all and the individual is nothing, he became at once a lover of the East, and at the end of last year swallowed potassium cyanide while muttering the invocation. But what came was not "nothingness" but a cloud hanging between heaven and earth. — A long-necked little bird like a dove (in truth, it says, a white elephant) tells him: your first Bon has come too; you may go home. But Jean, a suicide, has no family to call his soul back and no place made ready for him to return to. By a special mercy of the Buddha he is given leave to walk where he likes, with a curfew until twelve tonight. ... After strolling until dusk through the districts of Tokyo, all that came to Jean was disappointment. Beside a river that smells of the drains, a ring of people dancing the "Bon dance" to vulgar music. Nowhere any Bon lanterns, no farewell fires; nothing to be seen but banners for the "midsummer clearance sale" and far too many people trotting about. Are the Japanese really Buddhists? Is this the true state of the Japan he had dreamed of? ... Walking exhausted through the crowds of the busy quarter, he meets the eyes of a beautiful Japanese woman with long black hair, and is caught by the first love of his life. In a corner of a bar the two of them talked of solitude. To deny Japan is the most Japanese thing of all, the woman says. But the clock in the bar was bringing its two hands upright together upon twelve.

The opening (original text)

Inside the cloud Jean went on waiting irritably. Why should the examination of my qualifications alone take so long? The yellow-faced people who had been showing themselves and hiding again among the clouds until a moment ago are not to be seen at all now. Saying something about the first Bon, they have all vanished somewhere. And yet for Jean alone there is still no word. What in the world can this mean? Is it because I am French? Or is it because my death was suicide?

Jan no niibon by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Jean put his palms together. And he answered: "...C'est bon. Namamidābutsu." — But the long-necked little bird like a dove still did not look to him like a white elephant. In the cloud like an eternal twilight, wrapped as ever in thick white smoke, Jean thought that it would take a great deal more time yet, and went on with his invocation and his joined palms. He knew that there was nothing else left for him to do.

Jan no niibon by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)
