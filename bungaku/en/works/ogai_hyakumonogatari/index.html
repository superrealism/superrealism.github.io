About this work

A single evening in which "I" am invited to a hundred-tales gathering held at his villa in Terashima village by Shikamaya, a lavish spender known as the Kinokuniya Bunzaemon of his day, on the night of the river opening. Though it is a gathering where a hundred candles are to be lit and ghost stories told, not one ghost story is ever told; what "I" watch instead is the master with his gloomy face surveying his guests, and the beautiful geisha at his side. The narrator, who defines himself as "a born onlooker," comes to find the same onlooker in the master. Against the expectation of the uncanny raised by the title, this unusual piece of Ōgai's takes the observation of human beings for its very subject.

Summary

Invited by Shitomi, whose hobby is photography, "I" go to a hundred-tales gathering held on the day of the river opening. At the boathouse in Yanagibashi the place is full of faces I do not know, everyone sitting about with nothing to do. Going up the Sumida in a line of roofed boats, we reach an old villa in Terashima village, where behind the hedge stands a ready-made ghost of the kind used by ghost-story tellers in the variety halls, and I feel that we are being made fun of a little. In the room more than twenty guests are already gathered, and on no face is there any sign of tension or curiosity. Among them one man sits alone with empty places all about him: Shikamaya, the master of the entertainment. A man whose lavish spending fills the gossip columns, he sits pale-faced and staring straight ahead without moving. Beside him sits Tarō, said to be the most beautiful geisha in Tokyo, keeping him company without a sign of boredom and watching his humor now and then. To me the two of them look for all the world like a patient and a nurse. Tubs of sushi and buckets like holy-water pails are carried in, and the effects have been carefully thought out, but the party will not warm up. Looking at Shikamaya, I think how since childhood I have been "a born onlooker," unable to throw myself into the middle of anything, and guess that this master too may have become an onlooker through some invisible wound. My thoughts go too to the size of the sacrifice Tarō makes in staying by a man of whom even bankruptcy is now rumored. Before the ghost stories begin, I leave the house in silence. A few days later Shitomi tells me that after I left the master went upstairs with Tarō and went to bed — and I think that an onlooker must, after all, be making fun of people to some degree.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, who thinks of himself as "a born onlooker"
  • Shitomi　A young master of the downtown quarter whose hobby is photography. He invites me to the hundred tales
  • Shikamaya　A lavish spender called the Kinokuniya Bunzaemon of his day. He is the host of the hundred tales, and has a gloomy look
  • Tarō　A geisha said to be the most beautiful in Tokyo, who stays at Shikamaya's side
  • Yoda Gakkai　An old man of letters whom we meet at the boathouse

The opening (original text)

It was, I think, the day of the river opening in a year when for some reason it had been put off until after the hottest part of the summer. A good many years have passed and my memory has grown somewhat dim; but for that very reason certain points have been accented, and lie there in a corner of the lumber room of my brain where old imaginings are stored, colored in a clouded, muddied, and yet strong color. It was of course the first time in my life; and since nothing of the sort seems likely to happen again, I may as well say that I met with an event that came only once in a lifetime. It was my going to a gathering of a hundred tales.

The ending (original text)

It was not a gathering at which one had to take formal leave of the host; and as luckily I knew no one else among the guests, I stood up in silence, put on the old clogs with the teeth worn away on the slant that had been exchanged for mine when we came off the boat, and strolled out of that house of monsters. On the path through the fields in the dusk, where I had to grope until my eyes grew used to it, the insects had begun to chirp faintly in the shade of the grass at the roadside. Two or three days later I met Shitomi, and when I asked him what had happened after that, he said: "You picked exactly the right moment to go. We had been listening to the talk for a while when Shikamaya disappeared; and when we asked, it turned out he had gone upstairs with Tarō and had the mosquito net hung and gone to bed. He is not the sort of man who thinks he may be rude and no matter; he is simply careless, and so he does that kind of thing." An onlooker, I thought, must after all be making fun of people to some degree.
