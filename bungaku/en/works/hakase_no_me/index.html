About this work

Visiting Dr Lorenz, a world authority on animal instinct, the narrator sees behind those clear brown eyes a strange expanse that cannot help drawing a person in — a piece from Yamakawa Masao's series "Close Friends." The old scholar, who knows the faces, voices and marriages of a hundred and thirty ducks, gives a strange cry toward the frozen pond and introduces the one that waddles up as Mrs Davis. A quiet fantasy in which the outside of the human world softly opens.

Summary

When "I" visited the Max Planck Institute while attending a conference, the doctor, sixty-two years old, was waiting alone, leaning against the gate. A plain red brick main building and greenhouse that hardly look like a national institution, and three or four sheds like storehouses. "Here there is no change but the change of the seasons," the doctor laughed, and said, "To tell the truth, the animal called man is too complex and too high-grade for me. I am rather a companion of the fishes and the birds." After some hours of the afternoon in front of the aquarium tanks, the doctor takes the tired "me" to the ducks kept loose on the pond. In a shed like a floating pavilion on the ice, the young assistant's wife, looking through binoculars, tells us that the doctor knows the face, voice, character, habits, married state and even the health of every one of the birds as if he held them in his hand, so that if one is missing he knows it at once. "I look at them in their relations," says the doctor. A bird that becomes abnormal is at once shut out by the flock and driven off. One with a tumor in its brain died in that way. ... Then the doctor began to speak of a certain lady. A Mrs. Davis who had come to see the place turned pale at the voice of one of the ducks and cried, "That is my husband's voice," "That is my husband's face!" Her husband, who had come home from Korea with a fragment of a bomb in his brain, had at some point disappeared. — Suddenly the doctor put his hand to his mouth toward the pond and gave two or three strange cries. Over the ice one duck comes waddling straight toward us. "Allow me to introduce her.... This is Mrs. Davis." The duck perched on the window frame and the doctor were looking at each other with exactly the same eyes.

Characters

  • I　A psychologist who has come from Japan to the congress
  • Doctor Lorenz　A world authority on the endogenous behavior of animals, sixty-two years old
  • Mrs. Davis　A female duck to whom the doctor has given that name

The opening (original text)

It was on a day in early spring some years ago that I visited Doctor Lorenz at the Max Planck Institute. An international zoological congress happened to be held at a university nearby, and having come all the way from Japan I did not want to miss the chance of meeting the famous doctor. The doctor is a world authority on animal instinct — more exactly, on the endogenous behavior of animals. There is probably not a psychologist, and certainly not an animal psychologist, who does not know his name. But what remained most strongly in my impression at that time was the doctor's eyes. They were of a bright, clear, brownish color, and yet they gave a sense of a gentle, boundless expanse behind them, and held something strangely deep, as though they must draw every person down into their depths.

Hakase no me by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In my hotel room I woke drenched in sweat. Looking round the room, still close to darkness, I felt for a while that the doctor's magical brown transparent gaze was watching me from somewhere. That gentle, boundless expanse, that quiet deep world. I am rather a companion of the fishes and the birds. I felt as if I heard the doctor's voice saying so from somewhere. — And then it came to me suddenly. Might not a male duck stray, tomorrow, into that puddle of a pond at the institute? And might not the doctor give it my name?

Hakase no me by Yamakawa Masao (Aozora Bunko)
