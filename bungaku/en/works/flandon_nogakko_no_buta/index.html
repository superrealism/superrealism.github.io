About this work

A pig kept at an agricultural school understands that it is fated to be killed and turned into meat, and, shaking with terror, is pressed to sign a consent to its own death — the children's story in which Miyazawa Kenji asked most fiercely about eating. The reasoning on the human side and the pig's own sense of being alive pass each other without mercy. Kenji's thought as he leaned further toward vegetarianism, crystallized in the form of a fable.

Summary

A certain pig kept at Flandon Agricultural School can turn anything — water, a slipper, even straw, anything at all except metal or stone — into fat and protein. One evening, hearing a first-year student who has just studied chemistry say, "A pig's body is, well, like a living catalyst — just the same as platinum," the pig calculates that his own body, at twenty kan, would fetch six hundred thousand yen, and preens with pride, believing himself a gentleman of the very first rank. But before long, the pig begins to understand what that calculation truly means. One day the headmaster comes and asks the pig to sign a "death consent form," explaining that the law now requires an animal's own consent before it may be slaughtered. The pig trembles with terror, weeps, refuses food, and goes on refusing. But the livestock teacher cuts his rations, and the headmaster comes every day, pressing him: "Sign it." At last, driven into a corner, the pig, his forelegs trembling, presses his mark to the form. From then on the pig is fattened without mercy. Kept from exercise and made to store up fat, he is at last, on a snowy morning, washed and led away. Somewhere overhead a sharp, keen sound rings out, and beside him water gushes and roars. The livestock teacher stands there, pale, breathing hard, a great iron hammer in his hands. The pig gives two small snorts through his nose, and moves no more. "This story, in truth, is simply too pitiful. Let us stop here and go no further," Kenji writes — and, having told how the pig was taken apart into eight pieces and buried in the snow, he sets a coldly shining crescent moon above it all.

The opening (original text)

"...substances other than [metal and stone], it will unfailingly take in, and turn into fat or protein, storing them up within its body." So it was written, and so the assistants and errand boys of the agricultural school's livestock department brought whatever was not literally metal or stone, one thing after another, and flung it in.

Furandon nōgakkō no buta by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This story, in truth, is simply too pitiful. Let us stop here and go no further. In any case, the pig was, soon after, taken apart into eight pieces and piled up behind the stable. It was left steeping in the snow through the night. Now then, my university friends — that night the sky cleared beautifully, Taurus began to glitter, and the silver horn of the twenty-fourth-day moon, that coldly shining crescent, poured its pale, mercury-white light down upon the clouds thereabouts...... The moon passed by in silence. The night grew ever more crisp and clear.

Furandon nōgakkō no buta by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)
