About this work

Struck by Crime and Punishment, and moved at their first meeting as though by a waterfall of a thousand fathoms — Uchida Roan speaks intimately, in eight digressions, of his close friend Futabatei Shimei as he really was. A gourmet and yet careless of everything, fond of popular songs, doting on his dogs and cats, particular about prose, excited by the naval battles of the Russo-Japanese War: a lively collection of anecdotes about the man that no biography could hold.

Summary

A gathering of anecdotes in which Uchida Roan sets down the character of his close friend Futabatei Shimei in eight short chapters. He begins with their first meeting: Roan, whose view of literature had been changed as by a thunderbolt by Dostoevsky's Crime and Punishment, called on Futabatei for the first time at Sarugakuchō, and from his weighty manner and their exchange over the question "what is the purpose of prose?" received the impression of being struck by a waterfall of a thousand fathoms. There follow a night at Tsunokami spent until dawn on the upper floor of a woman photographer's, talking of that woman's life; his particularity about food and his indifference to utensils and clothes; his taste for the popular ballads, the Tokiwazu and the Shinnai, in the manner of a man of Edo; his affection for ugly stray dogs and lost cats, which he doted on like his own children, even acting as midwife at a cat's confinement; the habit by which, while preaching a bold theory of prose, he fretted endlessly over his own spelling of kana and his punctuation; the anecdote of the moonlit night at Tabata when he broke off at "O bright moon..."; and the day he burst in, excited as a chief of the naval staff, at the news of the victory off Tsushima. Touching on the "destructive power" with which he broke down without ceasing both the thought of others and his own faith, Roan takes Futabatei for a "bankrupt of character," full of contradictions, whom one cannot fix as a novelist or a politician or a gentleman; and he closes by quoting two passages from his old diary.

Characters

  • Futabatei Shimei　Properly Hasegawa Tatsunosuke. The author's close friend. In spite of his stern look he doted on dogs and cats and loved the popular ballads, in the manner of a man of Edo
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who first met Futabatei about the time he was struck by Crime and Punishment, and knew him for twenty years thereafter
  • Iwamoto Yoshiharu　Bushō. He praised Futabatei as "a philosopher" and was the occasion of the author's meeting him

The opening (original text)

1. My first meeting with Futabatei It was at the end of the autumn of the twenty-second year of Meiji that I first met Futabatei. Before telling this recollection I must, in due order, say a little about myself. Before that I had often heard of Futabatei from Iwamoto Bushō. Iwamoto was constantly praising the man, declaring warmly, "Futabatei is a philosopher, and a man of large outline besides." Ukigumo was one of my favorite books at the time, and I had read "The Tryst" and "Meguriai" over and over in admiration, so that I wanted very much to meet him once; but the chance had not come.

Futabatei yodan by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Such a man was Futabatei. A novelist who was not in the least like a novelist; a man who aimed at politics and was not in the least like a politician; a man who wished to be in business and was poor in enterprise and indifferent to money; a modest man who hated above all things to lose; a man who hated dogmas and yet clung stubbornly to his own conclusions; and, to add one more, a man of the greatest common sense who had no common sense at all — such a bankrupt of character, full of contradictions, who because of those contradictions ended a whole life in destruction. Let me quote two passages from Futabatei's old diary as material for calling up his figure and his character. "He who has not thought of casting off this world cannot know the true worth of this world." "When my spirits are low I think that going out might distract me a little; and yet it seems rather pleasanter to shut myself in on purpose and seek out my own torment."

Futabatei yodan by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
