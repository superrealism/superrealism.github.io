About this work

The third of Sōseki's early trilogy, serialized in the Asahi Shimbun from March to June 1910. A husband and wife who came together by betraying his closest friend live quietly, almost in hiding from the world; seeking release from that guilt the husband goes to the gate of a Zen temple, and can only stand before it. Introspective and still, a fitting close to the trilogy.

Summary

Sōsuke and his wife Oyone, living quietly in a rented house at the foot of a cliff, have a past they can tell no one. Oyone had been as good as the wife of Sōsuke's closest friend Yasui, and their coming together had forced Sōsuke to give up his studies and withdraw from the world, and long years had gone by with no word between him and Yasui. No child of theirs lives, and Sōsuke passes his days as a poorly paid clerk in a government office. One day he learns that Yasui, back from Manchuria, has been coming and going as a guest at the house of his landlord Sakai, and he is shaken. Afraid of running into Yasui, Sōsuke goes to a Zen temple at Kamakura in search of peace of mind, but comes down from the mountain after some ten days without having attained enlightenment. In the end he is told that Yasui has left for Manchuria without their having met, and he is relieved. When the long winter ends and the spring sunlight comes onto the veranda, Sōsuke and Oyone are glad together that spring has come round at last; but from Sōsuke's mouth comes the remark that yes, but it will soon be winter again.

Characters

  • Nonaka Sōsuke　A poorly paid clerk living quietly with his wife in a rented house at the foot of a cliff. The protagonist, who suffers under the sense of an old sin
  • Oyone　Sōsuke's wife, who had once been as good as Yasui's wife
  • Yasui　Sōsuke's former closest friend, betrayed over Oyone and cut off from him ever since
  • Koroku　Sōsuke's younger brother, who turns to his brother and sister-in-law when he cannot pay his school fees
  • Sakai　Sōsuke's landlord, a man of means who had known Yasui in Manchuria

The opening (original text)

Sōsuke had brought a cushion out to the veranda a while before and had settled himself comfortably cross-legged in what looked like a sunny spot; but presently he threw down the magazine he had been holding and lay over on his side. It was fine enough weather to be called an autumn day, and in so quiet a street the clatter of the clogs of people going by in the road came cheerfully to his ears.

The ending (original text)

Sōsuke went home and repeated to Oyone the exchange about the warbler. Oyone looked through the glass of the paper doors at the bright sunlight and said, "How grateful one is. Spring has come at last," and her brow cleared. Sōsuke went out to the veranda and, cutting his long-grown nails, answered, "Yes, but it will soon be winter again," and went on working the scissors with his head down.

Famous passages

For some time now Sōsuke had brought a cushion out to the veranda and sat cross-legged in a spot that looked sunny, but before long he tossed aside the magazine in his hand and lay down flat.

A quiet opening on a veranda on a fine autumn day. The daily life of a couple who carry a past with them and live at the edge of society is distilled into this one peaceful moment.

He was not a man who could pass through the gate. Nor was he a man who could get by without passing through it. In short, he was one of those unfortunate men fated to stand frozen beneath the gate, waiting for the day to end.

A line describing Sōsuke as he comes down the mountain, having given up on his Zen retreat. It names his position — neither reaching salvation nor able to return to daily life — by layering it onto the title's image of a gate.

What heals all wounds is time.

A maxim Sōsuke has engraved on his heart from his own experience. It is recalled in the scene where, on hearing news of Yasui, the wound of the past that time should have healed opens again.

“It's truly a blessing, isn't it? Spring at last,” she said, her brows lifting brightly. Sōsuke, out on the veranda cutting his overgrown nails, answered, “Yes, but it'll be winter again soon enough,” and went on working the scissors with his head bowed.

The ending. Oyone rejoices at the coming of spring; Sōsuke replies that winter will soon come again. The uneasy daily life of a couple for whom nothing has truly been resolved closes with a quiet exchange.
