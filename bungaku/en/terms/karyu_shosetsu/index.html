What is Karyū Shōsetsu (Pleasure-Quarter Fiction)?

Category
Form / Genre

Fiction set in the world of the licensed quarters and geisha, the so-called "flower and willow world" (karyūkai), depicting the loves, feelings, pride, and joys and sorrows of courtesans and geisha and of their patrons and those around them. Its subject recalls the tradition of Edo sharebon and ninjōbon, which captured the manners and witty talk of the pleasure quarters, and many writers took it up from the Meiji era onward. In the 1890s Saitō Ryokuu, in "Abura Jigoku" ("Oil Hell") and "Kakurenbo" (both 1891), portrayed with irony and humor the fortunes of naive young men who set foot in the quarters, while Hirotsu Ryūrō left a representative work of the "serious novel" in "Imado Shinjū" ("Love Suicide at Imado," 1896), depicting the doomed love and double suicide of a Yoshiwara courtesan. Izumi Kyōka portrayed the quarters as a world of romantic beauty in "Onna Keizu" (1907), in which the Yanagibashi geisha Otsuta appears, and in "Nihonbashi" (1914), which depicts the pride and passion of the geisha of Nihonbashi. The foremost writer in this field is considered to be Nagai Kafū, whose "Udekurabe" ("Rivalry," 1916–17), capturing the lives and maneuverings of the geisha of Shinbashi, is regarded as the representative work of modern pleasure-quarter fiction. In the Shōwa era, too, Kafū kept his eye on the women of the city's back corners in works such as "Hikage no Hana" (1934), which depicts the shadowed life of a woman living as an unlicensed prostitute and the man she keeps. Okamoto Kanoko's "Rōgishō" ("The Old Geisha," 1938) casts a light of its own on this lineage by portraying the inexhaustible vitality of an aging geisha who has achieved success and fame.
