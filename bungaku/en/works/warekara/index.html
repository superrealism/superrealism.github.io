About this work

A novel Higuchi Ichiyō published in the magazine Bungei Kurabu in May 1896. Machiko, a wife whose father is a moneylender and whose mother abandoned husband and child out of a longing for wealth, is tormented by the fear of being abandoned by a husband who is rising in the world. An offhand kindness to a student is worked up into a scandal by a maid's slander, and she is cast out unilaterally by a husband who keeps a mistress and a child. A full-scale novel from Ichiyō's last months, drawing sharply a woman's having nowhere to go and an inherited fate.

Summary

Machiko, wife of Tamamura Kyōsuke, lives in comfort on the fortune left by her late father, yet lies sleepless through frosty nights, tormented by anxious thoughts of her husband, who has risen in prominence in political circles and spends his nights away from home. Machiko's father Yoshirō was a moneylender so feared he was called the "Red Devil," but in his younger days he had been a poorly paid official, living humbly yet happily with his beautiful wife Mio. On a day of flower-viewing, Mio caught a glimpse of the glory and splendor of the nobility, and her heart, seized by longing for wealth, drove her in time to abandon both the young Machiko and Yoshirō and run away. Betrayed, Yoshirō became a changed man, devoting himself single-mindedly to amassing a fortune, and died estranged from Machiko, who so resembled her mother. Bearing her mother's likeness, Machiko, out of her own sense of having nowhere else to turn, relies wholly on her husband — yet the more Kyōsuke rises in stature and accomplishment in the world, the more she comes to fear that he will someday grow to despise her, unchanged as she remains. It happens, at just this time, that Machiko shows some small, unthinking kindness to Chiba, an honest young student-boarder in their house — giving him a kimono, and the like — and this is twisted by the resentful maidservant Fuku into a scandal, which reaches her husband's ears. Kyōsuke himself keeps a mistress and even a child by her in Iidamachi, and has done so for more than ten years — but, mindful of public attack and his own reputation, he allows Machiko not a single word of explanation, and orders her to live apart from him in Yanaka. Asking what crime she has committed, clinging to his sleeve and weeping, Machiko is shaken off by Kyōsuke, who, without so much as looking back, declares — "Machi. I shall not see you again."

Characters

  • Machiko (Omachi)　Wife of Tamamura Kyōsuke. Daughter of a moneylender and a mother who ran away from home; relying on her husband out of her own sense of having nowhere else to turn, she is tormented by fear of abandonment and, after a baseless scandal, is finally cast out
  • Tamamura Kyōsuke　Machiko's husband. Rising in prominence in political circles, he nonetheless keeps a mistress and child in Iidamachi, and divorces Machiko unilaterally to protect his own reputation
  • Chiba　An honest young student-boarder from the countryside. Machiko's unthinking kindness toward him is twisted by a maidservant's slander into a scandal
  • Yoshirō　Machiko's father. Betrayed by his wife, he becomes a moneylender feared as the "Red Devil," and grows estranged from Machiko, who resembles her mother
  • Mio　Machiko's mother. Seized by longing for wealth, she abandons the young Machiko and Yoshirō and runs away

The opening (original text)

On a frosty night grown late, a wind that seemed to come from nowhere in particular crept in through a gap in the side-door, and even the faint rustling of the paper screen sounded pitiable and lonely, with the master of the house away — until the clock in the bedroom struck twelve, and still the lady of the house could not, however she tried, fall asleep, turning over again and again, until she grew a little unnerved by it herself, and her thoughts began to wander from one useless corner of the world to another, until they settled on how, this time last year, her husband had gone constantly, without fail, to the Kōyōkan restaurant.

The ending (original text)

"Get up and go, now," he said. "That is far too cruel a thing to say," she answered. "If I have done some wrong, why will you not simply scold me for it? I will not accept being told to leave so suddenly, without a word of reason" — and she wept; but Kyōsuke would not so much as turn to look at her. "There is reason enough," he said. "Treat it as something out of the ordinary course of things — it would only pain you to hear each of your offenses listed out one by one. The rickshaw is already waiting; you have only to get in it." And with that he rose at once and made to leave the room. She ran after him and caught at his sleeve, but he shook her off, snapping, "Won't you let go, you shameless woman?" "Is this truly how you mean to treat me, no matter what?" she said. "Do you mean to cast me aside as something the world has thrown away? I am a woman alone, with no one in this world to help me — there can surely be no good reason for casting off so small and helpless a creature as myself. Do you mean to throw me away so splendidly, and keep this house all to yourself? Then try it — try casting me aside, and see. I have one thought, and one thought only, in my heart" — and she glared up at him, white-faced, unyielding; but he only thrust her off and, without so much as a backward glance, said: "Machi. I shall not see you again."
