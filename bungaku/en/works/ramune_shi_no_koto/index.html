About this work

The talk begins from Miyoshi Tatsuji's flat assertion that lemonade marbles were invented by a certain Monsieur Ramune. The first person to eat a pufferfish, the master mushroom-gatherer who died of a poisonous one, and the padres who found a word for love — an essay by Sakaguchi Ango about those who have changed the way things are.

Summary

Kobayashi Hideo and Shimaki Kensaku come to Odawara to fish for sweetfish, and over a meal at Miyoshi Tatsuji's house, the talk turns idly to soda pop. Kobayashi remarks that whoever invented the marble stopper that pops up with a fizz inside a bottle of ramune, only to expire the moment he'd invented that one thing, must have been an odd sort of fellow. At this Miyoshi straightens himself up and declares, with utter confidence, that the man's name is in fact known — that ramune is called ramune because it was invented by a man named Monsieur Lamennais, and that this is even recorded in a French dictionary. Later, when Ango checks the Petit Larousse himself, all he finds is the philosopher Félicité de Lamennais, whose portrait, for some reason, bears a striking resemblance to Kobayashi Hideo — and from this joke the essay sets off in an unexpected direction. Nothing around us remains in its purely natural state. Before the practice of preparing pufferfish was ever established, there must have been dozens, hundreds of martyrs along the way. In the middle section, Ango tells of being served some unidentifiable mushroom every night at a hot-spring inn in Shinshū. The master mushroom-gatherer who had guaranteed, absolutely, that the mushrooms were safe, is himself poisoned by his own mushrooms and quietly expires. This village, Ango says, had no Monsieur Ramune of its own. The final section turns to the age when the Christian missionaries first arrived and, struggling to find a word for "love," coined the term o-taisetsu. The scale of the result does not matter, Ango concludes — only those who commit themselves wholly to the pufferfish, wholly to the ramune bottle, have ever changed the way things are — and that alone, he says, is enough to make it a man's life's work.

The opening (original text)

Kobayashi Hideo and Shimaki Kensaku came to Odawara to fish for sweetfish, and over a meal of sweetfish at Miyoshi Tatsuji's house, the talk happened to turn to ramune. Whoever invented that little marble that bubbles and fizzes up to seal the bottle, Kobayashi said, must have been an odd fellow indeed, if he expired having invented that one thing alone. At this Miyoshi straightened himself up, looked around at the rest of us, and declared: actually, the name of the man who invented the ramune marble is known.

Ramune shi no koto by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In a manner of speaking, the writer of frivolous fiction, too, is his own kind of Monsieur Ramune. The discovery of that little marble, bubbling and fizzing up to seal a bottle, is a laughably trivial thing. And is not the writer who makes a life's work of trifling tales of love and romance every bit as laughable as that Monsieur Ramune? And yet the scale of the result is beside the point. Only those who commit themselves wholly to the pufferfish, wholly to the ramune bottle, have, in any case, ever changed the way things are. That alone should be enough. And if that is so, then it is more than enough to make it a man's life's work.

Ramune shi no koto by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)
