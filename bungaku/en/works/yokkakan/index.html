About this work

Futabatei Shimei's translation of a short story by Garshin. The original came out of the author's own experience serving as a private in the Russo-Turkish War, and caused a great stir in Russia when it appeared in 1877. A volunteer, shot through both legs and left behind on the field, spends four days beside the rotting corpse of the enemy soldier he himself killed — and it is carried through to the end on his monologue alone. Futabatei, an introducer of Russian literature, put it into Japanese around the time of the Russo-Japanese War. The rough, breathless colloquial prose carries over intact the indictment of a world that records war only as one soldier.

Summary

In the middle of a charge through the wood, "I" run my bayonet into a big enemy soldier hidden in a hawthorn thicket. Running out into a field and firing two or three shots, I find that my comrades have all rushed on and I alone am left there. Coming to myself, I have been shot through both legs and cannot stir. The stars come out and the moon rises. The large black thing lying five paces off is the enemy soldier I killed. I shout for help and no one answers; only the crickets and the moonlight answer. Unable to bear my thirst, I drag my aching body to the corpse and drink from his water bottle. Now I can live several days more — but is that not only stretching a suffering of three days out into a week? Again and again the thought crosses my mind of the English rifle lying beside me and of making an end at one stroke. With each sunrise the corpse beside me falls further to pieces. The hair comes away, the skin splits, maggots swarm, and at last even the trace of a face is gone, and a skeleton with only the buttons of the uniform shining smiles at me. "So this — this is war; this is its very shape." Before a companion who says nothing, "I" go on asking. For what fault did this man die? He was taken as an Egyptian peasant and came because he was told to come; and what grudge had I against him, that I killed him? And I too went off to the war dazzled by my fancies of volunteering and of love of country, and made my mother and my betrothed Marya weep. When our cavalry appeared on the bridge my desperate cries were drowned by the hoofs, and as I fell the water from the bottle was drunk up by the dry ground. The third day, the fourth — while the rustling branches whisper at my ear, "There, he is dying," "He is out of sight now," a corporal's voice rings out: "Bring a spade! There are two more here. This one is one of the enemy too!" They are about to bury me when at last they see. Carried on a stretcher and bandaged, I wake in a field hospital of the division. A famous doctor says: "You have got off lightly! You will do now. We have taken a leg from you, though."

Characters

The opening (original text)

I remember how we ran through the wood, how the bullets sang, how the branches we caught at were torn away, how we forced our way through the hawthorn bushes. The firing grew thicker. Something red began to flicker beyond the edge of the wood. Sidorov, a very young soldier of the first company — "how did he get into our line?" flashed through my head — suddenly sat down on the ground and looked at me in silence with great frightened eyes. From his mouth a stream of blood was running... yes, I can see it now.

Yokkakan by Futabatei Shimei (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

When I came to myself I was in a field hospital of the division. At my pillow were an army surgeon and a nurse; and among them was the familiar face of a certain famous doctor from Petersburg, bending over my wounded leg. The doctor was busy for a while about the leg with his hands covered in blood, and then turned to me. "You have got off lightly! You will do now. We have taken a leg from you, though — but what of it, my dear fellow, one leg like that is nothing at all. Can you speak yet?" I could speak. And so I told them the whole of it, from beginning to end.
