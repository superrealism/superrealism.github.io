About this work

The novel Dazai Osamu serialized in the magazine Shinchō in 1947. Through a mother and her two grown children, born into an aristocratic family now sinking, it sets those who go down together with the old morality against those who search, even so, for a new way to live, in the upheaval of values after the war. It struck home hard enough to put the phrase "the setting-sun class" into the language, and stands with No Longer Human as Dazai's major work and as one of the defining books of postwar Japanese literature.

Summary

Kazuko, daughter of a fallen aristocratic house, has given up the Tokyo house and lives modestly in a villa in Izu with her ailing mother and her brother Naoji, home from the war and addicted to drugs. Their mother, refined but unable to keep pace with the times, quietly weakens, and Naoji too walks his road to ruin between dissipation and self-loathing. Kazuko is secretly drawn to Uehara, the decadent writer who was Naoji's teacher, and chooses as her own revolution to throw off morality and conceive his child. Her mother dies; Naoji too leaves a testament and takes his own life; and Kazuko alone, carrying a child, sets her resolve to live on into the new age. The close, written as a last letter to Uehara, records both the passing of the old morality and the fierce will of one who means to live in spite of it.

Characters

  • Kazuko　The protagonist, born into a fallen aristocratic house. She sets out to throw off the old morality and choose a new way of living
  • Naoji　Kazuko's brother. Home from the war and addicted to drugs, he takes his own life after dissipation and self-loathing
  • Mother　The mother of Kazuko and Naoji. Keeping her refinement to the last, she weakens, unable to keep pace with the changing age
  • Uehara Jirō　The decadent writer who was Naoji's teacher, and the man Kazuko loves

The opening (original text)

At breakfast in the dining room my mother took a spoonful of soup, sipped it, and gave a faint cry. "Oh." "A hair?" I wondered whether something unpleasant had got into the soup.

The ending (original text)

There was one more small victim. Uehara. I have no wish now to ask you for anything; but for the sake of that small victim there is one thing, only one, for which I would ask your leave. It is that I be allowed to have your wife hold the child I bear — once will be enough. And that at that moment I be allowed to say this: "This is Naoji's child, that he had by a woman in secret." Why I should do so is the one thing I can tell no one. No — I do not properly understand myself why I want to be allowed to do it. But I must do it, come what may. For the sake of that small victim, Naoji, I must do it, come what may. Does it displease you? Even if it does, please bear with it. Take it for the one faint piece of spite left to a woman who has been cast off and half forgotten, and grant it, I beg you. M.C. — My Comedian. 7 February, Shōwa 22.

Famous passages

Human beings are born for love and for revolution.

Kazuko's declaration that we are born for love and revolution. It distills the passion of a woman living within a declining aristocratic household, and stands at the very heart of the novel.

But we intend to fight the old morality to the very end, and to live like the sun.

The closing of Kazuko's letter to Uehara. Her resolve to bear a child out of wedlock is declared, flatly, as a war against the old morality.
