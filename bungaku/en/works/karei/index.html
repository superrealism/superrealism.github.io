About this work

A short story Okamoto Kanoko published in Bungakukai in 1939. A loach restaurant on a hill in the uptown quarters has hung out a shop curtain dyed with the word life for generations. Through the eyes of Kumeko, the daughter who is to take it on, it draws the invisible passion — the house spirit, one might say — that haunts the family trade. An old metal engraver who has come down in the world and yet comes every night to beg for loach soup, and the girl who sees in him the likeness of her dead grandfather. A fine piece, drawing symbolically and in concise prose the karma of a person possessed by a craft, and the vitality of the ordinary women who sustain it.

Summary

Halfway down a slope that runs from the high ground of the uptown quarter to the low town, there is a famous loach restaurant with an old shop curtain on which the word "Life" is dyed out in white. This house, where loach and catfish and other strengthening foods are served cheaply, has never wanted for customers. It is run by a family of women only, and the narrator, Kumeko, sits between the kitchen and the counting desk as the daughter who is to succeed to it. To that shop there comes almost every night an old man who begs desperately, with his wasted body, for loach soup. This old man, Tokunaga, was once a fine metal engraver and is now come down in the world and cannot even pay properly for what he eats. Kumeko's mother serves him his loach soup without a sour look, and Kumeko too feels in that dogged appetite something that cannot be let alone. In time Kumeko comes to see that her dead grandfather too was a man possessed by his art and his pleasures who lived in this house, and that what has fed and upheld those possessed by an art has been an unseen power in this shop that may be called the "spirit of the house." The karma of an old engraver who cannot give up his thirst to create even in his ruin, and the tough vitality of the townswomen who take it in silence and feed him, are drawn by Kanoko with the curtain reading "Life" for a symbol, in a short piece with a deep resonance.

The opening (original text)

On the high ground of the uptown quarter there is a crossroads where the tram lines meet. Between the arms of the crossroads one narrow road branches off again and runs down a slope toward the valley of the low town. Halfway down that slope, facing the precincts of a Hachiman shrine, there is a famous loach shop. The entrance is opened in the middle of a polished lattice of fine bars, and an old shop curtain hangs there. On the curtain the word "Life" is dyed out in white in a flowing hand. Loach, catfish, soft-shelled turtle, blowfish, and in summer sliced whale — because these foods are said to become the strength of the body, the founder of this shop long ago named it "Life," thinking it a splendid idea.

Karei by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"The shop is busy," she says, and holding her breast with her sleeve she goes back to the shop alone. She sits down inside the window. Old Tokunaga, growing thinner and more wasted, comes every night to beg desperately for his loach soup.
