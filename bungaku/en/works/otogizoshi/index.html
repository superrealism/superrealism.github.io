About this work

A set of linked stories written in 1945, at the end of the war, and published by Chikuma Shobō that October. A father reading a picture book to his child in an air-raid shelter retells four old tales — "The Stolen Wen," "Urashima," "Crackle Mountain" and "The Sparrow Who Lost Her Tongue" — freely, as stories for adults. Comedy and tragedy of character, the karma of falling in love, husbands and wives at cross purposes: he breathes the truth of human psychology into familiar frames, in the finest display of his storytelling.

Summary

Four stories spun out in a father's mind as he reads a picture book in an air-raid shelter. In "The Stolen Wen," an old man fond of a party joins the demons at their revel, dances, and has his wen taken off; the earnest old man next door does the same and is given a second wen to carry. It is the tragicomedy of temperament — of a man made wretched though he has done nothing wrong. In "Urashima-san," the genteel Urashima Tarō is taken by a sarcastic turtle to the Dragon Palace, where the princess entertains him without a word; and even the jeweled casket that makes him three hundred years old when he opens it is said to be a mercy, the mercy of forgetting. "Click-Clack Mountain" is retold as the tragedy of an ugly badger of thirty-seven who has fallen for a rabbit like a beautiful girl of sixteen, and comes down to the one thing he lets slip as he sinks with the mud boat: that to fall in love is a man's karma. "The Tongue-Cut Sparrow" draws the misunderstandings between a silent, spineless old man and his grasping, managing wife, and the old woman dies in the snow with the great wicker basket still on her back. The old man, risen in the world on the gold from that basket, gives a faint wry smile whenever he is complimented, and murmurs: "No, no — it is all thanks to my wife. I gave her a hard life."

Characters

  • The father　A writer who spins the stories out while reading a picture book in the air-raid shelter
  • The old man of the wen　A drink-loving old fellow who dances at the demons' revel and has his wen taken off
  • Urashima Tarō　The genteel eldest son of the house at Mizunoe in Tango
  • The badger of Click-Clack Mountain　An ugly male of thirty-seven, in love with the rabbit
  • The old man of the tongue-cut sparrow　A silent old fellow with no backbone

The opening (original text)

"Ah, there it goes," says the father, and lays down his pen and stands up. He does not get up for a mere warning; but when the anti-aircraft guns begin, he stops work, puts the padded air-raid hood on his five-year-old daughter, gathers her up in his arms and goes into the shelter. His wife is already crouched at the back of it with their two-year-old son on her back.

Otogizōshi by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Whether it was thanks to that gold or not, the old man before long entered the service of the state, and rose at last, they say, to be chief minister of the whole country. People called him the Sparrow Minister, and put it about that his rise was the fruit of the love he had shown the sparrow in the old days. But every time he heard such flattery the old man is said to have smiled faintly, wryly, and answered: "No, no — it is all thanks to my wife. I gave her a hard life."

It is what one might call the tragicomedy of character. Beneath human life, this problem is always flowing.
