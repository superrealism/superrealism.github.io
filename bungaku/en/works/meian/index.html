About this work

Sōseki's last novel, written until just before his death and left unfinished. It dissects, in closer detail than anything he had written before, the fine egoism and psychological manoeuvring between husband and wife, relatives and friends. Around Tsuda, his wife Onobu, and Kiyoko, whom Tsuda once loved, it follows exactly how people bare the self without meaning to. Said to aim at the state he called "following heaven, leaving the self," it breaks off in the middle of the scene in which Tsuda meets Kiyoko again at a hot-spring inn, with only the word "unfinished" after it.

Summary

Tsuda Yoshio, an office worker, is about to undergo an operation, and alongside his new life with O-Nobu, the wife he has lately married, his mind is held by his own past. He once loved a woman named Kiyoko deeply, and she left without a word of explanation to marry another man. Carrying that wound, Tsuda married O-Nobu at the urging of those around him. Clever and proud, O-Nobu keeps herself on guard, trying to make sure of her husband's love, and among the people about them — Tsuda's sister O-Hide, the meddlesome Madam Yoshikawa and the rest — vanity, calculation and private designs are tangled together. Madam Yoshikawa, who sees straight through Tsuda's heart, quietly sends him off, on the pretext of a rest cure, to the hot-spring inn where Kiyoko is staying. Without telling his wife, Tsuda goes, and at last meets Kiyoko again. Why did she abandon him? — and as he is still trying to take the measure of her smile and solve that riddle, he returns to his room, and there the novel breaks off for ever with the two words "unfinished."

Famous passages

After the doctor had finished probing, he let Tsuda down from the operating table.

The opening of Sōseki's last novel, left unfinished at his death. Beginning with the raw scene of an examination for an anal fistula, it opens a story that layers bodily unease upon the unfathomability of the human heart.

At any moment this body of mine might meet with some change or other. Not only that: some change may be taking place within it even now, and I know nothing about it. What a frightening thing.

A passage in which Tsuda, after his examination, reflects on a crowded streetcar. It is followed at once by “The world of the spirit is exactly the same,” as unease about changes in the body leads into a fear of the shifting of the human heart.
