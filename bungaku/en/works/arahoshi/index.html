About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Kōdan Zasshi in 1944. Shun'e, a young monk at the Zen temple Shōheiji in Musashi, is tormented in his search for suchness — the truth that never changes — and grows so wild that he denies the buddha images themselves, and is called the wild monk. A story of religious seeking, drawing how a man who has searched in vain for a way beyond life and death comes to understanding, in the middle of Hideyoshi's attack on Oshi castle, through the terrible conviction of one warrior.

Summary

Shun'e, a monk at Shōhei-ji, a great Rinzai temple in Musashi province, was once the bright, precocious son of a poor rural samurai family, but after six years of ascetic training in Kyoto and Kamakura, he returns transformed into an imposing, powerfully built man, and comes to be called, for his taciturn and violent manner, the "Wild Monk." What has taken hold of him is a hunger for suchness — the absolute, unchanging truth that will not perish even when heaven and earth themselves perish. He climbs to the top of a torreya tree and shouts that he is searching for suchness; he digs up the ground behind the main hall to more than his own height; and at last he shouts down the temple's own image of Śākyamuni Buddha, denouncing it as nothing but a copy of the son of some petty lord of India, for which the monks bind him and shut him away in the sutra repository. How is the contradiction of life and death to be resolved? Shun'e's anguish only deepens. In the eighteenth year of Tenshō (1590), during Toyotomi Hideyoshi's siege of Odawara, at Oshi Castle in Musashi, besieged by Ishida Mitsunari and his forces, Shun'e at last finds his answer on the battlefield: the fierce conviction of a single samurai who vows that he will never enter Buddhahood, but will be reborn seven times, ten times, to protect his ancestral land and his lord. To live not as life, to die not as death, but to live only for that single vow of protection — was this not itself the path beyond life and death? Shun'e tears off his prayer beads, borrows the armor of a fallen samurai, and enters the castle himself, fighting like an asura and meeting a magnificent death in battle, leaving behind, for Hanayo, the girl who adored him, only the promise that all of this belongs to the next life. A story of the outer limits of religious seeking, written by Yamamoto during the war.

The opening (original text)

It was not so very long ago that Shun'e of Shōhei-ji came to be called the Wild Monk. ...Shōhei-ji was one of the great Rinzai temples of Musashi province, but by that time the prestige of the so-called Five Mountains of the Kantō had already faded, no figure of any real distinction had appeared there in some time, and it was, in truth, the most decayed period of the Rinzai sect, which still traced its lineage back to the glory days of the three masters Ōtōkan, so that even Shōhei-ji, which carried on the old tradition of Daitoku-ji, had already lost its former grandeur.

Arahōshi by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

At that time, among the defending troops, there was one warrior in a monk's tonsure who fought with especial ferocity, as the soldiers of the Asano army saw for themselves. He wore armor with its skirt-guards torn and tattered, took up a spear, and pressed forward without ever once looking back, plunging straight into the very center of the attacking force, fighting like an asura, and meeting his death there. Long afterward, whenever the men of the Asano army spoke of that day, they would say to one another, "That was surely a samurai of some real name and standing. ...An enemy, but truly, what a pity to lose such a warrior."

Arahōshi by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)
