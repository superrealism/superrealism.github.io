About this work

A short story published in Taiyō in 1907. It draws, with irony and at a distance, a middle-aged man abnormally fixed on looking at young and beautiful girls — a man with, so to speak, the girl disease. Losing himself every morning on the commuter train over young ladies and schoolgirls, he comes to an unexpected end, and through it the futility of a perverse fixation on beauty is set out coldly. Representative of Tayama's naturalism.

Summary

There is a middle-aged man living out at Sendagaya. A government clerk, he has an abnormal fixation on looking at young and beautiful girls — a "maiden sickness," as it were. Every morning, boarding the Yamanote line, he is drawn out of himself by the beautiful eyes, the white napes, the black hair and the slender fingers of the young ladies and schoolgirls in the carriage, and forgets where he is. Though he has a wife and children, he thinks of some pretty girl he saw in the street and frets over whose wife she will become, and passes the day in fancies of his own. One morning, in the usual crowded carriage, his heart is taken by a beautiful young lady and he gazes at her in a daze. Pushed by the other passengers until his body is nearly out of the car, and still not taking his eyes off her, he lets go of the brass rail as the train starts, and tumbles onto the line like a great ball. An up train comes at that unlucky moment, and he is horribly killed, one long red line of blood staining the rail. It is a naturalist story in which Katai draws, coolly and with irony, a perverse fixation on beauty and the empty ruin at the end of it.

The opening (original text)

1 About the time when the seven-twenty up train on the Yamanote line goes past below the cliff at the Yoyogi tram stop, shaking the ground, there is a man walking steadily across the fields of Sendagaya. There is no day on which this man does not pass; on rainy days he drags an old pair of boots along the deep mud of the field path, and on windy mornings he goes by with his hat pushed back to keep the dust off; and the people of the houses along the way know his figure at a distance, so that there is even an officer's wife who shakes her sleepy husband awake in the spring, saying that the man has gone by already.

Shōjobyō by Tayama Katai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The whistle blew and the car had gone a yard or two and had suddenly gathered speed; and then, by some chance — probably because two or three of the passengers beside him lost their balance and fell against him — his hand, while he was still gazing in a daze at the young lady's beauty, came away from the brass rail, and his great body turned a perfect somersault and rolled, for all the world like a large ball, down onto the line. The conductor's cry of warning came too late, or too early: an up train came shaking the ground at that unlucky moment, and in an instant the large black mass was dragged along several yards, and one long line of red blood stained the rail. The emergency whistle split the air and shrilled out.
