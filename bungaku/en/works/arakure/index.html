About this work

One of Tokuda Shūsei's central works, a novel following the life of one woman, serialized in the Yomiuri Shimbun from January to July 1915 and published by Shinchōsha in September of that year. Oshima, headstrong, refuses the marriage arranged by her foster family and runs from the house, and lives on stubbornly through marriage after marriage and separation after separation, drawn in a realist hand that refuses sentiment. Sōseki said it made one feel that this was what reality was: one of the central works of naturalism.

Summary

O-Shima, brought up in an adoptive family, is given to understand at eighteen that her adoptive parents mean to take a cousin of hers as her husband; and being a strong-willed girl who dislikes men, she runs away from the house. Coming up to Tokyo, she goes from place to place through a string of marriages and separations, and gets over her hardships by her native stubbornness and her love of work. In time she sets up house with Junkichi, who deals in tailoring, and together they build the business up; but her unsatisfied heart still does not rest. Her heart moved by a skilled young workman, O-Shima speaks to him just as she pleases while Junkichi is away.

Characters

  • O-Shima　The heroine, who runs away from her adoptive family and lives stoutly through marriage after marriage and separation after separation
  • Junkichi　O-Shima's husband, who keeps a tailoring business
  • The young workman　A skilled workman employed at Junkichi's shop, by whom O-Shima's heart is moved

The opening (original text)

When O-Shima was given to understand by her adoptive parents that a husband was shortly to be taken into the house for her, no clear thought at all arose in her head. O-Shima, who had turned eighteen, had by then the reputation in that neighborhood of disliking men. She need not have done anything of the kind: the family she had been adopted into was of a standing at which she might have passed perfectly well for a pretty girl of marriageable age, like the daughters of the town houses, by practicing needlework or the koto. But she had not been born with clever hands, and did not much like sitting still indoors; and so from childhood she had often gone out to play in the earth of the fields, or to plant rice with the young men, or to work at the reaping.

The ending (original text)

"I thought I would take a rest myself at a time like this. But it is dull alone, you know." In a high-spirited mood O-Shima spoke to the young workman, who had come dressed unusually neatly, and to Junkichi, who was uneasy at her unrestrained tone. "If I say the doctor advised me to come and take the waters, that settles it. It may be that I shall leave that shop, and have this man do the cutting, and set up on my own." So O-Shima said to Junkichi as well, hinting at the plan she had lately been turning over.

Famous passages

When her foster parents first let slip that an adopted husband would soon be coming for her, no clear thought of any kind yet rose in Oshima's mind.

An opening that begins with a marriage arranged without her consent. It depicts Oshima, a woman who keeps working without being swept along by her circumstances — a high point of Japanese Naturalist literature.
