Biography

Born in Koishikawa, Tokyo, in 1879; his real name was Sōkichi. After dropping out of the Chinese-language department of the foreign-language school attached to the Higher Commercial School, he apprenticed himself to Hirotsu Ryūrō to train as a writer. From 1903, at his businessman father's wish, he traveled to the United States and France, and the experience bore fruit in "Amerika Monogatari" ("American Stories") and "Furansu Monogatari" ("French Stories"). On his return he was recognized for "Jigoku no Hana" ("Flowers of Hell"), highly praised by Mori Ōgai, and on the recommendation of Ōgai and Ueda Bin he was appointed a professor at Keio University, where he edited the magazine "Mita Bungaku" and launched the careers of up-and-coming writers, including Tanizaki Jun'ichirō. Growing ever more devoted to the flavor of old Edo, he built a distinctive literary world depicting the customs of the old downtown and a beauty on the verge of extinction in works such as "Sumidagawa" ("The River Sumida"), "Udekurabe" ("Rivalry"), and "Tsuyu no Atosaki" ("During the Rains and After"), and kept the diary "Danchōtei Nichijō" throughout his life. His late masterpiece "Bokutō Kitan" ("A Strange Tale from East of the River") is considered the culmination of Kafū's literature. He died in 1959 at his home in Ichikawa, at the age of 79.
