About this work

A historical story Mori Ōgai wrote in 1912 out of the shock of Nogi Maresuke's suicide, published in Chūō Kōron, and placed at the starting point of his many historical works and biographies. It takes the form of the testament that Okitsu Yagoemon, an old retainer of the Hosokawa house, wrote on the night before following his dead lord in death, setting down in a concise and dignified epistolary style how a quarrel with a colleague over incense wood he had been ordered to procure led him to cut the man down, and his resolve to die with the lord who saved his life. A landmark piece in which Ōgai, at the end of Meiji, asked about a samurai's service and about death in a lord's train.

Summary

With the first anniversary of the death of Hosokawa Sansai (Lord Shōkōji), lord of the Higo Kumamoto domain, due the next day, the aged retainer Okitsu Yagoemon Kageyoshi resolves to take his own life before his late lord's grave, and on the night before, sets down for his descendants the full account of the matter. In his youth, while still serving Sansai, Yagoemon had been sent to Nagasaki together with his fellow retainer Yokota Seibei to seek out a rare stick of kyara aloeswood for use in the tea ceremony. Bidding against an official of the Sendai Date house for a truly magnificent piece of kyara, Yagoemon drove the price ever higher, and Yokota mocked him for it, saying it was folly to pour such a fortune into a mere scrap of wood to be burned in a brazier. Yagoemon, who held his lord's command above all else, and Yokota, who argued only for practical gain, fell into a fierce quarrel, and when Yokota at last drew his short sword and struck at him, Yagoemon cut him down with a single stroke. When Yagoemon offered to disembowel himself for it, Sansai pardoned him, judging that he had acted only out of devotion to his lord's command, and that very stick of aloeswood went on to earn even the Emperor's praise, becoming a treasured glory of the Hosokawa house. To repay the debt of having his life spared and remade, Yagoemon resolves in his heart to follow his lord in death. The day after finishing his testament, he calmly disembowels himself in a temporary hut built at the foot of Mount Funaoka. Inspired by the junshi (ritual suicide in loyalty to one's lord) of Nogi Maresuke, this was the historical tale in which Ōgai first took up the theme of junshi as his subject.

Characters

  • Okitsu Yagoemon Kageyoshi　An aged retainer of the Hosokawa house. A stalwart samurai who holds his lord's command above all else. The protagonist, who sets down in his testament his resolve to follow his late lord Sansai in death
  • Yokota Seibei　A fellow retainer who travels with Yagoemon to Nagasaki to seek aloeswood. Argues with Yagoemon for practical gain, and is cut down after drawing his short sword and striking at him
  • Hosokawa Sansai (Lord Shōkōji; Tadaoki)　Yagoemon's lord. Pardons him for cutting down Yokota, judging that he acted out of devotion to his lord's command, and so saves his life. Yagoemon follows him in death

The opening (original text)

Tomorrow, this humble servant's long-cherished wish of many years is at last to be fulfilled, and I am to carry out, without fail, my disembowelment before the grave of my lord, His Lordship of Myōkai-in (Lord Shōkōji). I therefore wish to set down, for the sake of my descendants, the full particulars of this matter, and take up my brush here at the house of my younger brother Matajirō, in Kyoto.

The Last Testament of Okitsu Yagoemon by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

On the second day of the twelfth month of Shōhō 4, Okitsu Yagoemon Kageyoshi paid his respects at the grave at Kōtō-in, and entered the temporary hut built at the foot of Mount Funaoka. Advancing onto the tatami, he took the short sword in his hand. Turning to Nomi Ichirōbei, who stood behind him, he called out, "I beg your service." Over his white robes, he cut his belly in three strokes. Nomi struck once at his neck, but the cut fell short. Yagoemon said, "Pierce my throat." But before Nomi could bring his blade down again, Yagoemon had already breathed his last. Around the hut, the old and young of Kyoto, men and women alike, gathered in crowds like a fence to watch. Among the topical verses making sport of the event was one that ran: "A peerless name he raises to the very clouds — Okitsu, his voice echoing as he follows his lord and cuts open his belly" (a verse punning on the name Okitsu Yagoemon).
