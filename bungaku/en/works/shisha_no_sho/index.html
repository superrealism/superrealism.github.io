About this work

Orikuchi Shinobu's central work, in which folklore scholarship and poetry come together, published in the magazine Nihon Hyōron in 1939. The setting is Yamato in the Nara period. In his grave on Mount Futakami, the soul of a prince who met a violent death wakes from long sleep, and his longing revives for the one woman he ever saw, Mimimo no Toji. Meanwhile the young lady of the southern branch of the Fujiwara sees, in the setting sun at the equinox, the vision of a figure hanging over Futakami, and strays as if led by it into a mountain temple forbidden to women. As the dead man's longing and the living woman's faith cross, she spins lotus thread and weaves a mandala, hoping to lay his soul to rest. A solemn and mysterious story, worked out of the legend of the Taima mandala, the tale of Princess Chūjō, and Orikuchi's idea of the visiting god.

Summary

Mount Futakami, near the capital at Nara. In the tomb at its summit, the soul of "that person" — a prince suspected of treason, who met a violent death by the pond of Iware — wakes slowly out of a long sleep in a darkness like ice. What burns steadily in his revived consciousness is an unbreakable longing for Mimimo no Toji, the woman he saw only once while he was alive. Listening to the dripping of water, drip, drip, drip, the dead man calls that face out of the dark. At the same time, in the capital, the young lady of the southern branch of the Fujiwara is passing her days in a devotion deep enough to copy out a thousand volumes of the Sutra in Praise of the Pure Land. One evening at the spring equinox, in the light of the sun setting between the male and female peaks of Futakami, she sees vividly the divine figure of a face she knows. Her soul carried off by it, she slips out of the house without telling anyone, follows the mountain road as that face draws her, breaks the boundary forbidden to women and strays deep into the precincts of the Manpōzōin at Taima. The temple is thrown into an uproar, and she is made to undergo a long seclusion to atone, shut up in a hut in the mountain shade. There an old reciter of Taima comes and tells her the stories of the distant ancestors of the Fujiwara and the old beliefs handed down in that place. Before long the young lady begins to see the soul of "that person" appear naked in the darkness. Trying to cover and wrap that cold figure of the dead, she spins thread from the stems of lotus, sits at the loom, and weaves the noble form she saw into a mandala. The longing of the dead and the prayer of the living meet on Mount Futakami and are raised into an act of requiem. On the woven surface there appear at last the figures of thousands of bodhisattvas springing from the earth. It is a story in which Orikuchi draws, in the language of a poet, the shape of the ancient soul and the origins of faith.

The opening (original text)

The sleep of that person came slowly to its waking. In the blackness of night, in something cold and pressing that lay stagnant there, he felt his eyes beginning to open. Drip, drip, drip. What comes as if conducted to the ear — is it the sound of water falling? In a darkness that seems to freeze, of itself lash draws away from lash. The knees, the elbows, seem to be recovering the feeling that had been buried in them; something is ringing in that person's head. The muscles that have stiffened over the whole body are beginning to cramp, with a faint sound, as far as the palms of the hands and the soles of the feet.

The ending (original text)

The women and the young attendants, not noticing the hours as they passed, sat without stirring, only staring in a daze at the mist of light that was opening before the lady. And at the instant when the young lady laid down her brush, let a gentle smile fall on the backs of these people kneeling in their circle, and quietly but without a sound went out of the hut at Yamada, not one of them noticed it. Still less was there anyone who saw that, as she vanished at the door, something ran in a fine line down the shining cheek of the lady as she looked back.

Famous passages

That one's sleep was slowly waking. In the pitch-black night, amid something colder and heavier still that lay stagnant there, he became aware of his eyes coming open. Shita, shita, shita. Was it the sound of dripping water, that came as if trickling into his ears?

The opening of the novel. An unforgettable beginning that depicts, from the sound of dripping water — “shita, shita, shita” — a dead man modeled on Prince Ōtsu waking from his long sleep inside a tomb on Mount Futakami.
