About this work

A classic introduction to Buddhism expounding the Heart Sutra — barely two hundred and sixty characters — in twelve lectures. Originally broadcast by the Tokyo station in 1934, it drew a great response for its plainness and the richness of its quotation, and was later collected into a book that has been read ever since. Its line of argument is clear, taking form is emptiness and emptiness is form as the negative and positive faces of one thing and starting from the principle of dependent origination, and it moves doctrine straight into the language of daily life, quoting freely from old poems, senryū and even Western proverbs.

Summary

If one is asked what the root thought of Buddhism is, it comes down to the single word "emptiness," the author writes in his preface. But that emptiness is an open secret of Buddhism, which everyone seems to understand and no one truly understands. The Heart Sutra explains it from the two directions of "form is emptiness" and "emptiness is form," and negation and affirmation, nothing and being, are dialectically united in it. And one further thing is indispensable: causation. The Heart Sutra says not a word about causation, but without understanding causation one cannot understand emptiness. Not to know the truth of causation is delusion; to see it clearly is enlightenment. ... The text is divided into twelve lectures. The first, "The Wisdom of Truth," asks what prajñā, that is, wisdom, is; the second, "Walking Rather Than Speaking," preaches practice, citing the silence of Vimalakīrti. The third, "Form Is Emptiness," and the fourth, "Eternal Life," speak of the meaning of neither arising nor perishing, laying it over the feeling between parent and child and over death poems; and from the fifth onward come "That Which Goes Through Emptiness," "Waking to Causation," "The Four Right Views," "A Mind Without Attachment," "That Which Has No Fear." Through the tenth, "Prajñā Is the Mother of the Buddha," and the eleventh, "True and Not False," the last lecture, "The Open Secret," treats of the mantra at the end and touches even on its differences from the Sanskrit original. In closing his lectures the author says that the gate into Buddhism is after all "faith," and that faith is a mind that loves and reveres; and he ends by speaking of the meaning of joined palms and of namu.

The opening (original text)

Preface To explain in the simplest terms what the root thought of Buddhism is, is by no means an easy thing; but if it is to be said in a word, I think it comes down to the single word "emptiness." And yet that emptiness is a kind of riddle in Buddhism — an open secret, one may say.

Hannya shingyō kōgi by Takagami Kakushō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

There is a proverb, "Marry in haste and repent at leisure"; but that is not a thing confined to marriage. There is no need for useless haste; and yet the men of old warned us that "in the Buddha's law there is no such thing as tomorrow." If one goes on promising "tomorrow" and "tomorrow," one must die forever without having tasted Buddhism and without having grasped the true meaning and value of life. We must, by following causation and polishing quickly the wisdom of prajñā, come to taste clearly and truly the world of enlightenment.

Hannya shingyō kōgi by Takagami Kakushō (Aozora Bunko)
