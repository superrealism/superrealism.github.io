About this work

One of the tales in Lafcadio Hearn's 1904 collection Kwaidan (in Tanabe Ryūji's Japanese translation). Kwairyō, a priest who was once a samurai, stops at a lone house in the mountains of Kai and sees through the people who live there: at night their heads leave their bodies — they are rokurokubi. Not in the least afraid of the creatures who mean to eat him, he deals with them handily. A Japanese tale of the uncanny retold by Hearn, with an offhand comedy running through the horror.

Summary

About five hundred years ago, Isogai Heidazaemon Taketsura, a retainer of the Kikuchi house in Kyūshū, was a warrior of great valor; but after the fall of the house he left the world, became a priest under the name Kwairyō, and traveled the provinces. One day, benighted in the mountains of Kai, he is taken in by a woodcutter and lodged at a lonely house; and in the night, looking into the next room, he is astonished. The bodies of the sleeping household have no heads on them at all. They are rokurokubi, monsters whose heads fly free. Overhearing the heads discussing how they might eat their guest, Kwairyō is not in the least afraid, and quietly moves the headless bodies to another place. Unable to return to their bodies, the heads writhe in agony and fly at him, but he pulls up a tree and beats them off without difficulty. One head, too slow to escape, fastens its teeth in the sleeve of his robe and cannot be got off. Kwairyō travels on afterward with that head hanging from his sleeve; and a highwayman who tries to frighten him learns the story instead, is filled with awe, and comes to give the head burial.

Characters

  • Kwairyō　Formerly Isogai Heidazaemon, a warrior of the Kikuchi house in Kyūshū. Having left the world to become a priest, he travels the provinces, and is bold enough to face the rokurokubi without fear
  • The rokurokubi　The monsters living in the lonely house in the mountains of Kai, whose heads leave their bodies at night, and who plot to eat Kwairyō
  • The highwayman　Who takes the head fastened to Kwairyō's sleeve, and who, learning the truth, is filled with awe and gives it burial

The opening (original text)

About five hundred years ago there was a man named Isogai Heidazaemon Taketsura, in the service of the lord Kikuchi in Kyūshū. He had inherited from many warlike ancestors a natural aptitude for arms, and extraordinary strength. While yet a boy he surpassed his teachers in swordsmanship, archery and the use of the spear, and showed all the daring and skill of a practiced soldier. Afterward, in the wars of the Eikyō era (1429–1441), he distinguished himself so greatly that high honors were bestowed upon him again and again.

Rokurokubi by Lafcadio Hearn (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In this way the robber came into possession of the head and the robe, and for some time went about as a monstrous priest, robbing travelers. But when he came into the neighborhood of Suwa he heard the true story of the head, and grew afraid of the vengeance of the rokurokubi's ghost. So he resolved to take the head back to the place it came from and bury it with the body. He found his way to the lonely hut in the mountains of Kai, but there was nobody there, and he could not find the body. He therefore buried the head by itself in the wood behind the hut, and had a service performed for the ghost of that rokurokubi. And the mound known as the Rokurokubi's Tomb can be seen to this day — so, at all events, the Japanese author declares.

Rokurokubi by Lafcadio Hearn (Aozora Bunko)
