About this work

"Give back my father, give back my mother" — the collection Tōge Sankichi, a poet exposed to the bomb at Hiroshima, wrote while suffering from radiation sickness himself. The opening poem is cut into the monument in the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park and has become the most widely read poem about the bomb in the world. An unflinching look at the horror, and the will that it should never happen again, tied together in pared-down words.

Summary

"To those whose lives were taken by the atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima on the sixth of August 1945 and on Nagasaki on the ninth" — the opening poem that follows the dedication has become the best known passage in any poem written in Japanese. "give back my father, give back my mother / give back the old people / give back the children / give me back myself, give back / the human beings bound to me / as long as human beings, a world of human beings, exists / peace that will not crumble / give back peace." The poem that follows, "The Sixth of August," sets down without turning its eyes away the thirty thousand people in the streets who vanished in an instant, the fallen bridges, the scorched, crowded streetcars, and the procession of naked bodies that walked in crowds and wept with skin hanging from them like rags. The pieces that follow — "Death," "Flames," "Blindness," "Grave Marker" — reach from the instant of the bombing to the days of surviving in the stench of corpses, to friends falling one by one of radiation sickness, and to the silence in which, under the occupation, it was forbidden to speak of it. In the poems of the later part, such as "A Call" and "The Sixth of August 1950," the protest against an age moving toward war once more is sung head-on. In his afterword Tōge writes that he was ashamed of how poor and weak this collection was, and even so closes: "It is a gift to all who love human beings, and at the same time a book of warning to those same people."

The opening (original text)

— Dedicated to those whose lives were taken by the atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima on the sixth of August 1945 and on Nagasaki on the ninth; and to those who to this day are tormented by the fear of death and by pain; and to those who, as long as they live, have no way of putting out their anguish and their sorrow; and further, to all the people of the whole world who hate the atomic bomb. Preface give back my father, give back my mother give back the old people give back the children give me back myself, give back the human beings bound to me as long as human beings, a world of human beings, exists peace that will not crumble give back peace

Poems of the Atomic Bomb by Tōge Sankichi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And yet this is, from me — no, from us in Hiroshima — to the people of the whole world, to that inborn eye that blinks quietly within every one of them whatever the case may be, the best gift I can make into the gentle hand that must be held out, when one is suddenly moved as a human being, in consideration for oneself and for others. Please receive this heart. This collection is a gift to all who love human beings, and at the same time a book of warning to those same people.

The “Preface” the bombed poet placed at the head of his collection. In a short appeal written entirely in plain hiragana, it demands that everything taken be given back.
