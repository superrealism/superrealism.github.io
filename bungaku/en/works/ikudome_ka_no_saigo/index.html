About this work

Written between 28 December 1952 and the small hours of New Year's Eve, and finished on the day the author took her own life at Rokkō station on the Hankyū line. In the form of a letter to an older acquaintance she calls the aunt from Kumano, it sets down — unrevised and unread over — her love for three men and her guilt, her hatred of family life, and the pain of being unable to write. Kusaka Yōko's last work, of which she said flatly that this is not a novel; all of it is true.

Summary

On the night of the twenty-eighth of December, with a fire in the hearth and a hot-water bottle at her feet, "I" begin to write on cheerless manuscript paper. This will pass into the hands of Mr. Fuji Masaharu, be printed in the coterie magazine, and come at last to your hands — and with that said, the writer, twenty-one years old, pours out the last several months of her life in no fixed order.

In March she took drugs and failed to die, came back to life and got consumption. The married man she loved at that time she calls "the Green Island." The man who came to visit her while she was recovering she calls "By the Railway," and it is he whom she now loves utterly. And the fiancé with whom she exchanged a contract with their thumbprints on it, on condition that neither of them fall in love, she calls "the Pale Colonel." The three of them go round in her heart like a merry-go-round, and each time she spits at her own ugliness.

The staging of a play of her own, a night rehearsal without sleep, misunderstandings, express letters, telegrams. On the day she had decided to die at Kurobe she was given the words "Will you marry me?" and thought that now she could not die. But one step inside the house, with her famous father, her brother who suffers under that reflected light, and her mother who knows nothing, and the heavy stone is laid on her head again. She posts the last letter; no answer comes by ten o'clock, and none comes afterward. "It is over now. Plainly over" — and at two in the morning of the last day of the year the writer lays down her pen.

Characters

  • I　The writer, twenty-one, who goes on writing without the courage to read it over
  • Auntie of Kumano　The person to whom this letter is addressed; the mistress of the room with flowers
  • The Green Island　A married man she loved enough to try to die for in March
  • By the Railway　The man she now loves utterly
  • The Pale Colonel　The fiancé with whom she exchanged a contract
  • Fuji Masaharu　A fellow member of the coterie magazine VIKING, to whom the manuscript is entrusted

The opening (original text)

To Auntie of Kumano. I have been writing to you often for four or five years now. But this is the first time I have written on such cheerless paper, in such fated, flavorless letters. Always it was on paper from Shinshū, or on colored art paper, sometimes in faint brush strokes, sometimes in more amusing and prettier characters — no, that is presumptuous of me. But you were fond of my hand.

Ikudome ka no saigo by Kusaka Yōko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Auntie. I have grown quiet. I have written it out. All of it. What a sinful woman. I am bound for hell, am I not? Auntie, take great care of yourself. The room where flowers are arranged. That dear room. The thirty-first of December, about two in the morning
