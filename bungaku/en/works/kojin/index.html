About this work

The second of Sōseki's late trilogy, serialized in the Asahi Shimbun from December 1912 to November 1913, with a break of about five months when his stomach ulcer worsened. The scholar Nagano Ichirō cannot believe even his wife Onao's heart, and goes so far as to ask his younger brother Jirō to test her fidelity. It carries the loneliness of the modern intellectual — that another person's heart can never be had — to its limit, and arrives at the line about dying, going mad, or entering religion. One of the furthest points Sōseki reached.

Summary

Jirō is to meet a friend in Osaka, and instead it turns into a journey through the Kansai with his mother and his elder brother and sister-in-law. His brother Ichirō is a scholarly, nervous man who suspects that his wife Nao's heart is not with him, and, taking Jirō out from their inn at Wakanoura to the temple Kimiidera, he asks him to put her fidelity to the test. Reluctantly Jirō goes off to Wakayama alone with Nao; a storm forces them to spend a night at an inn, and his brother's suspicions only deepen. Back in Tokyo, Ichirō's torment grows fiercer and the family live as though walking on eggshells. Nao gives no sign of what she is thinking; Ichirō tries absolutely to possess his wife's heart and cannot. Unable to bear it, Jirō leaves the house for lodgings of his own and asks their friend H to take his brother away on a journey. The novel ends with a long letter from H on the road. In it, as a record of a serious and painful soul, is set down the figure of Ichirō, struggling toward some absolute state and driven so far as to think that he must die, or go mad, or enter religion.

Characters

  • Nagano Jirō　The narrator, the younger son, caught between his brother and his brother's wife
  • Nagano Ichirō　Jirō's elder brother. A scholar tormented because he cannot believe even in his wife's heart
  • Nao (O-Nao)　Ichirō's wife. A quiet woman who gives no sign of what she is thinking
  • H　A friend of Ichirō's, who reports on him in a long letter from their journey

The opening (original text)

The moment I got down at Umeda station I hired a rickshaw, as my mother had told me to do, and had them run me to Okada's house. Okada was a man distantly related on my mother's side. I did not know exactly what relation he stood in to her, and remembered him only as a remote connection.

Kōjin by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

When I began this letter your brother was sleeping soundly. Now, as I finish it, he is sleeping soundly still. It strikes me as strange that I should have happened to begin writing while he slept and happened to finish while he slept. Somewhere in me there is a feeling that if he never woke again from this sleep he would surely be happy. And at the same time, somewhere in me, there is a feeling that if he never woke again from this sleep it would surely be very sad.

Kōjin by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

“Death, madness, or else religion. Before me lie only these three.”

Ichirō's confession of anguish, recorded in the long letter of his friend H. A celebrated passage that shows, by laying out three paths, the dead end of an intellectual cornered by the loneliness of the self.
