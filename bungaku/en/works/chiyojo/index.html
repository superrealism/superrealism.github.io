About this work

A short story Dazai published in Kaizō in 1941. Kazuko, a girl praised for her composition as a child, written up in the newspapers and made much of as a prodigy, finds because of that too-early fame that she can no longer write, and becomes an ordinary young woman — told in her own voice, in the woman's-monologue manner. A girl's confession, mixed with self-mockery, carrying a look at talent and at the adults who play with it. The title alludes to the haiku poet Kaga no Chiyojo.

Summary

"Women just aren't any good, after all" — Kazuko, about to turn nineteen, begins to speak, lamenting her own poor head. As a small child, Kazuko had shown an extraordinary talent for composition; her writing was published in the newspaper, and even the famous writer Mr. Iwami sent her words of encouragement, and she became known as a child prodigy. The adults around her competed with one another to sing her praises, expecting still greater masterpieces from her. But, fawned over and grown conscious of other people's eyes, Kazuko came, before she knew it, to find everything she wrote forced and artificial, until she could no longer manage even a single line. Expectation turned into a burden, her talent withered, and Kazuko, in time, turned into an ordinary girl with nothing in particular to recommend her. Through the figure of Kazuko, whose life was thrown off course by too-early fame, Dazai quietly draws out the sin of adults who play with talent, and the trap of self-consciousness.

Characters

  • Kazuko　A girl once celebrated as a child prodigy. About to turn nineteen. The narrator
  • Mr. Iwami　The famous writer who encouraged Kazuko's compositions
  • Uncle and parents　The adults who sang Kazuko's praises and pinned their hopes on her talent

The opening (original text)

Women just aren't any good, after all. It may only be that I, one woman among many, am no good — but I truly do think, deep down, that I myself am no good. And yet, even as I say that, I feel as though there is some stubborn, deep-rooted, dark thing coiled up in a corner of my heart, still relying on itself, still believing there must be some one good thing about me somewhere — and so, in the end, I only understand myself less and less.

Chiyojo by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Mother, I am no Chiyojo. A dim-witted, would-be literary girl who can't write a single thing — I once wrote a story to show my uncle, about how, sitting in the kotatsu reading a magazine, I'd grown sleepy and decided the kotatsu must be a sleeping-box for human beings, and he tossed it aside halfway through. Yesterday I secretly sent a letter to Mr. Iwami. I wrote, please do not abandon the genius girl of seven years ago. I may well go mad, one of these days.

Chiyojo by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)
