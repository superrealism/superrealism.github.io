About this work

An extraordinary experimental work by Dazai, carrying that famous line as its subtitle. Abandoning plot altogether, it strings together fragmentary monologue, aphorism and self-mockery in a torrent from opening chant to closing chant. Written in the middle of his addiction to Pavinal and a crisis of the spirit: a piece like a scream from the young Dazai.

Summary

"Suffering, however lofty, is not thereby noble" — the prologue, "Know the Fierceness of God's Flame," begins with a fable of two hollyhocks that grew up competing with each other across a hedge. Straining upward, higher, higher, they bear only two or three stunted blooms. A "child grown old in form" boasts of having climbed uninvited, straw sandals and all, into God's own garden, plucked flowers there, and even peeked in on God's sleeping face during his afternoon nap. The whole hall bursts into applause at his buffoonery, but he mistakes it for genuine praise, and at last blurts out, "I myself am God." From there, the work has no plot to speak of. Fragments of unfinished novels, letters to friends, half-remembered maxims, headlines from Paris newspapers, his own poems, and passages of self-mockery and evasion appear one after another, held together by nothing but their chapter titles. "The Battalion Commander," "March Thirtieth," "A Flurry of Cherry Blossoms," "Monkey Island" — Dazai lets his own upbringing, his falling-away from the leftist movement, his failed double suicide, his drug addiction, glimpse through the gaps between fragments, while avoiding ever writing of them head-on. The work stands at the very midpoint of the road that runs from "Recollections" and "The Flowers of Buffoonery" to "No Longer Human." Near the end, a parable is told: nearly thirty notebooks of jottings, seized by customs, were locked away in a warehouse, and when at last, ten months later, they could be retrieved, only a single small basket came back. Opening it, out sprang a hundred, a thousand small crabs of thought. Their owner, flustered, chased first one, then another, tore up one line after writing it, and grew steadily sadder, until at last, in the corner of a twilight room, pen clutched in hand, he simply wept.

The opening (original text)

Prologue: Know the Fierceness of God's Flame Suffering, however lofty, is not thereby noble. Again and again — two hollyhocks planted across a hedge from one another, each straining, higher, higher, to outgrow the other, growing spindly and thin, bearing only two or three stunted blooms, their faded, wrinkled petals sadly forgetting the crimson splendor they once boasted.

Nijusseiki kishu by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And so, before the eyes of you all, out sprang — oh, how unlooked-for — a hundred, a thousand small crabs of thought; their owner, flustered, chased first one, then another, tore up one line after writing it, tore up one word half-written, and grew steadily sadder, until at last, in the corner of a twilight room, pen clutched in hand, he simply wept.
