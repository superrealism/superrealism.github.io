About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in King in 1954. Munaki Takaya, told by his doctor that his illness cannot be cured. He accepts death quietly, entrusts the person dearest to him to a friend, releases the bear cub he had been keeping into the mountains, and leaves the world. A piece drawing feelingly the clean end of one man, against the pines of Matsuyama under the moon.

Summary

The doctor Kagaoka Dōen, his face gloomy, wipes his hands with great care and lights a pipe. To Sōjō Takaya's question, "So it's as I thought, is it," Dōen watches the smoke for a while before opening his mouth heavily. "There's no mistake. I'm sorry for you, but there's no mistake now." It was a sentence of incurable illness. But Takaya, without any show of distress, quietly accepts his own death. The story traces, feelingly, against a backdrop of the pines of Matsuyama under the moon, how Takaya, in his limited remaining time, puts his affairs in order and entrusts his thoughts to the people dear to him. At the close of the story, Takaya entrusts what is to come after him to his friend Taijirō. "Take care of that person for me." "I let the bear cub go, myself." Having released into the mountains the bear cub he had been keeping, and having entrusted what follows, Takaya's breathing stops. Taijirō, still holding Takaya's hand, breaks into violent weeping. The mist had all but cleared. A piece of Yamamoto's, drawing one man's clean, unflinching end with a quiet resonance.

The opening (original text)

Sōjō Takaya, putting on his tabi, glanced at the doctor as if to urge him on. Kagaoka Dōen, his face gloomy, wiped his fingers with great, careful attention, then drew the tobacco tray toward him and took a smoke. "So it's as I thought, is it," Takaya asked. Dōen, as though he had not heard, watched steadily the smoke rising from his pipe. "There's no mistake," Dōen said. "I'm sorry for you, but there's no mistake now."

The ending (original text)

"Take care of that person for me," Takaya said. "— I let the bear cub go, myself." Taijirō took hold of Takaya's hand. Takaya's breathing stopped. Taijirō, still holding Takaya's hand, broke into violent weeping. The mist had all but cleared.
