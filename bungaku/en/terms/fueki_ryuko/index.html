What is Fueki Ryūkō (The Unchanging and the Changing)?

Category
Other

A principle of haikai attributed to Matsuo Bashō, holding that what never changes across the ages (fueki) and what shifts with each moment (ryūkō) both spring from a single root. He is said to have taught it to his disciples in the latter part of the Genroku era, after completing the journey recorded in "Oku no Hosomichi" ("The Narrow Road to the Deep North"), and it was recorded for posterity by Mukai Kyorai in "Kyorai-shō" and by Hattori Tohō in "Sanzōshi" (Bashō himself never set down this four-character phrase in his own writing). "Sanzōshi" states: "In our master's poetics there is the unchanging of ten thousand generations, and there is the change of the moment. These two things come down to one thing, and that one thing is the sincerity of poetry." At the core of this principle lies the paradox that continually changing in the pursuit of novelty and thoroughly committing to the unchanging essence of poetry are not in conflict — that to truly master change is naturally to arrive at the unchanging. As a Bashō-school principle it is spoken of together with "sabi," the sense of a clear, quiet charm found in stillness, and "karumi," a light, unforced way of capturing the everyday as it is. It remains widely used today, beyond the bounds of haikai, as a phrase for speaking of the relationship between tradition and innovation.
