About this work

On a cold Sunday after a quarrel with his wife, he notices a young woman standing at the crossroads in front of the tobacconist's — a piece Yamakawa Masao wrote for "Close Friends," his series in Hitchcock Magazine. Two hours, six hours, twelve hours. He cannot leave alone the unhappiness of a beautiful woman waiting for someone on a midwinter street. A short story that quietly presses on us the absurdity and the pain of reaching toward another person's loneliness.

Summary

On a Sunday of all days he had a small quarrel with his wife, and she dressed and went out. Waking near noon, he telephoned a noodle shop from the red public telephone at the tobacconist's, and there saw a woman standing with her hand on the wire fence. A young woman in a dark brown coat, with beautiful legs. The tobacconist's wife lowers her voice: "She has been standing there like that for two hours already." ... From the lavatory window of his lodging he could look straight down on that crossroads. An hour later the woman was still there. She draws a line on the road with her toe, yawns hugely, walks in small circles. It was a chilly, clouded day. Three o'clock passed and half past four came, and the woman did not move. Again and again he went out to the street and started toward her, and came back without a word, with nothing but more boxes of Ikoi cigarettes. He did not want to admit it — that this young and beautiful woman was a pitiful creature stood up by a lover and left waiting at a winter crossroads. In the dusk he made up his mind and ran to her, and what he saw was a woman crouching on the road, bouncing a hard top-ball and singing in a low voice. "Please give it back." "...Are you waiting for someone?" "He is coming now." Snatching the ball back, her posture plainly refused and ignored him. In a shame that burned him he ran home to his lodging. Past nine the woman was still in the same place. — At eleven his wife came home. There had been no one in front of the tobacconist's, she says. When he asks whether she went to her parents' house, his wife answers: today she stood all day, alone, in a park at the edge of Tokyo.

The opening (original text)

It was a cold day. That morning he had a small quarrel with his wife. It was a Sunday of all days, and he wanted to sleep late; but his wife nagged at him about his wages, threw a pillow at him, and in the end dressed quickly to go out and left the room. With his head buried in the quilt he thought: there she goes again. She will not be back for half the day. Lately his wife often uses this move. No doubt she will go to her parents' and run me down to her heart's content. But if that satisfies her, well and good.

The ending (original text)

Without a word he put out his hand to his wife's knee. His wife came into his arms of herself. Her cheek was very cold, and yet at once, impatiently, her breathing came short. Suddenly he felt his love for her, and the memory like an open wound of the day three years ago when they first kissed, pierce his breast sharply. It was a far-off, distant ache, and yet it was the same ache he had been feeling all that day. That was what he remembered. The roar of a motorcar ran past along the road. For an instant the figure of the woman with the beautiful legs in the dark brown coat flashed before his eyes. It came to him that the young woman might have been a phantom born of his own heart.... But shaking his head as though to drive it off, he put more strength into the arms that held his wife's body, which still smelled of the cold winter air outside.
