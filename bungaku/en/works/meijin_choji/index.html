About this work

Chōjirō, a cabinetmaker said to have no equal in Edo. At a hot-spring inn at Yugawara he learns that he was a baby thrown away in a bamboo thicket by the parents who bore him, and that the parents who raised him were the benefactors who picked him up dying and saved his life. Between rage at his real parents and obligation to those who raised him, Chōji's fortunes roll toward an unlooked-for killing and a famous judgment. An unusual late work Enchō wrote in the colloquial style, suggested by a short story of Maupassant, drawing a craftsman's temper, human feeling, and a merciful judgment.

Summary

In the Kyōwa era, Chōjirō, apprenticed at ten to the master joiner Seibei — known as "Hako-sei" — becomes, by the age of twenty-eight, a craftsman said to surpass even his own teacher. He is a stubborn, uncompromising artisan, one who bows to neither money nor fame, and holds it a point of honor to put his very soul into work made with a slow, almost clumsy care. One day, while taking the hot-spring waters at Yugawara in Sagami to heal an injury from a fall out of a rickshaw, Chōji — as he is called — is told an unexpected truth about his own origins. The scar on his back, he learns, was left when his birth parents abandoned him as an infant in a bamboo grove, and the very people he had believed to be his birth parents were in truth the benefactors who had found the half-dead baby and saved his life. In time Chōji learns that his true father, Han'emon, was poisoned to death through a plot laid by his true mother, O-Ryū, together with the acupuncturist Gensai. Torn between his debt of gratitude to his adoptive parents and outrage at the wickedness of his birth parents, Chōji resolves, even at the cost of his own life, to lodge a direct appeal for justice, and avenges his father's death. Though the case seems certain to be judged as the unforgivable crime of matricide, since Chōji has laid hands on his own birth mother, the scholar-official Hayashi Daigaku-no-kami, drawing on a passage from the Book of Rites, rules instead that this was vengeance for a woman who had abandoned her child and murdered her husband, and therefore no matricide at all. Acquitted, and granted a reward, Chōjirō unexpectedly comes to inherit the great fortune of the Kamekōya house, takes the maid Shimaji as his wife, and lives out his days in comfort. Enchō's crowning late work, in which a Western tale is skillfully transplanted to Edo, to portray, in full, the stubborn pride and deep compassion of a true master craftsman.

Characters

  • Chōjirō / Chōji　A joiner celebrated as the finest master craftsman in Edo. A stubborn artisan who bows to neither money nor fame. In truth, he had been an abandoned child.
  • Seibei　The master joiner known as "Hako-sei." Chōji's teacher and foster father.
  • Han'emon　Chōji's true father. Poisoned to death through a plot by his wife and an acupuncturist.
  • O-Ryū　Chōji's true mother. Conspires with the acupuncturist Gensai to murder her husband Han'emon.
  • Gensai　An acupuncturist. Lured by money, he joins O-Ryū in murdering Han'emon.
  • Shimaji　A maid in the service of Lord Tsutsui. Later becomes Chōjirō's wife.
  • Hayashi Daigaku-no-kami　The official who judges Chōji's case to be "vengeance for his true father," and not matricide.

The opening (original text)

This is the life story of a joiner named Chōjirō, who became apprentice to the joiner Seibei in the second year of Kyōwa, at the age of ten, and who, from around the age of twenty-eight, in the early years of Bunsei, came to be called a master of his craft. Among those who are called skilled, in what is nowadays termed art — painting, calligraphy, carving, lacquer-work, and the like — there have always been a great many, since ancient times; but a true master is a rare thing indeed. A person of somewhat more than ordinary skill may, with application, become merely skilled; but mere application alone will never carry one to the place where a master stands. Unless one comes, of oneself, to grasp that natural, effortless excellence, one is no master.

Meijin chōji by San'yūtei Enchō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Now, Chōjirō lodged his direct appeal for justice fully prepared to pay for it with his life; but since Heaven itself bore witness that there was not so much as a hair's breadth of wickedness in his heart, through the investigation of Lord Tsutsui, the plea for mercy from Seibei, and the understanding shown by Lord Hayashi Daigaku-no-kami, the matter was in the end judged to be vengeance for his true father, and Chōjirō received a reward besides. He came, quite unexpectedly, to inherit the great fortune of the Kamekōya house, and was appointed by the authorities as purveyor of joinery to the shogunate. Chōjirō then took back his childhood name, Hannosuke, and, to console the spirits of both his true father Han'emon, who had died so cruel a death, and — owing to the bond of having been carried in her womb, wicked though she was — O-Ryū as well, he raised a memorial tower at Mount Kōya in Kishū, and, at the graveyard of Mukōyama in Yugawara, Ashigara District, Sagami Province, he raised gravestones for his foster parents too, and gave them every reverent care. Even without all this, Chōjirō's name had already rung out through all Edo some years before, over the altar bearing Lord Hayashi's own seal of approval; and now, with this rare case in which Lord Hayashi, by his lecture on the Book of Rites, had put his seal upon the word "vengeance," Chōjirō's name grew still more famous. Seibei and the others rejoiced, needless to say, and Sakakuraya Sukeshichi too was overjoyed; and one day, calling at Lord Tsutsui's mansion, he was told by his lordship that, in light of what the maid Shimaji had said some days before, and since Chōjirō was no longer a mere craftsman, it might be well to send Shimaji to him. Sukeshichi, only too glad to oblige, at once arranged the match and proposed the marriage to the house of Kamekōya, and Chōjirō, for his part, gladly consented; and so, on the first day of the third month of the fifth year of Bunsei, the year of the Horse, the wedding was celebrated, and the couple lived on together in harmony and prosperity. As no child was born to them, they took in an adopted son, and Chōjirō — Hannosuke — retired to Negishi, where he died on the second day of the ninth month of the second year of Kōka, at the age of fifty-three. His grave remains today at Tenryūji in Yanaka, under the posthumous name Kōtoku-in Chōyō Gishū Koji.

Meijin chōji by San'yūtei Enchō (Aozora Bunko)
