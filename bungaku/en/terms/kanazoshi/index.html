What is Kanazōshi?

Category
Form / Genre

A general term for reading matter written in kana or kana-mixed prose in the early Edo period, roughly from the first half to the middle of the seventeenth century. It is a transitional literature bridging medieval tale literature and ukiyo-zōshi, the full-fledged early-modern fiction that began with Ihara Saikaku; the term "kanazōshi" itself was coined by the scholar Mizutani Futō in the 1900s. Most of its authors were educated men such as priests and Confucian scholars, and its content ranged from didactic works preaching Buddhist and Confucian teachings, to entertaining collections of comic tales and stories of the strange, to practical guidebooks to famous places and reviews; well-known examples include Jorai-shi's "Kashōki," Anrakuan Sakuden's "Seisuishō" ("Laughter to Wake You from Sleep"), and Asai Ryōi's "Otogibōko" and "Tōkaidō Meishoki." The comic-tale collection "Seisuishō" in particular is regarded as the root of rakugo, and the tale of the uncanny "Otogibōko," adapted from Chinese fiction, opened the way for the ghost stories that followed. As a product of the period when commercial printing and publishing were first taking hold, it was sold as illustrated woodblock-printed books, which also distinguishes it from the medieval tales that had been copied by hand and passed down. Its significance in literary history lies in how, as warfare receded and publishing took root, it broadened its readers from samurai and priests to townspeople, and this extension leads directly on to the flourishing of ukiyo-zōshi.
