About this work

A short story drawing faith and worldly desire through two acolytes raised on Mount Hiei, published in Chūō Kōron in April 1918. Raised on a holy mountain forbidden to women, and taught that women are devils like she-demons, the boys still cannot suppress their curiosity about the beautiful illusion. One goes down from the mountain and one remains. An unusual work by Tanizaki, drawing where each of them ends in a quiet manner suggesting the court tales.

Summary

Senju-maru, fifteen, and Rurikō-maru, thirteen, had been entrusted to Mount Hiei — forbidden to women — before they could remember, and raised at the knee of a venerable holy man. What, then, were these "women" the sutras described as demons within, however bodhisattva-like without? Talking it over with a child's curiosity, the two boys grew absorbed in the question, and it was Senju-maru, the elder, who fell into the deeper brooding. One spring dawn he announced that he would go down the mountain for half a day to see for himself what this earthly desire truly was, and he never came back. Six months later a messenger arrived for Rurikō-maru bearing a long letter from Senju-maru extolling the beauty of the fleeting world. Rurikō-maru spent a night thinking it through, cut off his doubts, and chose to remain on the mountain — yet before long the first light of desire began to rise in his own breast as well, and on the holy man's instruction he shut himself in the Lotus Hall in retreat. On the night his vow was fulfilled, a noble old man appeared to him in a dream and began to tell of the karmic bonds of his past life.

Characters

  • Senju-maru　A fifteen-year-old acolyte who goes down the mountain to learn the truth of women
  • Rurikō-maru　A thirteen-year-old acolyte who looks up to Senju-maru as an elder brother and remains on the mountain to continue his training
  • The holy man　The virtuous priest who raised the two boys, and who teaches that the world beyond the mountain is a world of five defilements
  • Senju-maru's messenger　A servant-like man who comes from the village of Fukakusa to summon Rurikō-maru

The opening (original text)

The two acolytes were two years apart, thirteen and fifteen. The elder was called Senju-maru, the younger Rurikō-maru. Both alike had been entrusted, while still too young to know their own minds, to Mount Hiei — forbidden to women — and raised at the knee of a venerable holy man. Senju-maru, it was said, had been born into the house of a wealthy man of Ōmi province, but for reasons of his own was brought to this mountain lodging at the age of four. Rurikō-maru, though the young son of a certain Minor Counsellor, had likewise, for some particular reason, been given up to this sacred ground that guarded the capital, at the age of three, just as he was being weaned from his nurse's milk, his family forsaking the city for his sake.

Futari no chigo by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

It was just when he seemed at last to have reached the summit. In the whirling, ceaselessly falling snow, whiter still than the snow itself, as though a very spirit of it had taken shape, lay a nameless bird, wounded pitifully beneath one wing, scattering drops of blood like crimson petals as it rolled upon the ground, gasping and writhing in pain. The moment it caught his eye, Rurikō ran to it at once and gathered it into both arms, tight against him, as a parent bird shelters its chick. Then, from the depths of a storm too fierce for any voice to carry, he chanted the name of Amida again and again, and hung about its neck the crystal rosary he carried in his hand.
