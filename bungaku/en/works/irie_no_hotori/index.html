About this work

A novella Masamune Hakuchō published in Taiyō in 1915, and one of the central works of naturalism. In an old house facing an inlet on the Inland Sea, it draws the bleak days of the brothers and sisters who live there as though settling to the bottom. Tatsuo, the second brother, teaches at a school but buries himself in English on his own and barely speaks to the family; the sister Katsuyo, short-sighted, has missed her chance to marry and keeps only the dream of going to Tokyo; the younger brothers long for the city or for travel. Each carries an unsatisfied wish and grows older in a country life that never changes. The very essence of Hakuchō, looking coldly at the emptiness and tedium of a life.

Summary

In an old house looking out on an inlet of the Inland Sea, a large family lives around its aged parents. The eldest son, Eiichi, is a schoolmaster at Nara, and sends picture postcards on which he writes of the loneliness of travel. The younger brother Ryōkichi, home for the winter holidays, talks proudly of his student life in Tokyo and tells them how strange the electric lights and the illuminations are. The elder sister Katsuyo, in iron-rimmed spectacles for her short sight, has let the time for marriage go by, and says over and over that her one wish is to go up to Tokyo and live in a dormitory. "I am ready to die for it," she says with an air of resolve; but to one who cannot set a foot outside the village it is a wish that can never be met. Among such a family the second brother, Tatsuo, is the more marked in his solitude. Teaching at the elementary school, he sits at his desk until late at night studying English by himself, and has passed four years and five years in this way. His pronunciation and his way of construing are of his own making and are unlikely to pass anywhere in the world, and the family reproves him — to work at a language in the country is labor wasted — but he lets it go past like wind at a horse's ear and does not even answer. He became like this after they refused to let him learn the violin and become a musician; no, it is because there is something wrong with his body — so the family shrug him off, and in time they say, "Let us give him a bit of land enough to keep him in gruel and leave him alone," and nobody makes a question of him any more. He is not stirred by talk of going to Tokyo or of traveling, but only turns his dictionary in silence, and takes his one comfort of the day in warming himself at the foot-warmer. Watching the fishing fires shine on the inlet, he remembers a boyhood in which he was livelier, as though it were a former life. Everyone grows old to no purpose in the changeless life of the country, carrying an unsatisfied heart — and Hakuchō draws that unbearable state of living plainly, with a detached eye.

The opening (original text)

The picture postcard the eldest son Eiichi had sent from Nara passed from hand to hand among his three brothers and two sisters. But apart from Katsuyo there was no one who looked at it with any interest. "Wherever one goes it is withered fields and dreary. I mean to spend two or three days in Osaka and come home about the tenth" — and Katsuyo, in her iron-rimmed spectacles for her short sight, stared hard at the characters carelessly written with a brush and made them out one by one.
