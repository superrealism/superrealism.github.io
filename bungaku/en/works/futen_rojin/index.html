About this work

A long novel of Tanizaki's last years, serialized in Chūō Kōron from 1961. Utsugi Tokusuke, seventy-seven, carrying the aftereffects of a stroke and the pains of illness, keeps an obsessive daily record in a diary written in katakana. Played with by the free charm of Satsuko, his son's wife, who lives in the house, the old man sinks into the fantasy of having her feet copied as a Buddha's footprint and cut into his own gravestone. Held to be Tanizaki's last masterpiece, drawing through with grotesque humour the attachment to sex of a person with age and death before him.

Summary

Utsugi Tokusuke, seventy-seven, his hand crippled by the after-effects of a stroke, lives in fear of a blood-pressure attack even as he rules the household as a sharp-tongued, cantankerous old man. His one reason for living is Sasako, the wife of his son Jōkichi, who lives with him. Sasako, once a dancer, sees through the old man's heart and handles him shrewdly — allowing him a kiss in the bathroom in exchange for an expensive cat's-eye stone, and the like. Though the old man dimly senses something between Sasako and the young Haruhisa, he finds his own jealousy a kind of perverse stimulation to be savored. In time he becomes possessed by a plan to establish his own grave in Kyoto, and grows absorbed in a fantasy: he will take a rubbing of the sole of Sasako's foot, have it carved into his gravestone in the likeness of the Buddha's footprint, and lie eternally beneath that foot after death. Excited by taking the rubbing, the old man suffers an attack and collapses, and the diary breaks off. At the end are placed nursing records and his daughter's notes, which record, in flat, matter-of-fact terms, his later days — the old man, somewhat recovered, gazing out at the construction of the garden pool and losing himself in fancy.

Characters

  • Utsugi Tokusuke　The seventy-seven-year-old protagonist, who records his obsession with Sasako in his diary even as he suffers from illness
  • Sasako　The wife of his son Jōkichi. Toys with the old man using her free-spirited beauty as a former dancer
  • Jōkichi　The old man's son. Appears to tacitly overlook the precarious relationship between his wife and his father
  • The old woman　The old man's wife. Has long suffered under her husband's obsessive nature
  • Nurse Sasaki　The live-in nurse who attends to the old man's daily needs

The opening (original text)

The 16th. ...In the evening, went to see the evening program at the Shinjuku Daiichi Theater. The bill was 'Beyond Love and Hate,' 'The Tale of Hikoichi,' and 'Sukeroku Kuruwa no Momoyogusa,' but I saw none of the others — Sukeroku alone was my purpose. Kanya's Sukeroku leaves something to be desired, but I heard Totoshō was to play Agemaki, and wondering just how beautiful that would be, found myself drawn more to Agemaki than to Sukeroku himself. Went with the old woman and Sasako. Jōkichi rushed straight there from the office as well.

Diary of a Mad Old Man by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

From mid-April on, on fine days, he began taking a walk of twenty or thirty minutes in the garden. For the most part a nurse accompanied him, though on rare occasions Sasako would take his hand and lead him. By then, work had already begun on the pool he had once promised to have built for her, and the garden lawn was being dug up. "There's no point building it," Sasako said. "Once summer comes, Grandpa won't be able to go outdoors in the daytime anyway. It's a waste of money — better to stop it." To which Jōkichi replied, "Just watching the work on the pool get started, the way he was promised — that alone sets all sorts of fancies stirring in the old man's head. And the children are looking forward to it too."
