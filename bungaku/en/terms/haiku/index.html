What is Haiku?

Category
Form / Genre

The world's shortest fixed verse form, based on seventeen syllables in a 5-7-5 pattern and using a seasonal word (kigo) and a cutting word (kireji). It originally referred to the "hokku," the opening verse of haikai (haikai no renga), in which multiple poets alternated long and short verses in succession, coming to be appreciated as an independent poem on its own. In the Edo period Matsuo Bashō gave the hokku profound poetic depth, and Yosa Buson and Kobayashi Issa opened distinctively individual worlds, but from the late Edo period an age set in in which sect masters mass-produced formulaic verses. It was Masaoka Shiki who transformed this: in "Dassai Shooku Haiwa," serialized in the newspaper "Nippon" from 1892, he fiercely criticized the old school as "tsukinami" (formulaic), and, taking "shasei" (sketching from life), learned from Western painting, as his method, he established the term "haiku," as against "haikai," as a single poetic style. After Shiki's death, Takahama Kyoshi took the lead in the haiku world through the magazine "Hototogisu," championing objective sketching and the poetic celebration of birds and flowers, while Kawahigashi Hekigotō's "new tendency" haiku movement gave rise to free-verse haiku that abandoned both fixed form and seasonal words, and Taneda Santōka and Ozaki Hōsai opened their own distinctive terrain there.
