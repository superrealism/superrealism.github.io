About this work

The Ken'yūsha, which swept the literary world of Meiji under Ozaki Kōyō. Uchida Roan, who ran alongside it at the time, draws from the inside a long reminiscence of the literary world: from the society's birthplace at Nakazaka in Iidamachi, through its rise on Garakuta Bunko and Shincho Hyakushu, the company of talents around it, and the political skill with which Kōyō led his party, to the height of its power and its decline.

Summary

A long recollection in which Uchida Roan, with the eye of a contemporary onlooker, draws the rise and fall of Ozaki Kōyō and the Ken'yūsha, which built up a great power in the literary world of Meiji. Beginning from Nakazaka in Iidamachi, where Bakin's old house stood, as the birthplace of the Ken'yūsha, he follows the course by which Ishibashi Shian, Yamada Bimyō and Ozaki Kōyō founded the circulating magazine Garakuta Bunko and, banding together as a party, went out into the literary world. He sets Bimyō, who ran ahead for the credit of the first stroke and was isolated, against Kōyō, who led a great body of supporters and spread his power as a group; and he weaves in the amateur theatricals at the Kōkakurō, the way Kōyō's Iro zange took Bimyō's place in Shincho hyakushu and brought Rohan's Fūryūbutsu before the world, and portraits of such men as the ill-fated writer Kitamura San'a, the clever Iwaya Sazanami, and the handsome Kawakami Bizan and his unhappy end. Calling the Ken'yūsha "the Seiyūkai of the literary world" and praising Kōyō's power of command as greater than Hara Kei's, he traces the rise of the Red Gate and the Waseda groups and the decline that followed Kōyō's death. He conveys the complicated character of a Kōyō who declared himself ignorant of foreign literature and yet read Zola with delight, and the last anecdote of his sending for a costly great dictionary on the point of death; and he closes: "He was truly of the stuff of a great writer, and by no means merely a clever man."

Characters

  • Ozaki Kōyō　The head of the Ken'yūsha, the author of Iro zange and Konjiki yasha, who led his party and swept the literary world of Meiji
  • Ishibashi Shian　Shian Gaishi of Nakazaka. One of the founders of the Ken'yūsha, who wrote the opening words of the Garakuta Bunko
  • Yamada Bimyō　One of the founders of the Ken'yūsha, who ran ahead early and was isolated
  • Iwaya Sazanami　Sazanami Sanjin. A clever man of the Ken'yūsha, who later became a master of fairy tales
  • Kawakami Bizan　The handsomest writer of the literary world, who came to an unhappy end
  • Kitamura San'a　An ill-fated writer, who left the one work Ishikura Shingozaemon
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who ran alongside the age from outside the Ken'yūsha

The opening (original text)

Among the historic remains of Tokyo destroyed in the earthquake, one of those most to be regretted is the well of the water for Bakin's inkstone. Bakin's old dwelling has been repaired so many times that it had almost lost its old look, and its falling down is no great loss; but the well by the kitchen door was, they say, just as it was in Bakin's lifetime, and to hear that it has been filled in is a matter for regret. Still, being a well, even if it is filled with tiles and earth it is easy enough to restore. This well of the water for Bakin's inkstone is at the back of an alley halfway up Nakazaka in Iidamachi, across from the Yotsugi Inari. Nakazaka is not much known except to the people of the neighborhood, but it is the steep slope next after Kudan. Strangely, this Nakazaka is not only a site connected with the great writer Bakin but also the birthplace of the Ken'yūsha, which made an epoch in the literary history of Meiji.

The ending (original text)

To speak plainly, I did not admire Kōyō's works as much as the world made of them. There was much in his life and his manner and his person with which I was not satisfied. That I constantly repeated my hard words, less for the sake of literature than for Kōyō's own sake, was after all because I grudged that so uncommon a talent should not be brought to perfection; and for that Kōyō bore me a grudge and we fell out against my will. But at his very last extremity his eagerness to raise his knowledge did not flag in the least, and I could not but bow to it. Kōyō was truly of the stuff of a great writer, and by no means merely a clever man.
