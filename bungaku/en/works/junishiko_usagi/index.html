About this work

One of the pieces in Minakata Kumagusu's central work Studies of the Twelve Branches: the folklore and legends of the hare, for the year of the hare, published in the magazine Taiyō in 1915. He begins by arguing the distinction between hare and rabbit from the names in various languages, and examines the errors that books East and West have handed down about them. He shows that tales resembling the white hare of Inaba are distributed in South Asia and Europe too, and so draws how the imagination around a single animal has been shared across borders.

Summary

Kumagusu begins by stating the distinction he makes in this essay between what he writes as "hare" and what he writes as "rabbit." The former answers to the English hare, the German Hase, the Latin lepus, the Sanskrit śaśaka; the latter to the English rabbit, the French lapin, the German Kaninchen. That a lullaby in Wakayama runs, "the hare of Mount Kachi-Kachi has long ears because it eats bamboo grass," can hardly have been founded on the Sanskrit śaśaka — and from such associations his pen runs freely on. He then examines what the books of East and West have handed down about the hare: that the Bencao gangmu says the hare has no spleen; the odd doctrine that it has nine holes in its rump; the Chinese belief among pregnant women that eating hare will bring forth a harelipped child. Kumagusu brings in even what he has heard himself from hunters of hares, and guesses at where these popular notions arose. The talk widens further to the hare in the moon of the Buddhist scriptures, the race between the tortoise and the garuda, and the German tale of the dwarf tailor who tricks a giant; and at the end, as a thing resembling the story of the tortoise who tricked the beasts, he cites the white hare of Inaba in the Kojiki, closing with the promise to treat its parallels and its origin in another paper.

The opening (original text)

Before writing this essay I must state the distinction between what is written simply as "hare" and what is written as "rabbit." What is written here as hare is what gives the English hare, the German Hase, the Latin lepus, the Spanish liebre, the French lièvre and the rest; the Arabic al-arnab, the Turkish tavşan, the Sanskrit śaśaka; and by the German Möllendorff's account it is called in the region of Peking mountain hare, field hare, or field cat.

Jūnishikō (usagi) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Resembling the story told above of the tortoise that tricked the beasts, there is in our own country the tale in the Kojiki of the white hare of Inaba, which tricked the crocodiles and crossed the sea. Its parallels and its origin I mean to treat in the number of Nihon oyobi Nihonjin of the fifteenth of the first month or the first of the second.

Jūnishikō (usagi) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
