About this work

The year after the defeat, a beautiful white-haired old man becomes the subject of talk at an upland summer resort. Hisao Jūran's central short story proceeds as the narrator's written account of who he is. A young man who has studied characterology is taken into the family of a businessman and pressed to kill Hamlet. Transposing Shakespeare's plot into contemporary Japan, it draws the border between madness and sanity, guilt and forgiveness, in a finely worked style.

Summary

In this first summer after the defeat, at a summer resort three thousand seven hundred feet up, there was an old man often spoken of on hotel verandas and by the fireside of villas on misty nights. He was a man of about sixty with beautiful shining white hair, thin and clean as a crane, with a solemn face that suggested Goethe or Liszt in old age; and everyone could not help staring and wondering what sort of high spiritual life he had led — and from there the story goes back to the history of this old man, Sofue. ... "I," who have made a special study of psychopathology and the science of character, come to frequent the house of the businessman Sakai. When I speak of Allport's method of studying personality, Sakai shows a strong interest, and in time even a match with his daughter Ayuko is proposed. But what Sakai really wanted was something else. There certainly are people of the sort who would be happier dead than alive, he says; the man himself may be thinking he no longer wishes to live; have you no mind to help him? — and so saying, Sakai presses me: "Kill Hamlet." It is not that no one but you could kill him, he says; I want you too to have a weakness like mine; I want you to get yourself muddied for once. Telling me to think it over until tomorrow, Sakai left the study. At the end of the story, when his listener says, "There is a good deal of religion in this tale of yours," Sofue smiles and nods, and quotes the verse of the great fish that swallowed Jonah. Only, whether it was fortunate or unfortunate for Hamlet that hell threw him back, he says, he still cannot tell.

Characters

  • Sofue　The white-haired old man who draws every eye at the summer resort; the writer of the memoir
  • Sakai　The businessman who presses him to "kill Hamlet"
  • Kotoko　Sakai's wife
  • Ayuko　Sakai's daughter

The opening (original text)

In this first summer after the defeat, at a summer resort three thousand seven hundred feet up, there was an old man often spoken of on hotel verandas and by the fireside of villas on misty nights. He was an old man of about sixty with beautiful shining white hair, thin and clean as a crane, with a solemn face belonging to the phenotype of Goethe or Liszt or Paderewski in old age; and since so deep an expression with such spirituality in it appears very rarely in a Japanese face, one could not help staring and wondering what sort of high spiritual life he had led.

The ending (original text)

"...And the verse that says, 'Now the Lord had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah,' is a beautiful one. Providence, I too have lately come to believe, is made without a gap, like the works of a machine. Only, whether hell's having thrown Hamlet back was fortunate for Hamlet or unfortunate, I still cannot tell."
