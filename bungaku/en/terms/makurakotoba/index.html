What is Makurakotoba (Pillow Words)?

Category
Style & Technique

A fixed phrase placed before a particular word in waka to lead into it. As a rule five syllables long, it has the distinctive feature that the word it leads into is nearly fixed — "ashihiki no" leads into "mountain," "hisakata no" into light or the heavens, "tarachine no" into "mother," "nubatama no" into black or night, "aoniyoshi" into Nara, "akanesasu" into the sun or the color purple. In most cases it need not be translated into modern language; its function is to give a rhythm to the line while lending the word it precedes an ancient association and a certain dignity. It is used with particular richness in the "Man'yōshū," and for not a few pillow words, their origin and meaning had already become obscure by the time of the Nara period. It differs from the jokotoba, another form of prefatory phrase, in that a jokotoba runs to seven syllables or more and its word of destination is not fixed, a fresh creation for that single poem, whereas the makurakotoba is short and handed down by convention across generations. In later ages it came to be used more formulaically, and it nearly vanished from modern tanka, but it remains, better than anything else, a demonstration of how Japanese verse is built up carrying the memory of ancient words within it.
