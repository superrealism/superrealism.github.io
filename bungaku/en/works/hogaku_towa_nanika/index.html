About this work

"April is the month in which, every year, a great many young people newly set their minds on law and enter its gate" — a late piece in which a leading authority on civil law and the sociology of law described, for beginners, what kind of study law is. Starting from his own struggles with trigonometry at middle school, he states frankly that most students of a law faculty graduate without knowing what they were studying for. His argument that interpretation is the creation of law, and his closing sketch of the relations among legal philosophy, the sociology of law and practical jurisprudence, are still read as a classic introduction.

Summary

To know at the outset the outline of the study one is about to take up raises the efficiency of learning — so the author begins, and cites his own experience: when he was taught trigonometry in his fourth year of middle school, because his teacher tried to drive the contents of the textbook into him without telling him the purpose of the study, it seemed to him terribly difficult. Unlike those who enter the science faculty or the faculty of letters, the student who enters the faculty of law usually has not even a layman's knowledge of the study he is about to take up. Even the author, whose father was for many years a judicial officer, found on entering that they seemed to be teaching a study rather different from what he had expected, and for some while he was in a fog. Most students, until they graduate, cannot grasp what they are studying law for, and in fact labor in ordinary times over making notes and at examination time over learning those notes by heart. ... After this preamble the author goes on to explain what kind of study law is. And at the end he sketches his own view. What forms the center of legal study is "practical law," which divides broadly into the study of interpretation and the study of legislation; but the essence common to both, as a science, is the study of legal policy. The study of interpretation is not a technique of interpreting statutes formally; its aim is to create the law proper to each concrete case. Even where it looks on the surface like a formal interpretation, the creation of law is in fact taking place. Further, practical law is given on the one hand its ideals and its indices for policy by the philosophy of law, and on the other is taught the means of realizing policy by the social laws that the sociology of law discovers. It resembles, says the author, the relation of engineering to natural science, and of clinical to basic medicine.

The opening (original text)

1. Introductory 1. April is the month in which, every year, a great many young men set their minds on the study of law and enter its gate. The purpose of this piece is to give these young men some preliminary knowledge of what sort of nature the study of law has as a study.

Hōgaku toha nanika by Suehiro Izutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I believe that when this science has developed to the point of providing practical law with the social laws it needs, as fully as the natural sciences do, the study of law will develop into a study truly worthy of the name of science, and the practice of law, in legislation and in judgment, will surely become far more rational and far less wasteful and forced.
