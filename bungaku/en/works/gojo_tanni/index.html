About this work

A short story published in the collection Nantōtan in 1942, subtitled as the notes of the monk Wujing. One of the two pieces of Nakajima's "My Journey to the West," drawn from the Chinese classic. Wujing, following the priest Xuanzang, sets down in his notes his admiration for the boundlessly free Sun Wukong, his affection for the likeable Zhu Bajie, and his thoughts on Xuanzang, who leads them quietly. Introspective and given to worrying over everything, Wujing deepens his reflections on human beings and on how to live through his companions. A still, meditative piece in which Nakajima gave Wujing his own likeness.

Summary

The story takes the form of notes written by Sha Wujing, a monk traveling with Tripitaka's party toward India, about his companions on the road. Wujing feels a longing and an awe he cannot master for Sun Wukong, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, who handles the arts of transformation without effort and shows a wild power that fears not even heaven. Wukong is a man of action who goes beyond all argument and lives out each present instant with his whole strength — the exact opposite of the inward Wujing, who broods over everything. There is Zhu Bajie too, true to his appetites and hard to dislike; and there is Tripitaka, who looks unreliable and yet quietly leads them all. One night in the mountains, looking up at the stars, Wujing suddenly understands the meaning of his master's clear gaze, which always seems fixed on something far away. His master is always looking at eternity, and pouring out an unending pity upon all the frail lives of this earth that must one day perish, set against that eternity. Nakajima drew Wujing almost as a second self: a man who, afraid of being hurt, has never been able to give himself fully to others, and who feels a warm light kindled deep in him by the company of his remarkable companions.

Characters

  • Sha Wujing　The inward-turning monk traveling with Tripitaka's party, who watches his companions and writes these notes. The narrator
  • Sun Wukong　The Great Sage Equal to Heaven, of matchless and unrestrained power. The man of action for whom Wujing feels an ungovernable longing and awe
  • Zhu Bajie　One of the party, true to his appetites and hard to dislike
  • Tripitaka　The master who leads them, whose gaze always holds eternity and a pity for the perishable things of earth

The opening (original text)

After the midday meal, while our master was resting a while under a pine tree by the road, Wukong took Bajie out to a field nearby and set him to practicing the art of transformation. "Try it!" says Wukong. "You must really want to be a dragon. Do you hear? Really. With the most extreme, the most concentrated feeling, you must want it. Throw away every other thought. Do you hear? In earnest. In the utmost, the last, the absolute earnest."

Gojō tanni by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I get up and look into the face of my master, sleeping beside me. And as I look a while at that peaceful sleeping face and listen to his quiet breathing, I feel a faint warmth, as though something had been lit deep inside me. — From "My Journey to the West" —

Gojō tanni by Nakajima Atsushi (Aozora Bunko)
