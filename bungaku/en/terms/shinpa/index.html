What is Shinpa (New School)?

Category
Form / Genre

A new form of theater that arose in the mid-Meiji era, opposing itself to the "old school" of kabuki. It is said to begin with the Dai Nihon Sōshi Kairyō Engeki-kai, launched in Osaka in 1888 by Sudō Sadanori, a soshi (activist) of the Freedom and People's Rights Movement, followed by the shosei shibai ("student theater") of Kawakami Otojirō, known for the "Oppekepe Bushi" song, which won popularity. At first it was politically colored soshi theater, but after scoring a hit with war plays during the First Sino-Japanese War, it shifted its footing toward contemporary drama depicting domestic tragedy and the world of the geisha quarters. The theatrical adaptations of newspaper novels such as Ozaki Kōyō's "Konjiki Yasha" ("The Golden Demon") and Tokutomi Roka's "Hototogisu" ("The Cuckoo") became great hits, and this point — how fiction and theater raised each other's popularity in tandem — is not to be overlooked in thinking about Meiji literature. Izumi Kyōka, too, became a major shinpa playwright with "Onna Keizu" ("A Woman's Pedigree") and "Nihonbashi," and the Yushima Shrine scene written for the stage adaptation of "Onna Keizu" is known as one of shinpa's great set pieces. Distinguished by its distinctive convention of onnagata (female-role actors) sharing the stage with actresses, it stands, as against the shingeki movement that sought to transplant Western modern drama, as a popular theater depicting the manners and sentiment of contemporary Japan, and it continues to this day.
