About this work

The short story that made Higuchi Ichiyō's name, published in the twenty-third number of Bungakukai in December 1894. It opens the fourteen miraculous months of her last creative period, and, praised warmly by Mori Ōgai and Kōda Rohan, established her as a writer. Through a maidservant driven into a corner in the house where she works, it draws sharply the gap between rich and poor and the kindness people are still capable of.

Summary

O-Mine, a maidservant in the Yamamura house, is valued even by her proud mistress for her honest work, but she carries an anxiety no one knows. Her uncle and his wife, laid low by illness, are at the very bottom of want; and at the end of the year, on the last day of the twelfth month, she had asked the mistress for the two ryō needed to help her uncle, and had once been promised it. But when the day comes the mistress puts her off with an innocent face, saying she remembers no such promise. Driven to the wall, O-Mine, tormented by her conscience, takes two pieces of gold from the drawer of the inkstone chest and hands them to her uncle's son Sannosuke, who has come to fetch them. Meanwhile the house has a prodigal son, Ishinosuke, all but disowned, who comes that night too to beg money and gets fifty yen out of his father before he leaves. In the evening the great reckoning shows the money short, and just as O-Mine has made up her mind to confess, there is found in the drawer a note in Ishinosuke's hand: "I have borrowed what was in the drawer as well." Without their knowing it, the prodigal Ishinosuke has taken her guilt upon himself.

Characters

  • O-Mine　A maidservant in the Yamamura house. The heroine, who puts her hand to her master's money to help her sick uncle
  • The mistress　The lady of the Yamamura house, who promises O-Mine the loan and afterward goes back on it
  • Ishinosuke　The prodigal son of the Yamamura house, all but disowned. He comes to beg money even on the last night of the year, and ends by taking O-Mine's guilt upon himself
  • Yasubei　O-Mine's uncle. He is bedridden with illness and his household is destitute
  • Sannosuke　Yasubei's son. A dutiful boy of eight who sells freshwater clams to help the household

The opening (original text)

The well works by a pulley, and the rope is twelve fathoms long; the kitchen faces north, and the dry wind of the twelfth month blows whistling through it — oh, unbearable, she thinks, playing with the fire before the stove, where one minute stretches into an hour; and for a bit of firewood no bigger than a splinter she is scolded as if it were a great matter. Such is the lot of a maidservant.

Ōtsugomori by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

O-Mine had taken only two; the rest should have been eighteen, and how it happened no one knew, but the whole bundle was gone. They turned the drawer over and shook it, in vain. What was strange was a scrap of paper fallen among the litter, a receipt written at some time or other. ("I have borrowed what was in the drawer as well. Ishinosuke.") So it is the prodigal, they thought, looking at one another; and O-Mine was never questioned. Had the merit of her filial piety, without her knowing it, become Ishinosuke's crime? No, no — perhaps he knew, and took the crime on himself while he was about it. If so, Ishinosuke must be O-Mine's guardian deity. I should like to know what came afterward.

Ōtsugomori by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

The well had a windlass and a rope twelve fathoms long; the kitchen faced north, and the dry December wind blew clean through it, whistling, bitterly cold.

An opening that begins with the cold of the kitchen where she is in service. In the celebrated mixed classical-colloquial cadence, it depicts a single day of the girl Omine, driven to desperation for money on New Year's Eve.
