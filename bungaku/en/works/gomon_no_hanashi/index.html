About this work

A piece in which Okamoto Kidō set down the kinds and the procedure of the tortures actually used in the jails of Edo, from the testimony of a former officer of the city magistrate's court. The stages — flogging, the stone weights, the shrimp binding, the hanging — and the trial of the thief Kichigorō, who never confessed to the end, are told in an even hand. A valuable record for understanding the background of historical fiction as well.

Summary

On the twelfth day of the fourth month of the fifth year of Tenpō, Kichigorō, a vagrant from Banshū with no fixed abode, was arrested by the Edo city magistracy and sent to the prison at Temmachō. He was a habitual thief who had already been tattooed and sentenced to a heavy flogging once before in Osaka. Coming to Edo with accomplices, he went on stealing, and it came to light that he had stolen four tortoiseshell combs from a sundries shop in Ningyōchō, Nihonbashi, and sold them to a receiver of stolen goods. But since not one of his accomplices had been caught, Kichigorō stubbornly denied the crime to the last. From here the essay turns to the actual practice of torture within Edo's criminal justice system. Flogging, stone-sitting, the shrimp bind, and, most severe of all, suspension torture — each method is described in concrete detail, along with the requirement that a physician be present and that permission from the senior councillors be obtained, drawing on the teachings of Sakuma Nagataka, a former yoriki of the North Magistracy. Kichigorō submitted to none of it. After an investigation stretching across nearly three years, the magistracy petitioned the senior councillors for a satto-zume ruling — an extraordinarily rare procedure, unheard of for a man of samurai rank, by which guilt is determined from circumstantial evidence rather than the accused's own confession — and after repeated deliberation, permission was finally granted. On the twenty-third day of the fifth month of the seventh year of Tenpō, Kichigorō was sentenced to death. The ranking prisoners within the jail, wishing to send him off in style, presented him with a new hemp robe, a new undershirt, a new sash, and new white tabi. As Kichigorō, dressed in this finery, was led out, the assembled prisoners sent up after him every manner of praise — "Japan's finest," "the boss," "Ishikawa Goemon."

The opening (original text)

On the twelfth day of the fourth month of the fifth year of Tenpō, the year of the Horse, Kichigorō, a vagrant of Banshū with no fixed abode, was arrested by the hand of the Edo city magistracy and sent to the prison at Temmachō. He went by the common name Sadazō, and had already, some years before in Osaka, been tattooed and sentenced to a heavy flogging — a habitual thief.

Gōmon no hanashi by Okamoto Kidō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But in the end, this was still a satto-zume ruling, not his own confession. He had not submitted to torture, to the very last. The ranking prisoners within the jail, wishing to send him off in style, gave him a new hemp robe, together with a new undershirt, a new sash, and new white tabi. When Kichigorō, dressed in this finery, was led out of the jail, the assembled prisoners who saw him off sent up after him every manner of praise — "Japan's finest," "the boss," "Ishikawa Goemon."
