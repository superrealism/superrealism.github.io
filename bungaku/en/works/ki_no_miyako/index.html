About this work

An autobiographical short story Oda Sakunosuke published in 1944. Set in Osaka, called a city without trees, it follows the trees of the Uemachi plateau that coloured his early memories — the camphor at the Ikutama shrine, the trees along Kuchinawa slope and Genshōji slope — and looks back on his own boyhood. In the latter part, the sad story of the record shop Meikyokudō halfway up a slope, visited again after ten years, and of the family's intellectually disabled son Shinbō, is told feelingly against the background of wartime. A fine work showing well the lyrical side of Oda, run through with a deep attachment to Osaka.

Summary

"Osaka is a city without trees," people say, but strangely enough, the author's memories of childhood are bound up with trees. The old camphor tree in the grounds of Ikukunitama Shrine, said to be home to a snake god and too frightening to approach; the ginkgo tree whose branches once held out the author's kimono to dry after he fell into the lotus pond at Kitamuki Hachiman; the greenery covering Genshōji Slope and Kuchinawa Slope — tracing these memories of the trees of the Uemachi plateau, "I" calls to mind his own boyhood. In time the account turns to a record shop, "Meikyokudō," that once stood halfway up that slope. The family who ran it had a son, little Shin, who loved records and music above all else and had an intellectual disability. When "I" visits the slope again after ten years and looks in on Meikyokudō, he finds the shop shut tight, a notice pasted to the door reading "Closed in light of the present circumstances." Asking the old man next door, he learns that, for the sake of Shin, who was forever pining for the old house, the family had made up their minds to move to Nagoya, where Shin now was, to live and work together there. Kuchinawa Slope stood cold and bare, its trees withered, a white wind running through it. Descending the stone steps, "I" reflects that the sweetness of youthful reminiscence has come to an end, and that a new reality has turned to face him. A lyrical, fine piece by Oda Sakunosuke, filled with tender affection for the slopes and trees of Osaka.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, evoking the author himself, raised on the Uemachi plateau of Osaka. Traces his childhood memories of trees and his own youth
  • Little Shin　The son of the record shop Meikyokudō, halfway up the slope. Has an intellectual disability and loves records and music
  • The master of Meikyokudō and his daughter　The family who close the shop for Shin's sake and move to Nagoya, where Shin now lives

The opening (original text)

Osaka is said to be a city without trees, but my memories of childhood are strangely bound up with trees all the same. There was the old camphor tree in the grounds of Ikukunitama Shrine, too frightening to go near because they said a snake god lived within it; the ginkgo tree in the grounds of Kitamuki Hachiman, on whose branches I once hung my kimono to dry after falling into the lotus pond there; the old pine in the grounds of a temple in Nakadera-chō, which hid the color of cicadas among its needles; the trees that covered Genshōji Slope and Kuchinawa Slope in green — I was never, in truth, raised in a city without trees. Osaka, at least for me, was never a city without trees.

Ki no miyako by Oda Sakunosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

On Kuchinawa Slope the trees stood cold and withered, and a white wind ran through them. As I descended the stone steps, I thought that I would probably not be climbing up and down this slope again for some time to come. It seemed to me that the sweetness of youthful reminiscence had come to an end, and that a new reality had turned to face me. The wind struck hard against the treetops.

Ki no miyako by Oda Sakunosuke (Aozora Bunko)
