About this work

Stevenson's novella (1886), which depicts good and evil living together in one man under separate shapes and has become the byword for the split personality. It is translated by Sasaki Naojirō, known for his renderings of Poe, whose translator's afterword is dated November 1940. The case is followed by the lawyer Utterson, and the truth about the sinister little man Hyde is revealed at the last by the doctor's own written statement. The taut narration of the original and the feel of the London streets in fog are carried over into shapely Japanese.

Summary

The lawyer Utterson hears a strange story from his kinsman Enfield while out walking. At daybreak a little man trampled a child at a street corner and went on unmoved, and for reparation handed over a check signed with the name of a gentleman of the first rank — the name of his old friend Dr. Henry Jekyll. And Jekyll's will provides that in case of his disappearance or absence his whole estate is to pass to one Edward Hyde. Utterson tracks Hyde down, and finds in his appearance something that stirs an unspeakable loathing. Before long Sir Danvers Carew, a man of note, is beaten to death in the street by Hyde, and Hyde vanishes. Jekyll grows cheerful and renews old friendships; but from a certain time he shuts himself up in his laboratory again and will see no one. Called by the butler Poole and breaking down the door, Utterson finds the body of Hyde in Jekyll's clothes. What is left behind is a sealed packet from his old friend Dr. Lanyon and Jekyll's own detailed statement. The experiment of trying to separate the good and evil within a man by means of a drug, and the terror of finding that before long the other personality came out regardless of his will — all of it is written there.

Characters

  • Dr. Jekyll　A man of medicine who attempts the separation of good and evil
  • Edward Hyde　The loathsome little man who comes out of the doctor
  • Utterson　Jekyll's old friend, a lawyer, who follows the case
  • Dr. Lanyon　Jekyll's old friend, a physician, who leaves part of the truth in a sealed packet
  • Poole　The butler of Jekyll's house, who tells Utterson of the strange goings-on in the laboratory

The opening (original text)

Story of the Door Mr. Utterson the lawyer was a man of a rugged countenance that was never lighted by a smile; cold, scanty and embarrassed in discourse; backward in sentiment; lean, long, dusty, dreary, and yet somehow lovable. At friendly meetings, and when the wine was to his taste, something eminently human beaconed from his eye — something indeed which never found its way into his talk, but which spoke not only in these silent symbols of the after-dinner face, but more often and loudly in the acts of his life. He was austere with himself; drank gin when he was alone, to mortify a taste for vintages.

Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde by Sasaki Naojirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Will Hyde die upon the scaffold? Or will he find the courage to release himself at the last moment? God knows; I am careless. This is my true hour of death, and what is to follow concerns another than myself. Here, then, as I lay down the pen and proceed to seal up my confession, I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end.

Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde by Sasaki Naojirō (Aozora Bunko)
