About this work

Kikuchi Kan's central historical novel, published in Chūō Kōron in September 1918. With Matsudaira Tadanao, grandson of Tokugawa Ieyasu and lord of six hundred and seventy thousand koku in Echizen, for its protagonist, it draws the tragedy of a young lord who happens to learn what his retainers really think, falls to the bottom of a mistrust of everyone, and turns into a tyrant. Reading behind Tadanao's cruelty the loneliness and suspicion of a man to whom, because of his position, no one will speak a true word, it shapes him as a victim of the feudal system. Famous in literary history as the type of Kikuchi's thesis novel, digging into one subject with a lucid structure.

Summary

Immediately after the Summer Siege of Osaka, Matsudaira Tadanao, Lesser Captain of Echizen, is scorned to his face by his grandfather Ieyasu for the poor showing of his own troops, and suffers a deep humiliation. The young Tadanao throws himself into martial training to restore his honor, but one night he overhears that his retainers, in their true hearts, despise his skill and merely flatter their lord with empty praise. Even in sparring matches, he learns, his retainers had let him win on purpose. Having chanced to overhear this, Tadanao becomes possessed by the suspicion that every word and gesture around him is nothing but sycophancy toward his rank. From then on he cannot help but test his retainers again and again, and as he uncovers their betrayals and self-serving cowardice, his suspicion swells, little by little, into savage cruelty. Striking down maidservants and retainers alike, Tadanao earns throughout the realm the infamous name of "tyrant," until at last the shogunate strips him of his domain, and he casts aside his sixty-seven-thousand-koku fief as though it were a worn-out sandal, exiled to Bungo. And yet, in his place of exile, stripped of every rank and reduced to a single human being, Tadanao spends his final years in a gentle, humble calm, as though some possessing spirit had at last left him. Nowhere does the shadow of the former tyrant remain; and it is this man, who came to love others without regard for rank, that his guard sets down in writing in the "Record of Lord Tadanao's Conduct."

Characters

  • Tokugawa Ieyasu　Tadanao's grandfather. Scorns Tadanao to his face over his conduct at the Siege of Osaka, setting off his grandson's downfall

The opening (original text)

Summoned to Ieyasu's headquarters, Lord Tadanao's chief retainers were dressed down without mercy and sent away in utter disgrace. "Today, while the forces of Ii and Tōdō fought their hard battle, were the men of Echizen's household napping, that they knew nothing of it? Had you pressed in behind both camps and closed on the castle, the fall of Osaka would have been at hand — but your commander is young, and you are the greatest cowards in all Japan, and so you botched a battle that should have been ours for the taking" — so Ieyasu railed at them, bitter to the last word, and rose abruptly from his seat.

Tadanaokyō gyōjōki by Kikuchi Kan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Of Lord Tadanao's life in his final years, no historical record has come down to us. There exists only a single volume, the "Record of Lord Tadanao's Conduct," which Takenaka Utaemon-no-shō Shigetsugu, lord of Funai Castle and charged with Tadanao's guard, had his own retainers compile from his observations of Tadanao's conduct, and sent on to Doi Ōi-no-kami Toshikatsu, a councillor of the shogunate. One passage of it reads: "Since His Lordship's removal to this province of Tsumori, he has shown not the slightest trace of violent behavior, and has lived out his days in peace. ...In all things His Lordship is modest and circumspect, showing pity to those who attend him closely and tenderness to the people of this land, so that even now people cannot help but marvel, wondering how a man who lost a domain of sixty-seven thousand koku could seem so far from any lawless tyrant."

Tadanaokyō gyōjōki by Kikuchi Kan (Aozora Bunko)
