About this work

The piece for the year of the cock — legends of the fowl — serialized in five parts in Taiyō in 1921. It begins from an examination of the old Chinese custom of nailing a cock to the gate at the New Year to drive off evil influences, and ranges over the beliefs East and West that make the egg auspicious, and the myths and tales about fowl. At the end, as a naturalist, he describes in detail the four wild jungle fowl from which the domestic bird descends, and compares them with the account in the Wakan Sansai Zue.

Summary

According to the Wen lisu, cited in the commentary on the Jingchu suishiji, the first day of the first month was made the day of the cock, the second of the dog, the third of the sheep, the fourth of the pig, the fifth of the ox, the sixth of the horse and the seventh of man. On New Year's Day a cock was painted on the gate, and on that day no cock was killed. On the second day men and cocks were feasted — such records Kumagusu sets out with care. But in older times, on the contrary, a cock was crucified and hung at the gate on New Year's Day to drive off evil influences. Together with the custom of hanging a sheep's head at the gate, this was explained as killing the sheep that gnaws the hundred grasses and the cock that pecks the five grains, so as to help the vital force at the time when the breath of the earth rises and the grasses and trees begin to stir. In time, instead of crucifying a cock, people came to paste a picture of one at the door. The talk then moves to the egg: the method of "tempering the form" by swallowing an egg on New Year's Day to harden the body; the Western custom of exchanging eggs at Easter; the creation myths of ancient Greece and India in which the world begins from a golden egg. That the Sanskrit aṇḍa means both egg and testicle leads him even to the slang of China concerning eggs, and the run of his pen is unrestrained. In closing he describes in detail four wild fowls — the red jungle fowl, the gray jungle fowl, the Ceylon jungle fowl and the rest — which are the wild stock of the domestic bird.

The opening (original text)

In the commentary on the Jingchu suishiji of Zong Lin of the Jin, the Wen lisu of Dong of the Wei says: the first day of the first month is made the cock, the second day the dog, the third the sheep, the fourth the pig, the fifth the ox, the sixth the horse, the seventh man. On New Year's morning a cock is painted on the gate and on the seventh day a man is pasted on the curtain; and that today no cock is killed on the first day, no dog on the second, no sheep on the third, no pig on the fourth, no ox on the fifth, no horse on the sixth, and that on the seventh no punishment is carried out (no man is killed) — this too is of that meaning, and so forth.

Jūnishikō (tori) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

There are various wild fowls, but let us take four. In English they are called jungle fowl. The first, the red jungle fowl, is beyond doubt the wild stock of all domestic fowls; it lives in the forests and bamboo thickets from India to farther India, and is found also on the island of Timor near the Philippines.... I would suggest that there is ground for what the Wakan sansai zue says: that if the egg the domestic hen lays each day is taken away each time she will go on laying without number, but that if they are left she will lay twelve eggs and stop.

Jūnishikō (tori) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)
