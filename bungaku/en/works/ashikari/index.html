About this work

A novella Tanizaki published in the magazine Kaizō in 1932. It has the frame of a court tale: the narrator, visiting the site of the Minase detached palace of the retired emperor Go-Toba, drinks and views the moon on a sandbar in the Yodo river, and hears the history of a man who appears from among the reeds. What the man tells is the strangely pure love of his dead father for the beautiful widow Oyū. Its flowing style, largely in kana with the punctuation held back, and its fantastical ending were highly praised, and it is counted with "Arrowroot of Yoshino" and A Portrait of Shunkin among the central works of his classical period.

Summary

One September day, "I" visit the site of the old Minase Palace and, gazing at the moon from a sandbar in the Yodo River, drink alone. From among the reeds a man appears and begins to tell his story. The man's father, Shinnosuke, had as a young man fallen in love with Oyū, the beautiful widow of an old Osaka family. But Oyū, still carrying the memory of her late husband and bound by obligation to his family, could not remarry. Her younger sister Oshizu, wanting only to serve her, entered into a marriage with Shinnosuke in name alone, and the three went on living together in a strange household centered on Oyū. When a child dies, suspicion from those around them grows, and Oyū is married off again to a sake brewer in Fushimi, while Shinnosuke and Oshizu become husband and wife in truth — and the man, he says, was born of that union. He tells "me" that even now, on the night of every full moon, he goes to peer at the villa by Lake Ogura where Oyū lives — but when "I" ask whether Oyū would not by now be nearly eighty, the man's figure dissolves away, as if melting into the moonlight.

Characters

  • I　The narrator, who visits the site of Minase and hears the man's story by the river
  • The man　A mysterious figure who appears from among the reeds on a moonlit night. Recounts his father's memories of Oyū
  • Oyū　A beautiful widow of an old Osaka family. Gentle and elegant, living waited upon by all around her
  • Shinnosuke　The man's father. Loves Oyū while entering a marriage in name only with her sister
  • Oshizu　Oyū's younger sister. Marries Shinnosuke so as to serve her sister, and becomes the bond between the two

The opening (original text)

Now that you are gone, how bitter it has grown (ashikari) — more wretched still, this dwelling by Naniwa's shore. It was in September of a year when I was still living in Okamoto. The weather was so fine that toward evening — though evening here means only a little past three — I found myself suddenly wanting to go out walking somewhere nearby. It was too late in the day for a long excursion, and I had already come to know most of the places close by; searching for some forgotten spot within two or three hours' reach that no one else would think of, I realized I had long meant to visit the site of the Minase Palace, without ever finding the occasion.

Ashikari by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Yes, indeed," he said, "and tonight too I am on my way there now. Even now, on the night of the full moon, I go round to the back of that villa and peer in through the gap in the hedge, and there is Oyū-san playing the koto while she has her maid dance for her." I thought this an odd thing to say, and asked whether Oyū-san would not by now be an old woman close to eighty — but there was only the wind sighing softly through the grass, and not even the reeds that had covered the water's edge were to be seen any longer, and the man's shadow too had vanished, melting away, before I knew it, into the light of the moon.

Ashikari by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)
