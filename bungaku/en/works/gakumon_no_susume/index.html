About this work

A work of enlightenment in seventeen parts, published by Fukuzawa Yukichi between 1872 and 1876. It is known above all for its opening line — "Heaven creates no man above another, nor any man below" — and argues that people are born equal, that the differences between them arise from whether or not they pursue learning, and that what Japan needs is practical knowledge, all in deliberately plain language. Said to have sold several million copies at a time when that approached a tenth of Japan's population, it was the great bestseller of the Meiji modernization and is still read today.

Summary

The first part opens from the line "Heaven creates no man above another, nor any man below," and argues that there is no inborn distinction of rank or wealth between one person and another, and that such differences as exist arise from whether or not one has pursued learning. The parts that follow take up the relation between the independence of the individual and the independence of the nation, rights and duties under the law, the uses of public speaking and debate, the proper office of the scholar, and how to earn regard and get on with others — everything, in plain colloquial prose, that is needed to leave feudal habit behind and live as a modern citizen. Throughout runs the single insistence that learning must not be idle theory but practical learning, of real use in daily life and in society, and with it the enlightenment conviction that the nation is to be upheld by each person standing on their own feet.

The opening (original text)

Part One It is said that "Heaven creates no man above another, nor any man below." Heaven, then, in bringing forth human beings, makes all of them equal, without distinction of high or low from birth; it is the intent of heaven that they should use the workings of the body and mind that make them the noblest of creatures to draw upon all the things that lie between heaven and earth, and so provide themselves with food and clothing and shelter, and pass through this world freely and at ease, each without hindering another.

The ending (original text)

Many as human beings are, they are neither demons nor serpents, and there is no enemy who sets out on purpose to do you harm. Have no fear and no reserve: show what is in your mind and deal with people briskly. To widen one's circle, then, one must enlarge that mind as far as possible, be accomplished in many things rather than confined to one, and meet people from many directions. With one you may associate through learning, with another through trade; there are friends in painting and calligraphy, and partners at go and shogi. Anything short of dissipation and vice may serve as an occasion for gathering friends. Do not, being human, take a dislike to your fellow human beings.

Famous passages

It is said: “Heaven does not create one person above another, nor one below another.”

The opening line, emblematic of the enlightenment of modern Japan. It declares that no one is born noble or base, that the difference between people lies only in whether they learn — a manifesto of independence and self-respect.

The difference, then, between the wise and the foolish comes about simply through whether one learns or not.

A passage following the celebrated opening. It argues that a person's worth is decided not by birth but by whether he learns, stating plainly the book's purpose of encouraging learning.

Independence means governing oneself, with no inclination to lean upon others.

A sentence defining independence in the third installment. Fukuzawa goes on to describe judging right and wrong for oneself and earning one's own living, linking individual self-reliance to national independence.

If we Japanese, too, now set our minds on learning, firm up our spirit, first seek our independence as individuals, and thereby bring about the wealth and strength of the nation, why should we fear the power of the Westerners? We shall associate with those who have reason on their side, and drive away those who have none. This is what is meant by “when the individual is independent, the nation is independent.”

A passage from Part Three. Teaching that each person's learning and self-reliance lead to the independence of the nation, it forms the core of Fukuzawa's thought, known by the phrase “when the individual is independent, the nation is independent.”
