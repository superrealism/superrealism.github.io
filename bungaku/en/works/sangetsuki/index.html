About this work

The short story with which Nakajima Atsushi entered the literary world, published in the magazine Bungakukai in February 1942 together with "The Curse of Letters" under the joint title "Old Tales." Based on the Chinese tale Renhu zhuan, it follows a poet destroyed by his own timid pride and arrogant shame, who is transformed into a tiger. Its spare, formal prose in the Chinese manner and its sharp anatomy of a man seeing himself from outside have made it a fixture of Japanese school textbooks and the best known of all Nakajima's work.

Summary

Ri Chō, famous for his brilliance, would not stoop to remain a petty official; he resigned his post and threw himself into poetry, but fame did not come easily and he fell into poverty. Returning to office for the sake of his wife and children, wounded in his pride at having to take orders from men he had once looked down on, he goes mad at last and disappears. The next year his old friend En San, travelling, hears rumours of a man-eating tiger. The tiger that comes out of the grass astonishes him by calling out in a human voice, asking whether this is not his friend Ri Chō — for it is Ri Chō himself. As a tiger he speaks of what destroyed him: a timid pride and an arrogant shame. He entrusts En San with his unfinished poetry and recites some of his verse. Asking his friend to look to his wife and children, and saying that they must never meet again, Ri Chō shows him at the last his own hideous tiger's shape, roars at the moon, and vanishes back into the depths of the grass.

Characters

  • Ri Chō　A learned poet. The protagonist, who goes mad from his wounded pride and is turned into a tiger
  • En San　Ri Chō's old friend. Travelling as a censor, he meets Ri Chō again in the form of a tiger

The opening (original text)

Ri Chō of Longxi was learned and gifted. In the last years of the Tianbao era his name was set while he was still young on the tiger list of successful candidates, and he was appointed subprefect of Jiangnan; but he was stubborn and unbending by nature, with a very high opinion of himself, and would not stoop to a petty post. Before long he retired from office, went back to live in seclusion in his home country, broke off all society, and gave himself wholly to writing poetry.

The ending (original text)

When the party had reached the top of the hill they looked back as they had been told, at the grassy clearing among the trees. In an instant they saw a tiger spring out of the thick grass onto the road. The tiger looked up at the moon, white now and without light, and roared two or three times; then it leapt back into the grass, and they saw it no more.

Famous passages

Ri Chō of Longxi was a man of broad learning and keen talent; in the last years of the Tianbao era he passed the civil examinations while still young and was appointed a district officer in the south of the Yangtze. But he was proud and unbending by nature, thought a great deal of himself, and could not bring himself to be content with the post of a petty official.

An opening that introduces its protagonist in a single dignified sentence of Sino-Japanese cadence. Already it hints at the tragedy to come: a man whose pride as a poet will bring him down into the body of a tiger.

Both are the doing of my own cowardly self-regard, and of a shame so overweening it became pride.

A scene in which Ri Chō, now a tiger, reveals his heart to an old friend. This line, naming both a poet's pride and his fear, is often quoted as a figure for the human heart itself.

It was not only the night dew that had left his fur wet.

A line Ri Chō, now a tiger, lets slip as he parts from his friend. This restraint — conveying tears without ever saying he wept — sets the dignified tone of the entire story.

While I toyed with glib epigrams like “Life is too long to do nothing, yet too short to accomplish anything,” in truth all I was, was a cowardly fear that my lack of talent might be exposed and a laziness that shrank from hard work.

The confession of Ri Chō, now a tiger, regretting a past in which he failed to polish his talent. Quoting a famous epigram with self-mockery, he gazes painfully at his own weakness in avoiding effort.

They say every man is a tamer of wild beasts, and that the beast is each man's own nature. In my case, this overweening shame was the beast. It was the tiger.

Ri Chō explaining why he became a tiger. He realizes that everyone keeps a beast within, and that his present form is the result of failing to master his own.

“That voice—is it not my friend, Ri Chō?”

What En San cries out, having nearly been attacked by a tiger, when he recognizes the voice from the thicket. This unexpected reunion with an old friend draws out Ri Chō's confession.

If the human heart within me were to vanish completely, I would probably be the happier for it. And yet the human being within me feels that very thing to be the most terrible of all.

Words from the scene in which Ri Chō, turned into a tiger, opens his heart to his old friend. Knowing that he would be at ease if he lost his human heart, yet fearing that above all else — the ache of being human is condensed in this passage.
