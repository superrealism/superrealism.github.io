About this work

One of the pieces in Minakata Kumagusu's central work Studies of the Twelve Branches — a linked series of essays citing tales, legends and folklore of all ages and countries about the animals of the twelve branches — treating the tiger for the year of the tiger, published in the magazine Taiyō in 1914. From the origin of the tiger's name to the accounts of it, the superstitions about magic and divination, and even stories of wolves and monkeys raising human children, he traces the genealogy of the imagination about people and beasts with astonishing learning, citing works East and West at will.

Summary

In "On the Meaning of the Name," Minakata lines up the Sanskrit, Tamil, and Arabic names for the tiger, down to the English "tiger," and connects its etymology to the Persian word for "arrow," explaining the belief that the tiger runs swiftly as an arrow flies. He goes on to cite descriptions of the tiger's habits, and examples such as the Japanese use of "a tamed tiger" as a phrase compared to a cat, drawing from The Tale of Genji and countless other books. In the latter half, he introduces the superstition of "tiger divination," in which a tiger is said to draw marks on the ground to divine its prey; the popular Annamese belief that to be eaten by a tiger is the working out of karma from a former life; and tales in which a tiger transforms into a human being to give prophecies, and a sorcerer transforms into a tiger. Further still, drawing on real cases of Japan's mountain god (the wolf) helping and raising human children, and even a London newspaper account of a boy raised by a troop of monkeys in South Africa, he widens his associations from tiger to wolf to monkey, ranging widely over folk tales from around the world concerning people and beasts.

The opening (original text)

(1) On the Meaning of the Name The tiger's Sanskrit name is vyāghra; in modern Indian languages, bāgh; in the Tamil of South India, pili; the Javanese name is macan, the Malay is rimau, the Arabic is nimr, and in English, tiger — and most other European languages have names similar to this, all of them based on the Greek and Latin tigris. That name, tigris, is said to derive from the Old Persian tigri, meaning "arrow," comparing the tiger's swift running to the flight of an arrow. In our own country too, people have long believed, without ever actually having seen one, that a tiger could run a thousand ri, and a play jokingly praises Katō Kiyomasa's swiftness by calling him Torahonosuke, "one leap a thousand ri."

Jūnishikō (tora) by Minakata Kumagusu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And here is quite a recent one, of a monkey raising a human child. One might suspect I concocted this myself, given that Count Itagaki Taisuke's daughter happens to be named Saruko, but it is no invention of mine — it appeared in the Morning Post, published in London, on August 9 of last year. Twenty-five years ago, two mounted policemen in the wilds of a state southeast of the Cape of Good Hope found a boy mixed in among a troop of monkeys, brought him home, named him Lucas, and taught him farming; though his intelligence fell short of his fellows, he surpassed them in strength, diligence, and trustworthiness, served his master faithfully, and was especially fond of watching over the man's child. The strange thing about him was that he had no concept whatsoever of time. (End)
