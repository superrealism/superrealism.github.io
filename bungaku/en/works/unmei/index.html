About this work

A fine historical narrative Kōda Rohan published in the first number of the magazine Kaizō in 1919. Its subject is the Jingnan campaign, the struggle for the throne after the death of the Ming founder, the Hongwu emperor, between his grandson the Jianwen emperor and his uncle the prince of Yan, later the Yongle emperor. The legend that the deposed Jianwen emperor wandered China disguised as a monk is told in a strong and splendid classical prose. Rohan had long put down the novelist's pen, and here brought his ripened learning to bear on the pitilessness of history and the unmeasurable fortunes of men.

Summary

The Hongwu emperor, founder of the Ming, dies, and his grandson, the young Jianwen emperor, comes to the throne. Jianwen honors his Confucian ministers and means to rule benevolently; but seeking to cut down the strength of the great princedoms held by his uncles, he punishes one prince after another. His uncle the Prince of Yan, enfeoffed at Beiping, sees the danger closing on him, raises troops, and turns against his nephew under the pretext of "removing the evil ones at the sovereign's side." This is the Jingnan campaign. After more than three years of bitter civil war, the Prince of Yan's army at last takes the capital, Nanjing. The palace is wrapped in flames, and it was said that the Jianwen emperor burned to death; but there remained a legend that he had in fact escaped in secret in the guise of a monk. The Prince of Yan, having usurped the throne, comes to it as the Yongle emperor, and in his suspicion has the whereabouts of the former emperor searched for relentlessly. Meanwhile the Jianwen emperor in his surplice wanders the mountains and rivers of China for decades under the protection of loyal followers, and only in old age is his identity discovered, so that he is taken into the palace and quietly ends his life. The Yongle emperor too, the victor of history, meets a mysterious end on the road of a campaign — and the author, weaving together history and legend, sighs again and again at how immeasurable a thing that "destiny" is which lies beyond human knowledge.

Characters

  • The Jianwen emperor　The second emperor of the Ming and grandson of the founder, the Hongwu emperor. Driven from the throne by his uncle the Prince of Yan, he is said to have become a monk and wandered from place to place
  • The Yongle emperor (the Prince of Yan)　The Jianwen emperor's uncle, a prince enfeoffed at Beiping. He raises the Jingnan campaign, usurps the throne, and searches relentlessly for the former emperor
  • Cheng Ji　A loyal follower who attends the Jianwen emperor and supports him to the end of his wandering life

The opening (original text)

Is there in the world such a thing as destiny? If one says there is, it is as though there were; if one takes it that there is none, it seems there is none. Though the flood rose to heaven, the merit of Yu governed it; though the great drought scorched the earth, the virtue of Tang relieved it: so it is as though there were a destiny, and yet as though there were none. The First Emperor of Qin made the empire one and took the honored title, and his majesty was truly not to be withstood. And yet a water god appeared one night at Huayin and gave a jade disk to his messenger, saying that this year the Ancestral Dragon would die; and in fact the First Emperor died before long at Shaqiu. Xuanzong of Tang enjoyed thirty years of peace in the Kaiyuan era and gave himself for fourteen years to the luxury of the Tianbao. And yet at the height of Kaiyuan the ācārya Yixing memorialized that His Majesty would make a progress of ten thousand li and that the sacred reign would be without bound; and the emperor thought it a hard saying — until the rebellion of An Lushan broke out and, entering Shu in the fifteenth year of Tianbao, he came to the Bridge of Ten Thousand Li and started, and understood. Thinking of such things, it seems there is no destiny, and yet it seems there is. Looking at what the tales and unofficial histories record — the Dingminglu, the Xu dingminglu, the Qiandinglu, the Gandinglu and the rest — one begins to doubt whether fortune and misfortune are not all of them fixed, and whether our drinking and pecking, our laughing and weeping, do not all depend upon the will of heaven. But what are these confused miscellanies, that they should be believed? And even granting that there is a destiny, destiny is a thing that cannot be measured. Rather than fear a destiny that cannot be measured, and bow the head before diviners and readers of faces, it is better to follow the way that can be known and set the heart at rest under the teaching of the sages and worthies of old. And besides, it is the common feeling of men that he who has failed invokes the decree of heaven and laments, and he who has succeeded speaks of his own strength and boasts. Both are to be called mean. If, when a thing fails, a man lays it to his own want of virtue, and when a work succeeds leaves it to the fixedness of destiny, then such a man may be called true and not false, and his capacity great and not small. The ancient sage said: he who knows does not speak; he who speaks does not know. He who speaks of destiny does not know destiny; and he who does not speak of it may perhaps know it well.

The ending (original text)

Destiny? Is it destiny? The ordination certificate, the surplice and the razor in the red box — ah, how strange again. The Taoist's dream of warning, the single boat on the palace moat — ah, how strange again. Once, reading the History of Ming, I was astonished at these strange things and wished to utter with the Jianwen emperor those words, "this is destiny." Afterward I read the life of Daoyan. In it is written: Daoyan died in the sixteenth year of Yongle; and as he lay dying the emperor asked what he wished to say. Yan said, "A monk named Puqia has been imprisoned this long while. I beg that he may be pardoned." Puqia was the Jianwen emperor's registrar-monk. When the emperor first entered Nanjing, some said that the Jianwen emperor had become a monk and fled and that Puqia knew how it stood, and others said that he was hidden at Puqia's. So the emperor imprisoned Puqia on another charge, and ordered the supervising secretary Hu Ying and others to search out the Jianwen emperor. For a long time they did not find him. Puqia lay in prison for it more than ten years, and now at last the emperor, on Daoyan's word, ordered him let out. Yan bowed his head in thanks, and presently died. The vermilion writing in the box, the Taoist's dream, the words of Wang Yue, the death of Wu Liang, Daoyan's petition and Puqia's silence — ah, whether these were destiny or not, Daoyan may perhaps have known. But whether the death far from home at Yumuchuan and the roasting death of Gaoxu were destiny or not — that is a thing Daoyan and Yuan and their like could never have known; heaven alone may know it.
