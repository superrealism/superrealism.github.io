About this work

A short story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Asahi Graph in 1933. In the quiet residential district of Suma, a rumour spreads that a leopard has escaped. But the lips pressed on Shōzō in a dark corridor — that was the true leopard, lurking nearer than the beast that was shot. A sharply cut piece of weird and unsettling psychology.

Summary

Getting off the train at Tsukimiyama, Shōzō found the road, usually so quiet, astir with the movement of people. As he climbed toward the hillside, he found that even at the gate of the musician's house — the one the neighbors called the "Rose Mansion" — the young wife stood at the center of a knot of neighborhood women, all whispering together. "At our place we've sent someone out to buy pistol bullets" — it seemed a leopard had escaped from somewhere. But what held Shōzō's attention was not the escaped beast at all. Shōzō was staying at the house in Suma together with his sister-in-law, his elder brother's wife — and in the dark corridor, something had been pressed against Shōzō's own lips. At the story's close, hearing his sister-in-law's voice, rich with feeling, ask, "There was a sound out back just now, wasn't there? Could it be the leopard, I wonder?" — Shōzō feels a chill run through him. Had she not already known, hours before, that the leopard had been shot dead? More intimate, more immediate, than that shot beast — the thing that had been pressed against his own lips in the dark corridor — that, surely, had been the true form of the "leopard." Shaken by a sudden chill, Shōzō leaves the house in Suma that very afternoon and returns to Tokyo. A short story of Yamamoto's that cuts sharply into an eerie and unsettling state of mind.

The opening (original text)

Getting off the train at Tsukimiyama, Shōzō saw people stirring about on a road that was, as a rule, always so quiet. Thinking little of it, he strode on up toward the hillside in long steps, and at the gate of the musician's house standing at the corner of a vacant lot — the house the neighborhood called the "Rose Mansion" — four or five women from nearby had gathered around the musician's young wife, whispering something together.

Hyō by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

But then — had she not, even at that moment, already known that the leopard had been shot dead hours before, near the Matsukaze Murasame-dō? Shōzō, feeling as though a chill had struck through him, bit down on his lip and rose to return to his room. And he understood, then, that it was not the shot beast, but something far, far closer to him — the thing that had been pressed against his own lips in the dark corridor — that had been the true form of the leopard. That very afternoon, Shōzō left the house in Suma and returned to Tokyo.
