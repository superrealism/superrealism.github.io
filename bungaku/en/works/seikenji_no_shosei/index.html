About this work

A lyrical essay in elegant classical prose that Takayama Chogyū wrote in 1901. Staying at Kiyomigata in Suruga to nurse his illness, the author listens to the bell of Seikenji sounding at midnight and sings feelings six years in the making. Contrasting the shifting human world with mountains and water that do not lie or change, and looking at his own ill fortune, it is full of the pathos of Chogyū in illness.

Summary

Waking in the middle of the night, the author hears the sound of the bell of Seikenji ringing out. Having spent six wasted years longing to hear this very bell, he resolves now to listen through the night, his thoughts running free. He recalls the days when, once before, illness had made him a stranger sojourning in this land — greeting morning and evening to the bell of Seikenji, weeping through the night as he gazed at the moon over the pine grove of Miho — and, though he longed to hear that bell again, the press of worldly affairs had kept him from it. But last autumn, struck once more by unexpected illness, he found himself wandering back to this land by the eastern sea, and at last, on a clear night, his long-held wish to pour out years of feeling to the sound of that bell is fulfilled. He reflects on the strange twist of fate that has kept this sickly body of his, meant to journey off toward the distant west, lingering instead among the mountains and rivers of his native land. The mountains and the water remain unchanging — without falsehood, without envy, without strife — and thinking of the human world, lost in life and death, turning through rise and fall, he feels the shame of his own condition all the more keenly. He reflects, too, on the folly and the pathos of a luckless body, soon to end as nameless, weathered bones, that still cannot bring itself to cast off life, and goes on, day after day, in its wandering. The bell rings out again and again, and his thoughts grow only deeper with each toll. The lingering echo of the final peal fades away, leaving behind only its plaintive resonance, and the author concludes that there is nothing but this very bell that can unravel the feeling that words themselves cannot carry. Once the year turns and he returns to the capital, he will no longer be able to hear the bell of Seikenji — and so he wishes only for one moon-bright night, to face the sea wind and let out one long, lingering cry.

The opening (original text)

Waking in the middle of the night, the sound of a bell rang out. And I thought — I am, after all, a wanderer at the foot of Seikenji. What a longed-for sound, this bell. Six springs and autumns I have spent in vain, longing to hear this very bell. How bittersweet, how strangely pleasant, is my own present state. Come, then — let me listen it through, just as my thoughts run. Autumn deepens, and the ten thousand mountains turn sallow and shed their leaves. I raise my pillow, and hear a sorrowful cry out in the fields. Ah, this sound of the bell — is it bidding my troubled breast to brood upon its cares? How am I to hear this sound, unearthly as it is? How strange — though I had no thought at all of brooding, before I knew it, tears were already running down my cheeks.

Seikenji no shōsei by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The final peal of the bell rose up. Its lingering echo carried far, leaving behind, wherever it reached, the resonance of a plaintive cry. Ah, this beloved sound of the bell — my many thoughts, too hard to rise to the words of men — who but this very bell could ever unravel them? Once the year has turned, I shall return to the capital. My thoughts will once more knot themselves within my breast. Waking in the middle of the night, I shall no longer be able to hear the bell of Seikenji. Even now I find myself thinking of it again and again. If only there were some way to speak out, in a single word, this feeling of mine. And so, on some moon-bright night yet to come, I mean to face the sea wind and let out one long, lingering cry. How much lighter, then, would my heart become.

Seikenji no shōsei by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)
