About this work

A short story published in Shin Seinen in 1926. A dwarf called Midori, attached to a travelling troupe, is mocked and humiliated by his fellows over drinks. Under his ingratiating smile he hides a crushed sense of shame and a dark passion, and the story draws his terrifying transformation. Under the moonlight, a black shape falls on something round: an unforgettable ending that freezes the reader. A grotesque piece.

Summary

At a drinking party of a traveling show troupe, the dwarf Midori-san is made the plaything of his drunken fellows. Called to in a gentle voice, the little man twists his big mouth and grins in his usual good-natured way; but beneath that cringing smile a dark, pent-up passion at his daily humiliations is whirling. As the troupe's cruel horseplay grows worse and worse, what has piled up in Midori-san's breast bursts out at last in a hideous shape. At the end of the story, under a moonlight like water, a black shadow carries the round thing like a watermelon that it has been holding up to its mouth with both hands, and stamping its feet, bites into it and lets go, bites and lets go, and dances on as though in the greatest delight. From the round thing, and from the shadow's own lips, thick black liquid can plainly be seen dripping, drop by drop. A piece of Ranpo's weird short fiction, drawing the dark passion of the downtrodden in a vivid grotesque image.

The opening (original text)

"Here, Midori-san, what are you moping about? Come over here and take a cup with the rest of us." A man in flesh-colored tights, wearing loincloth breeches of purple satin edged with gold thread, planted himself in front of the sake cask with its head knocked out and spoke in a strangely gentle voice. Since there was something meaning in his tone, the men and women of the company, who had had their minds on the drink, all looked toward Midori-san at once.

Odoru issunbōshi by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The man carried the round thing he had been holding up to his mouth with both hands. And stamping his feet, he bit into the thing like a watermelon. He let go and bit again, let go and bit again, and danced on as though in the greatest delight. A moonlight like water threw the shadow of that grotesque dance up jet black. And from the round thing in the man's hand, and from his own lips, thick black liquid could plainly be seen dripping, drop by drop.
