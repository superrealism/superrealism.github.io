What is Shinyō Shōsetsu (State-of-Mind Novel)?

Category
Form / Genre

A type of I-novel that places its main emphasis not on dramatic incident or frank confession but on the expression of the writer's own clear, settled state of mind as he confronts his everyday surroundings. In the literary establishment of the Taishō era it was prized as an ideal form of serious literature, in which the deepening of the author's own character became, in itself, the value of the work. Shiga Naoya's "Kinosaki nite" ("At Kinosaki," 1917), quietly contemplating the life and death of small creatures he observed while convalescing at a hot spring, is held up as the archetype. Kume Masao, in the essay "'Watakushi' Shōsetsu to 'Shinkyō' Shōsetsu" ("The 'I-Novel' and the 'State-of-Mind' Novel," 1925), argued that even the great works of Tolstoy and Dostoevsky were, in the end, nothing more than "magnificent popular novels," and that it was precisely the I-novel and state-of-mind novel, in which the author lays out the self just as it is, that formed the very essence of prose art — an argument that fixed this term firmly in the literary establishment. The lyric ennui of Satō Haruo's "Den'en no Yūutsu" ("Rural Melancholy," 1918), Kajii Motojirō's "Fuyu no Hae" ("Winter Flies," 1928), and Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's late "Shinkirō" ("Mirage," 1927) all carry this lineage of the state-of-mind novel forward as well.
