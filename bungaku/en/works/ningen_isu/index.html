About this work

One of Edogawa Ranpo's defining works of the grotesque and fantastic, published in the magazine Kuraku in September 1925. A chairmaker tormented by his own ugliness hides inside a chair of his own building to feel the warmth of the people who sit on it, and confesses the experience in a letter. Widely known as the representative work of Ranpo's "erotic-grotesque" vein.

Summary

Yoshiko, celebrated as a beautiful woman writer, receives one day a long letter from a man she does not know. In it a chairmaker, embittered and made a recluse by his own ugly face, describes how he hollowed out the inside of a splendid armchair built with all his skill, and began to live concealed within it. Savouring in secret the weight and warmth of the people who sat on him, sunk in that perverse pleasure, he followed the chair when the hotel sold it off, and made his way into the house of its purchaser — Yoshiko herself. Living beside her as an unseen housemate, he found his feelings growing past bearing, and he ends the letter by confessing that he has resolved to come out of the chair at last and show himself to her. As Yoshiko stares in terror at her own chair, a second letter arrives. It is a perfectly businesslike note from a contributor, explaining that the earlier letter was the manuscript of a story submitted for her opinion, and that he would like to give it the title "The Human Chair."

Characters

  • Yoshiko　A beautiful woman writer. The point-of-view character, who reads the letter that arrives from a stranger
  • The writer of the letter　A man who confesses that, tormented by the ugliness of his face, he lived hidden inside a chair

The opening (original text)

Every morning, once she had seen her husband off to the ministry — it was always past ten by then — Yoshiko would become her own person at last, and it was her custom to shut herself up in the study she shared with him in the Western wing. There she was now at work on a long piece for the enlarged summer number of the magazine K. As a beautiful woman writer she had of late become famous enough to cast her husband, a secretary in the Foreign Ministry, somewhat into the shade. Letters came to her every day, any number of them, from admirers she did not know. This morning too, seating herself at the desk in the study, she had first to look through those letters from strangers before she could begin her work.

The Human Chair by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Forgive me, I beg you, the impertinence of writing to you so suddenly. I am one who has long read your work with love. What I have sent under separate cover is a poor story of my own. If you would look at it and give me your opinion, I could ask no greater happiness. For certain reasons I posted the manuscript before writing this letter, so I presume you have already read it. How did you find it? If my poor work has made even a little impression on you, nothing could give me more joy. I deliberately left it off the manuscript, but I should like to give it the title "The Human Chair." And so, with apologies for my rudeness, I make my request. In haste.

The Human Chair by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Who could possibly imagine anything so absurd as a human being hiding inside a chair?

A line from a letter confessing to having taken up residence inside a chair. Depicting the abnormal lurking within an everyday piece of furniture, this story became synonymous with Ranpo's name.
