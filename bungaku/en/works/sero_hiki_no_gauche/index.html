About this work

One of Miyazawa Kenji's central children's stories, unpublished in his lifetime and brought out in 1934. Gauche, the worst cellist in the town orchestra and forever being scolded by the conductor, is visited night after night by animals; through these odd encounters he becomes, without noticing it, a far better player. A warm and much-loved story about music, nature and what we owe to others.

Summary

Gauche, who plays the cello at the town's picture house, is the worst player in the orchestra, and at the rehearsals for the coming concert the conductor scolds him without mercy. Smarting, Gauche goes home to the watermill house and practises alone late into the night. And night after night the animals come — a calico cat, a cuckoo, a young badger, a mother field mouse and her child — each with a request, and each getting Gauche to play. Angry and bewildered by turns, he goes along with them; and while he does, his playing improves visibly. At the concert itself, when he has finished, Gauche is called back for an encore, plays alone the piece he had played through that night, and is met with thunderous applause. Home again that night, he remembers the cuckoo he once shouted down, and quietly begs its pardon in his heart.

Characters

  • Gauche　A young man who plays the cello in the town orchestra. The protagonist, a poor player who improves through his dealings with the animals
  • The conductor　The orchestra's conductor, who scolds Gauche harshly for his poor playing
  • The calico cat, the cuckoo, the young badger, and the mother field mouse and her child　The animals who come to Gauche night after night, each with a request

The opening (original text)

Gauche was in charge of the cello at the town's picture house. But he had a reputation for not being very good. Not very good is putting it kindly: he was in fact the worst of all the players, and so the conductor was forever picking on him. That afternoon they were all sitting in a ring in the dressing room, rehearsing the Sixth Symphony for the town's coming concert. The trumpet was singing away for dear life.

Gauche the Cellist by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Late that night Gauche came home. He gulped down water again. Then he opened the window and, looking out at the far-off sky where he thought the cuckoo had flown that night, he said, "Ah, cuckoo. I am sorry about that. I was not really angry with you."

Gauche the Cellist by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Gauche played the cello at the moving-picture theater in town. But he had a reputation for not being very good at it.

An opening that introduces a poor cellist, forever being scolded by his conductor. The nightly visits and practice sessions with animals quietly begin to change him.

Ah, cuckoo. I'm sorry about back then. I wasn't really angry at you.

The closing, on the night his concert succeeds. His apology to the bird he once drove away, after making it endure his practice, quietly shows how far Gauche has come.
