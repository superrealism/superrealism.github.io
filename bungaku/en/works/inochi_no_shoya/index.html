About this work

A young man named Oda, told he has leprosy, enters a sanatorium, and the story draws the first night he spends there. Discovered by Kawabata Yasunari, published in Bungakukai and awarded the Bungakukai Prize, it is Hōjō Tamio's central work. "We are phoenixes" — the words Sagaki speaks from the bottom of despair sound not only for the sick but as a question about being alive at all.

Summary

Walking twenty minutes from the station through a copse, Oda comes to the hedge of the hospital. Ever since he was told of his disease half a year ago, he has been a man of twenty-three who looks at the branch of a tree and reckons whether it would bear his weight, and who remembers the names of sleeping draughts whenever he passes a pharmacy. When his admission is settled, the man who shows him round is Sagaki, an attendant. What Oda sees when he enters the ward is the severe cases with their faces fallen away. People living on with their noses gone, their eyes gone, their fingers gone. To Oda, standing frozen with horror, Sagaki says quietly: those people are no longer human beings; they are life itself. "We are phoenixes. When we have a new thought and a new eye, when we win the life of a leper completely, we shall rise again as human beings." That night, unable to sleep, Oda listens to the groaning of a bandaged patient and imagines over and over hanging himself with a rope. Sagaki too goes on writing his manuscript with blindness certainly approaching. Toward dawn the two go outside. In the cold air Sagaki said, "Even knowing perfectly well that I shall go blind, Mr. Oda, I shall go on writing all the same." To suffer, he says, takes a talent; there are those who cannot even suffer. A blazing sun appears beyond the wood, and stripes of light flow across the treetops. Whether he can reach Sagaki's world Oda still deeply doubts; but one must try living after all — thinking so with all his strength, he goes on watching the stripes of light.

Characters

  • Oda　A young man of twenty-three who, told he has leprosy, enters the sanatorium
  • Sagaki　An attendant, who goes on writing fiction with blindness approaching

The opening (original text)

Walking some twenty minutes through the copse after leaving the station, one begins to see the hedge of the hospital; and yet in between there were places dipping like valleys and long gentle slopes of small hills, and not one thing that looked like a human dwelling. It was hardly more than twenty miles from Tokyo, but there was a stillness as of deep mountains and the air of a place far from any village.

Inochi no shoya by Hōjō Tamio (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Anguish — it will follow you until you die, no doubt. But did not someone say that to suffer takes a talent? There are those who cannot even suffer." And Sagaki took one great breath, and even his walk, treading the earth firmly step by step, was full of an unshakable youth. Whether he could reach Sagaki's world Oda still deeply doubted; but one must try living after all — thinking so with all his strength, he went on watching the stripes of light.

Famous passages

They are not human beings, you know. They are life. Life itself — life, nothing but life.

Words spoken to Oda, newly admitted to the sanatorium, by the older patient Sagaki. The idea that even when one has perished as a human being one goes on living as life itself shows the heart of this work, regarded as the starting point of Hansen's disease literature.
