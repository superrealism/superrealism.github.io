About this work

An essay in which Kitaōji Rosanjin speaks about the essence of eating, taking the plain dish of tea poured over rice for his door. Taste in food, he says, divides greatly according to means, age and the surroundings one grew up in, and he takes tea over rice as the representative simple pleasure that a person tired of luxury suddenly wants. Arguing that what is good can only be understood through long habit, he says that true happiness in eating lies in honestly eating what one's own body asks for. Rosanjin's view of food shows well here.

Summary

Rosanjin argues that cooking differs as much as heaven from earth between those of ample means and those of scant means, but that there is a moment when even someone weary of luxury craves something simple and delicious. Tea over rice, sought at such a moment, has its own gradations, from nothing more than salt and tea poured over rice to sea bream chazuke, and he says it is only natural, and delicious, to eat honestly whatever the body happens to crave at the time — pickled radish, dried fish, eel, beef. Good cooking cannot be understood without long years of habit, and money spent on it does not make it comprehensible to just anyone — like calligraphy and painting, he says, it can only be understood by those who understand it. Further, he says that a person raised on expensive food craves it because of his upbringing, and, drawing the contrast between a Tokyoite who will pay for tuna and an Osakan who won't, concludes that one's taste in food should suit one's own station in life, and ought never to be forced or twisted out of shape.

The opening (original text)

It is not only true of tea over rice, but cooking is always as different as heaven from earth between someone of ample means and someone of scant means. And yet a person of ample means, who never lacks for a thing, whether sashimi or beef, whatever ingredient he pleases, sometimes grows weary of luxurious cooking and wants a meal of something simple and delicious. This is the time when the nutrition doctors speak of has filled the body to overflowing, and, physiologically, more nutrition has become unnecessary. It is at such a time that one wants to eat rice with tea poured over it.

Ochazuke no aji by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

And whether a person can eat only delicious food, or can eat nothing but poor food, is a matter of the environment he was raised in, and this should never be forced or twisted out of shape. It must suit one's own station. If it does not, there is no talking about food at all. One will end up unable to attain the happiness of good eating. Let this be preface enough; let us move on now to the matter at hand.

Ochazuke no aji by Kitaōji Rosanjin (Aozora Bunko)
