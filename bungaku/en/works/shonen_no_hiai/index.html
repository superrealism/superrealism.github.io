About this work

A short story Kunikida Doppo published in Shōtenchi in 1902, its title read as a child's sorrow. The narrator, who passed his boyhood at an uncle's house near an inlet on the Inland Sea, is taken one summer night at twelve by the manservant Tokujirō to a brothel by the harbour, and meets an unlucky young woman about to be sold to Korea. The deep sorrow of the world, touched for the first time in boyhood, sung by Doppo in rich poetry: a fine lyrical short story.

Summary

If the joy of boyhood is a kind of poem, then so too is the sorrow of boyhood — so begins a man recalling one of the griefs of his own childhood. From the age of eight to fifteen, "I" spent a happy boyhood running wild over the hills and fields at my uncle's house, near an inlet along the Inland Sea. One night when I was twelve, Tokujirō, a dark-skinned, high-spirited orphan of about twenty-five who worked as a manservant in my uncle's house, invited me along, saying, "Tonight I'll take you somewhere interesting." On a moonlit summer night, Tokujirō rowed me across the inlet, fragrant with the scent of rice plants, in a small boat, to a house of pleasure by the harbor. There, waiting for us, was a pale, listless young woman of nineteen or twenty, looking almost ill. She was soon to be taken away to Korea, and, hearing that I closely resembled the younger brother from whom she had been separated as a child, had asked Tokujirō to bring me to her so that she might see me just once. The woman rowed me out to sea in a small boat, and there, by moonlight, speaking of the brother she had lost, she broke down weeping, her voice hushed. "Please don't forget about me," she repeated again and again, as she sent me on my way. Seventeen years have passed since that night, and "I" have never been able to forget the sight of it. The thin mist of sorrow that wrapped itself around my heart that night has only deepened with the years. A lyrical masterpiece in which Doppo, with rich poetic feeling, sings of the deep sorrow of this world, first glimpsed in the days of boyhood.

Characters

  • I　A boy raised at his uncle's house by the Inland Sea. In the summer of his twelfth year, led by the manservant Tokujirō to a house of pleasure, he meets an unfortunate woman and comes to know a sorrow he can never forget
  • Tokujirō　A manservant at the uncle's house. A dark-skinned, high-spirited orphan, he takes "I" to see the woman
  • The drifting woman　A pale woman of nineteen or twenty, soon to be sold into Korea, who longs to see "I", who closely resembles the younger brother from whom she was separated

The opening (original text)

If the joy of boyhood is poetry, then the sorrow of boyhood is poetry too. If the joy that dwells within the heart of nature deserves to be sung, then surely the sorrow that whispers within the heart of nature deserves to be sung as well. In any case, I mean to try telling of one of the sorrows of my own boyhood. (So began the man who told this tale.) I was raised, from the age of eight to the age of fifteen, in my uncle's house, for in those days my father and mother were living in Tokyo.

Kodomo no kanashimi by Kunikida Doppo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Even now, seventeen years after that night, I remember the scene clearly, and try as I might to forget it, I cannot. Even now the poor woman's face still flickers before my eyes. And that fragment of sorrow which, like a thin mist, wrapped itself around my heart that night has only grown denser with the passing years, until now, merely calling to mind what I felt at that time is more than I can bear — a deep, quiet, helpless sorrow that will not leave me. Tokujirō, after that, became through my uncle's good offices a fine, respectable farmer, and is now the father of two children. As for the drifting woman, whether she wandered on, after crossing over to Korea, to end her fleeting life in some yet more distant place, or whether she has already left this world and gone instead to the quiet land of the dead, I do not know, of course, nor does Tokujirō seem to know either.
