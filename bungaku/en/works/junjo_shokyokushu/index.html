About this work

Hagiwara Sakutarō's second collection, published by Shinchōsha in August 1925. It brings together "Poems of Love and Pity," from his boyhood, and the ten newer "Poems in View of My Native Place," both cast as pure lyrics in a plaintive classical diction. From the elegant sweetness of the earlier group to the violence of the later, in which he lays his own gloom and anger over the scenery of his native Maebashi, it conveys the range of his work. "On a Journey" in particular — that he should like to go to France, but France is too far away — has been widely recited as a song of youthful longing and carried in school readers.

Summary

The "Poems of Love and Pity" gather the elegant, sweet lyricism of the poet's boyhood, from the time when Sakutarō was beginning to write, with something in them like the scent of iris perfume. "Night Train," "The Heart," "Setting Out," "Goldfish" and the rest stand side by side, colored with fresh feeling and a faint sensuousness; and of them "Setting Out" is the most famous, singing an unreachable longing for a distant France, and the wish at least to put on a new suit and go off on a journey as one pleases. The ten "Poems Looking on My Native Place" that make up the second half, by contrast, take Maebashi and its scenery — where the poet lived so long — as their subject, and cut into them his loneliness, his brooding, and a fierce anger at the people there. "Hagitei," "Ōwatari Bridge," "The Bench in the Park" and the others sing, on a violent literary rhythm, the bitter rebellion and homesickness of a man his home town will not receive. From sweet lyricism to a harsh assertion of self — the book holds the two faces of the poet Sakutarō in a single volume, and is an important collection of modern Japanese poetry.

The opening (original text)

Setting Out I should like to go to France, but France is far too far away. At least let me put on a new suit and set out on a journey as I please. When the train goes along the mountain road I will lean against the water-colored window and think, alone, of pleasant things, in the first light of a May morning, as my heart pleases, like young grass coming up.

Junjō shōkyokushū by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The Bench in the Park Leaning on a bench in the deserted park, what I think today is fierce again. Why are the people of my home town so hard on me, that I must bite the stone of a bitter plum? Snow shines on the far mountains of Echigo; does the barley too shake and tremble at men's anger? The voices that mock and laugh at me fill the fields and hills, and the cry of my suffering has burst my heart. So much — be gone, this clinging to what is heartless. Ah, tread the soil of the home town where you were born, and be gone from it. With a knife sharply whetted in my fingers, in the season of the leafing cherry, I carved on that lonely bench the word "Revenge."

Junjō shōkyokushū by Hagiwara Sakutarō (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

I long to go to France, but France is much too far.

The opening of “Journey.” Voicing a longing he knows he cannot fulfill, and resolving at least to board the train in a new suit, this is a light, celebrated line of colloquial free verse.
