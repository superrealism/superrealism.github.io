Famous passages by Yosano Akiko

Well-known lines from the works of Yosano Akiko.

8 passages

Tangled Hair

Never once have you touched this soft skin, this hot welling blood — do you not feel lonely, you who preach the Way?

A poem cast at a man who preaches morality without ever having known the warmth of young skin. Challenging old ethics head-on, it symbolizes the passion of the Myōjō school.

That girl of twenty—her black hair flowing through the comb, the pride of her springtime—how beautiful it is!

A poem that sees the proud beauty of youth in a twenty-year-old girl's black hair, flowing through the comb. Boldly affirming youth and one's own beauty, it represents Tangled Hair.

Crossing Gion on the way to Kiyomizu on a night of cherry blossoms and moon—everyone I meet tonight is beautiful.

A poem on the way to Kiyomizu through Gion in Kyoto on a moonlit night of cherry blossoms. To a heart full of the joy of love, everyone she passes looks beautiful.

Somehow feeling that you were waiting for me, I stepped out — into a field of autumn flowers under the evening moon.

A poem from Tangled Hair. Feeling somehow that her lover is waiting, she steps out into a field of flowers with the evening moon above. One of Akiko's representative poems, melting the premonition of love into a scene of the autumn fields.

In the third month of spring, the koto without its bridges gave out a sound — touched, by chance, by my tangled hair of the evening.

A poem that includes the words “tangled hair,” echoing the title of the collection. A koto without its bridges sounds when her loosened hair brushes against it by chance: the allure of a spring evening is drawn through the feel of a sound.

Akiko shihen zenshū

Ah, my brother, I weep for you, you must not go and die. Born the last of us, you were loved best by our parents — but did they raise you, put a blade into your hand, and teach you to kill? Did they raise you for twenty-four years telling you to kill and then to die?

The first stanza of a poem addressed to her younger brother, sent to fight at Port Arthur in the Russo-Japanese War. Posing the question, “Did they teach you to kill?”, it is a celebrated poem that defied the mood of its time.

The day comes when the mountains move. Though I say so, no one believes it. The mountains have only been sleeping a while; long ago, they all burned with fire and moved. But you need not believe that. O people, ah, believe only this: all the women who were sleeping are now awakening and beginning to move.

The opening part of the poem sequence “Idle Words,” contributed to the first issue of Seitō, the magazine founded by Hiratsuka Raichō and others in 1911. Likening to a mountain the day when women who had been sleeping awaken and begin to move, it is still quoted as a declaration of women's liberation.

Would that I might write only in the first person — I, a lonely woman in a corner. Would that I might write only in the first person — I, I.

A poem from the sequence “Idle Words,” following “The Day the Mountains Move.” The wish to write only in the first person expresses a woman's will to independence — to speak of herself in her own words.
