About this work

A gambler's road story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in King in 1936. On the Kōshū road in early spring, at the Sasago pass, the wanderer Inosuke stakes his life for obligation and feeling. Washing Shōbei's blood with his own and clearing the way for Hantarō and Onobu before he falls, his single-hearted way of living is drawn against the scattering blossom of the peach.

Summary

Under a sky clear and sharp with a pale indigo light, even the slow-coming spring of the Kōshū road cannot deny the light of a March day; along the embankment the knotweed is putting out its first shoots, and on the rolling hills the vermilion of peach blossoms and the green of the wheat are bright to the eye. On the road leading from the post town of Ōtsuki toward the Sasago pass, at a teahouse on the pass itself — there the story of the wanderer Inosuke begins. Caught between obligation and feeling, Inosuke sets out to settle a certain matter of fate. To cleanse the bond between the young couple Hantarō and Onobu, and to sever the blood tie bound up with Shōbei, he throws away his own life. "I've washed Shōbei's blood with my own — that ought to cleanse things properly between Hantarō and Onobu now. All that's left is to beg Shōbei's pardon in the next world." With these words spoken, Inosuke pitches forward and breathes his last. Outside the window, in the night mist, peach blossoms scattered soundlessly, petal by petal. A piece of Yamamoto's wandering-gambler stories, drawing the single-minded way of life of a wanderer who throws away his life for obligation, against the falling peach blossoms.

The opening (original text)

A sky clear and sharp with a pale indigo light. Even the slow-coming spring of the Kōshū road cannot deny the light of a March day; along the embankment the knotweed is putting out its vigorous first shoots, and in the farmhouses' back fields, on the rolling hills, the vermilion of peach blossoms and the green of the wheat are bright to the eye. Even the cry of the wagtail, skimming low over the white gravel bed of the Sasago river, sounds peaceful and unhurried.

Burai wa utazu by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"I'm grateful. I've washed Shōbei's blood with my own — that ought to cleanse things properly between Hantarō and Onobu now. All that's left is to beg Shōbei's pardon in the next world." With these words spoken, Inosuke pitched forward and breathed his last. Outside the window, in the night mist, peach blossoms scattered soundlessly, petal by petal.
