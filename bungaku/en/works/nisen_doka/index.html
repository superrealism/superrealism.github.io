About this work

Edogawa Ranpo's debut, a short detective story published in a special number of the magazine Shin Seinen in April 1923. Taken as the first genuine detective story written by a Japanese author, it is the point from which his career begins. Its strengths are a trick built on braille and cipher-breaking, and a reversal waiting at the end.

Summary

"I" and Matsumura Takeshi, two poor young men renting a room over a clog shop on the edge of town, are so hard up that they can hardly keep themselves. A gentleman thief who had carried off an electrical company's payroll had been caught, but where the fifty thousand yen had gone was still entirely unknown. One day Matsumura's eye falls on a two-sen copper coin on "I"'s desk, and he demands to know where it came from. In fact the coin is a hollow container, and inside was a coded message using braille, by which the thief was to tell an accomplice where the money was hidden. Borrowing the knowledge of a blind masseur, Matsumura deciphers the code and, sure that he has found where the fifty thousand yen is, sets off in high spirits. But "I" points out that the deciphered text is itself a second code hiding another meaning altogether, and produces a completely different answer. Aghast, Matsumura opens his bag and finds nothing but a bundle of toy banknotes. The whole thing was an elaborate practical joke played by "I," who had been matching wits with him.

Characters

  • I　A poor young man. The narrator, who plays an elaborate practical joke on his friend Matsumura
  • Matsumura Takeshi　"I"'s friend, who sets about deciphering the code in the two-sen copper coin

The opening (original text)

"I envy that thief." We were badly enough off that such words passed between us. This is a story of the days when Matsumura Takeshi and I lay about in the single six-mat room over a poor clog shop on the edge of town, with two battered lacquered-paper desks side by side, doing nothing but working up strange fancies.

The Two-Sen Copper Coin by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Only, what I feared most in this performance was not these dramatic aspects but one very practical point, trifling in the whole and with a touch of the comic about it: whether the toy notes I had seen would still be at the printer's, undelivered, when Matsumura went to collect them. About the price of the toys I had no anxiety at all. My relative and the Daikokuya did business on credit, and, better still, the Shōjikidō ran its affairs in a thoroughly primitive and loose way, so that Matsumura could not have failed even without bringing a receipt from the master of the Daikokuya. Finally, as to the two-sen copper coin that was the starting point of his trick, I regret that I must avoid a detailed explanation here. If I write something clumsy, the person who gave me the thing may afterwards come to unexpected trouble. The reader need only suppose that I happened to have it.

The Two-Sen Copper Coin by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

“I envy that thief.” Things had grown so desperate between the two of them that words like these were exchanged.

The opening of the work regarded as the starting point of orthodox Japanese detective fiction. A conversation that begins as a lament over poverty rolls on into a battle of wits over a cipher.
