About this work

The title story of Miyazawa Kenji's collection The Restaurant of Many Orders, subtitled "Ihatov Tales," the only book of his stories published in his lifetime, brought out at something close to his own expense in Morioka in December 1924. Two hunters, lost deep in the mountains, wander into a strange Western-style restaurant. Light and comic in the telling, it is a stinging piece of satire aimed at people who hold nature cheap.

Summary

Two young gentlemen got up exactly like English soldiers set off deep into the mountains to hunt with their dogs, but find neither game nor their way, and their dogs collapse into the bargain. Hungry and uneasy, they come upon a fine Western-style building — the Wildcat House, Western Restaurant — and go in delighted. Inside, door follows door, each with a written order posted on it: tidy your hair, leave your guns, take off anything metal. Thinking it a restaurant of a great many orders indeed, the two obey meekly, sure that they are being welcomed as guests. But the orders on the further doors grow steadily more sinister, and when they are told to rub cream all over themselves the two realize at last, shaking, that this is no place for entertaining guests but a trap laid by wildcats who mean to eat them. At the last possible moment their lost dogs come bursting in and drive the wildcats off, and the two escape with their lives. They come down the mountain with the hunter who has finally come to find them; but their faces, crumpled once like waste paper in their terror, never went back to what they had been, even after they returned to Tokyo.

Characters

  • The two gentlemen　Young men who have come hunting got up like Western soldiers. The protagonists, who stray into the Wildcat House
  • The wildcats　They pose as the proprietors of a Western restaurant and scheme to eat the two men

The opening (original text)

Two young gentlemen, got up exactly like English soldiers, with glittering rifles on their shoulders and two dogs like white bears at their heels, were walking deep in the mountains where the fallen leaves rustled, and this is what they were saying. "The mountains hereabouts are a disgrace. Not one bird, not one animal. I don't care what it is, I should like to let off a bang at something soon." "Two or three shots into the yellow flank of a deer would be splendid. Round and round it would go, and then down it would come with a thud."

The ending (original text)

At that the two were relieved at last. They ate the dumplings the hunter had brought, and on the way back bought ten yen's worth of pheasant and returned to Tokyo. But their faces, which had crumpled up like waste paper that once, never went back to what they had been — not in Tokyo, not in the bath, not ever.

Famous passages

Two young gentlemen, dressed up exactly like English soldiers, shouldering gleaming rifles, and leading two dogs as white as polar bears, were walking deep in the mountains, over a stretch of rustling fallen leaves, saying things like this.

An opening that begins with the figures of two gentlemen who make light of the mountain. It opens an ironic children's story in which the one who “orders” and the one who is ordered eventually change places.

But their faces, which had once crumpled up like paper, never went back to how they had been — not even after they returned to Tokyo, not even after a hot bath.

The final line, which coldly casts off the two men, saved and back out of the mountain. Only the single fact that their terror-twisted faces never recover again remains, as their punishment.

“Please, everyone, do come in. There is no need to hold back.”

The first of the notices posted at the entrance of the Wildcat House. The two gentlemen read it in the way that suits them and rejoice, but on a second reading a hidden meaning appears. This wordplay sets the story's trap in motion.
