About this work

The first work of Kitamura Tōkoku, written at twenty: a long poem in sixteen stanzas giving the monologue of a young man arrested as a political offender and held in a prison cell. It sings the despair of a prisoner deprived of freedom, his thoughts of the girl he loves best, and his hope of meeting her again, in a fresh prosody learned from the European Romantics. A landmark announcing the dawn of modern Japanese poetry.

Summary

As a champion of the movement for popular rights, devoted to affairs of state, "I" am seized as a political criminal who has broken the law, and imprisoned together with comrades who had sworn to share life and death with me, and with the girl I love best, still young as an unopened bud. Shut away in a cell cut off from the world by double walls, my hair grown wild, my body wasted thin, my voice gone hoarse — the eloquence and keen vision that once moved the world are lost to me now, and I, become a prisoner, think of the fate of my own house, whose grandfather and father alike fell for the sake of the nation. Only a thin shaft of sunlight slipping in, a crack in the wall, and a single nightingale that steals into the cell bring any comfort to my heart. Seeing the changed, wasted forms of the comrades imprisoned with me in the same jail, and anxious for the girl in the next cell, "I" sink into a despair so deep that I come even to long for death. But at the story's close, the jailer appears and announces a general amnesty, and "I" am at last set free. Stepping through the gate, many friends come to greet me, and my bride, running to me, takes my hand, and we weep together; and that nightingale, too, plays its beautiful song once more. Condensing the anguish of captivity, the thirst for freedom, and the joy of release into sixteen stanzas, this narrative poem — self-published by the twenty-year-old Tōkoku — was to exert enormous influence on later poets such as Shimazaki Tōson, and stands as a pioneering masterpiece of modern Japanese poetry.

Characters

  • I　A young man imprisoned as a political criminal. Leader of the champions of the movement for popular rights
  • The girl (the bride)　The one "I" love best. Imprisoned together with him, the two worry over each other's fate in the cell
  • The comrade-champions　Fellow prisoners jailed together with "I". Men who once held sway across the land

The opening (original text)

One Once, in error, I broke the law, 　　and was seized as a criminal of state; among the many stalwarts who swore 　　life and death with me, I was their chief; 　　and among them, too, my most beloved, 　　a girl still a bud unopened, 　　was taken as well, all for the country's sake — 　　both this bridegroom and this bride.

Soshū no shi by Kitamura Tōkoku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Sixteen The nightingale forsook me and was gone, and I sank once more into gloom and melancholy; how might it be, this spring, within the capital? Surely, surely, the capital stands now in bloom! 　Even as such fancies filled my mind, after a long while, the jailer came in at last. And I was set free at last, and gave thanks for the great mercy of the amnesty. Stepping through the gate, many friends 　had gathered there, and came to greet me; and among them, my most beloved bride 　came running, and seized my hand; in her eyes, and in mine, the very same tears fell — 　and the many friends, rejoicing, danced; and that dear nightingale of before came too, once more, 　and let its beautiful song be heard by all.

Soshū no shi by Kitamura Tōkoku (Aozora Bunko)
