About this work

A tale of the Heian court drawn from the anecdotes about Heichū — Taira no Sadafun — and his amorousness as they are handed down in the "Konjaku Monogatarishū" and the "Uji Shūi Monogatari." It opens by setting out passages from three classical texts and then drawing Heichū in the manner of an appraisal of a figure in a picture scroll. The most celebrated lover of his day, of whom it was said that there was no woman he could not win, can make no headway at all with one lady-in-waiting called Jijū, and in his desperation goes so far as to steal away her close-stool box. Borrowing a comic tale from the classics, Akutagawa here draws an obsession with beauty and the breaking of it.

Summary

Under a lacquered cap fit for an age of peace, a white face full at the jaw — the Heichū of the painted scroll has skin like rice cake with the color of blood showing through, and in his narrow eyes a bright smile always floating. He was the greatest lover of his time, of whom it was said that there could not be a woman in court service, nor even a daughter of a house, whom he did not visit in secret. And this Heichū met at last with one he could not win. She was a lady-in-waiting known as the Honin Jijū; she returned nothing that could be called an answer to his letters, and when a reply did come at last it was nothing but a corner torn from his own letter, with the one word "seen" written on it. When he did contrive to meet her he was put off; when she opened the door to him on a rainy night, he was simply left there. Longing turns into obsession, and Heichū decides at last that since even this woman is human, the sight of that unclean thing must surely cool his fever, and steals away Jijū's close-stool box. What he sees when he lifts the lid is a thing made of incense, scented with aloeswood and cloves. Understanding that his design had been seen through, Heichū groans, "Jijū! You have killed Heichū!" and falls to the floor, and in his half-dead eyes there floats the figure of Jijū smiling upon him, ringed in a halo of gold.

Characters

  • Heichū (Taira no Sadafun)　A nobleman reputed the greatest lover of his day
  • Jijū　A lady-in-waiting in service at the Honin, who turns aside every one of Heichū's approaches
  • Jijū's page girl　The girl Heichū asks to act as his go-between

The opening (original text)

1. The Painted Figure Under an elegant, glossy lacquered cap fit for an age of peace, a face full at the jaw is looking this way. If a fresh red shows in those plump cheeks, it is not that rouge has been blended in; it is that a skin like rice cake, rare in a man, lets the color of the blood through of itself. Of mustache, under a nose of good breeding — or rather to the left and right of thin lips — there remains only the barest trace, as though it had been brushed on in thin ink. But on the glossy hair at his temples even the color of a sky without a trace of haze is faintly reflected in blue. Of the ear, at the edge of that hair, only the slightly lifted lobe can be seen; and if it has the warm color of a clam shell, that seems to be a trick of the faint light. The eyes are narrower than most men's, and in them a smile floats continually.

The ending (original text)

Heichū tilted the box and quietly tried a sip of the water. The water too was without doubt the clear part of a broth in which cloves had been boiled down. "Then is this thing aloeswood as well?" Heichū bit into the piece some two inches long that he had just picked up. There was a sweetness in it mixed with a bitterness sharp enough to go through his teeth. And on top of that his mouth was all at once full of a fine fragrance, cooler than the blossom of the orange. Jijū — from what had she guessed it? — had made an imitation out of worked incense in order to break Heichū's design. "Jijū! You have killed Heichū!" Groaning so, Heichū let the lacquered box fall with a clatter. And there on the floor he pitched forward full length. While in his half-dead eyes there floated the figure of Jijū, ringed still in a halo of purple gold, smiling charmingly upon him…. (September 1921)
