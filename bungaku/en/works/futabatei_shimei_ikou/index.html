About this work

Futabatei Shimei, who refused to be counted a man of letters at all, saying that literature always carries an element of play. Uchida Roan, having sorted the posthumous papers for his collected works, looks in this short essay at the conflict of a man who suffered all his life from the collision of Eastern thought with modern thought, and who understood the modern without ever quite becoming a modern man.

Summary

On the completion of the collected works of Futabatei Shimei and the memorial gathering held for him, Uchida Roan, who put the posthumous papers in order, considers in a short essay why Futabatei called himself all his life "no man of letters." Taking as his clue Futabatei's saying that "literature always carries with it some element of play, so that one can never give oneself to it to the death," he reads that loathing of the "element of play" back to the empty literature of the East — Saikaku and Kyōden and the rest — and further to the Confucian thought soaked into him from childhood. Futabatei knew Western literature well and understood modern thought fully, and yet could not shake off the bent of Eastern thought; and the struggle of these two opposing ways of thinking never left his head. It was for this reason, Roan holds, that in his last years he made light of Turgenev, over whom he had once been in raptures, as mere fancy, and mocked Tolstoy as an old man talking in his sleep. That dissatisfaction reached not only to literature but to politics and to business and to his household; and above all it was dissatisfaction with himself that was the greatest, so that his life was a history of this struggle and this anguish. Setting it beside the lives of Kunikida Doppo and Kitamura Tōkoku, he places Futabatei's life as a "history of modern torment and hardship" with still more meaning in it.

Characters

  • Futabatei Shimei　Properly Hasegawa Tatsunosuke. He called himself "no man of letters" and gave up on literature, and suffered all his life from the conflict of Eastern thought with modern thought
  • Uchida Roan　The author, who as one of the editors of the collected works put the posthumous papers in order
  • Tsubouchi Shōyō　A man of letters who associated with Futabatei, spoken of as touched by the same kind of burden of thought
  • Shimamura Hōgetsu　Cited as a man who, while holding the most modern attitude of all, carried the same sort of struggle

The opening (original text)

Now that the collected works of Futabatei Shimei are complete and a memorial gathering has been held for him by his friends, we who as one of the editors put his posthumous writings in order cannot but feel much upon it. As to Futabatei's calling himself all his life "no man of letters," the inner meaning of it may be various; but in short, that he himself said plainly at a certain gathering, "Literature always carries some element of play with it, so that one can never give oneself to literature to the death," must be one strong reason for it. But does literature indeed always carry an element of play with it?

Futabatei shimei (ikou) by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Among the men of letters of Meiji, the life of Mr. Kunikida Doppo was interesting. The life of Mr. Kitamura Tōkoku too is of the greatest interest. But compared with these two, Futabatei's life was a history of modern torment and hardship with still more meaning in it.

Futabatei shimei (ikou) by Uchida Roan (Aozora Bunko)
