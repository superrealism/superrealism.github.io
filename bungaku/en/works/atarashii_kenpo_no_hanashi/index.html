About this work

A booklet the Ministry of Education issued in 1947, to coincide with the coming into force of the Constitution of Japan, as a social studies textbook for first-year middle-school pupils. It explains popular sovereignty, the renunciation of war and fundamental human rights in plain language, addressing the reader directly. The passage in the chapter on the renunciation of war, praising as a very fine thing a constitution that has decided to hold no armed force at all, is well known. Its use as a textbook ended within a few years, but it has been reprinted again and again as a model introduction to the constitution.

Summary

"Everyone, a new constitution has been made" — with that address this small textbook begins. If we compare the country to a house, it explains, the constitution is the most important set of rules, the pillars; and two things are written in it: how the country is to be governed, and the "fundamental human rights" of the people. The following chapters explain that democracy means the whole people governing the country; that sovereignty has passed from the Emperor to the people; that the Emperor has become the symbol of the State of Japan and of the unity of the people; and that war is renounced. "In this constitution two things have been decided so that the country of Japan shall never go to war a second time. One of them is that we shall have no soldiers, no warships, no aeroplanes — nothing at all for making war." That, the children are told, does not put the country in danger; it is doing the right thing ahead of the world. From there it goes on to fundamental human rights, the parts played by the Diet, the Cabinet and the courts, finance, local self-government and the procedure for amendment; and in the last chapter, "The Supreme Law," it confirms that fundamental human rights are eternal rights that can never be violated hereafter. And it closes with the appeal: "Everyone, the new constitution is a constitution made by the Japanese people, a constitution of the Japanese people. From now on, let us keep this constitution and make the country of Japan flourish."

The opening (original text)

Everyone, a new constitution has been made. And from the third of May in the twenty-second year of Shōwa we, the people of Japan, are to keep this constitution. A great many people took great pains to make this new constitution. Now, do you know what a constitution is? Is there anyone who thinks it is something that has nothing to do with him? If so, that is a great mistake.

Atarashii kenpō no hanashi by Ministry of Education (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This constitution, which is so important, is kept by His Majesty the Emperor as well, and the Ministers of State, the members of the Diet and the judges all have the duty of keeping it. It was also decided in the constitution that the promises the country of Japan has made with other countries (these are called "treaties"), and the rules that have grown up for countries in their dealings with one another (these are called "international law"), shall be kept by the country of Japan in good faith. Everyone, the new constitution is a constitution made by the Japanese people, a constitution of the Japanese people. From now on, let us keep this constitution and make the country of Japan flourish.

Atarashii kenpō no hanashi by Ministry of Education (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

But none of you need feel the least bit uneasy. Japan has done what is right ahead of any other country. There is nothing in the world as strong as what is right.

A passage from the chapter “The Renunciation of War” in the textbook the Ministry of Education issued for junior high school students when the new constitution came into force. It speaks gently to children, telling them they need not feel uneasy about giving up armed forces.
