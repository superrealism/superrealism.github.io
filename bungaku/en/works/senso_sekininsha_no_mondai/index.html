About this work

An essay written from his sickbed by the film director Itami Mansaku the year after the defeat. Against calls to purge those responsible for the war in the film world, he argues that it is rather the people themselves — all saying with one voice that they were deceived — who must be asked to answer. To be deceived is itself a kind of sin, and a people who have lost the power of criticism will repeat the same mistake. Eighty years on, still read as one of the central acts of self-examination in postwar Japan.

Summary

He has heard that the people of the Free Film People's League are naming those responsible for the war in the film world and urging that they be purged, and that his own name is among the advocates — beginning so, Itami Mansaku confesses that no question is harder for him to understand than this one. Many people say that in this war they were deceived. But not one has said, "It was I who deceived you." The civilians say they were deceived by the military and the officials; the military and the officials say that they in turn were deceived from above. Trace it back and by the reckoning only one or two are left at the end, and one or two could never have deceived a hundred million. In other words, he points out, the whole Japanese people were frantically deceiving and being deceived by one another. During the war, what most directly tormented us were the head of the neighborhood association, the small shopkeeper, the petty official, the schoolteacher — were they not our own countrymen close at hand? And yet everyone believes that he alone deceived nobody. And then Itami comes to the heart of it: what is dangerous is precisely the attitude that moves oneself unconditionally into a position of no responsibility with the single word "I was deceived." Those who were deceived are guilty too — guilty of not judging things for themselves, of having thrown away their power of criticism. So long as this unawareness and irresponsibility of the people is not corrected, the Japanese will do the same thing again — and with that warning, sent out from his sickbed, this short essay closes.

The opening (original text)

Lately I have heard that the people of the Free Film People's League are naming those responsible for the war in the film world and urging that they be purged, and that my own name is among the advocates. I have not yet heard in detail when or in what form this was published, but people who have seen it have begun coming to me and asking whether that is really my opinion. So I should like to take this chance to state my real opinion on the question and make my position clear; but the truth is that for me no question of late has been harder to understand than this one.

Sensō sekininsha no mondai by Itami Mansaku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Now, many people say that in this war they were deceived. Every one of them says with one voice that he was deceived. So far as I know, there is not yet a single person who has said, "It was I who deceived you." It is about here that things begin, little by little, to become unintelligible. Many people seem to think that the distinction between the deceivers and the deceived is perfectly clear, but that, it appears, is in fact an illusion.

Sensō sekininsha no mondai by Itami Mansaku (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

And not content with pointing out that those who were deceived are not necessarily in the right, I wish to go a step further and assert that “being deceived is in itself already an evil.”

A passage written by the film director Itami Mansaku in the year after Japan's defeat, against the mood of escaping responsibility by saying “we were deceived.” Declaring that being deceived is itself an evil, it asks each person to face their own responsibility.
