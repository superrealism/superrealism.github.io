About this work

The note Akutagawa left just before he killed himself. In July 1927, at thirty-five, he set down as plainly as he could the psychology of a suicide, in the form of a letter to an old friend. He gives as the motive for his death the famous phrase — only a vague unease — and looks coldly at how one may die without pain. A posthumous document that has its place in the history of modern Japanese literature.

Summary

No one has yet written the psychology of a suicide as it actually is — so Akutagawa begins. In this last letter, addressed to "a certain old friend," he wishes to convey that psychology plainly. The world looks for the motive of suicide in hardship of living, in the pain of illness, in mental suffering; but according to Akutagawa that is not the whole of the motive. In his own case it is "only a vague uneasiness" — only a vague uneasiness about his future. Having thought of nothing but dying for the last two years or so, he sets down calmly, and with a cruel honesty, how he examined the question of how to die without suffering; how he rejected hanging and drowning and throwing himself under a train out of an "aesthetic disgust" and came to choose death by drugs; and even his consideration of the place of his suicide and of his family. "What I live in now is a world of sick nerves, transparent as ice" — looking at the pitifulness of human beings who live in order to live, and speaking of the beauty of nature as it appears to the eye of a dying man, Akutagawa walks on toward his own death. A famous posthumous manuscript, announcing one ending of modern Japanese literature.

The opening (original text)

No one has yet written the psychology of a suicide as it actually is. That must be owing to the suicide's pride, or to a lack of psychological interest in himself. In the last letter I send to you I want to convey this psychology plainly. ... But at least in my case it is only a vague uneasiness. Only a vague uneasiness about something in my future.

The ending (original text)

A note. Reading the life of Empedocles, I felt how old is the desire to make a god of oneself. My manuscript, so far as I am conscious of it, makes no god of me. No — it makes me one of the commonest of the common. You will remember the time twenty years ago when we argued about "Empedocles on Etna" under that linden tree. In those days I was one of those who wanted to make a god of himself. (July, the second year of Shōwa. Posthumous manuscript)

Famous passages

In my own case, at least, it is only a vague unease. Some vague unease about my future, and nothing more.

A line from a memoir entrusted to a friend just before his suicide. Written as an answer to the question of why he chose to die, it has been read ever since as words that captured the mood of intellectuals at the end of the Taishō era, reaching beyond any one man's private reasons.

Only, to one such as I am now, nature is more beautiful than ever. You will laugh at my contradiction — loving the beauty of nature and yet intending to kill myself. But nature is beautiful because it is reflected in my dying eyes.

A passage near the end of the note in which he gave “a vague unease” as the motive for his suicide. The idea of “dying eyes,” to which nature appears all the more beautiful in the face of death, is also known from Kawabata Yasunari's essay of the same name.
