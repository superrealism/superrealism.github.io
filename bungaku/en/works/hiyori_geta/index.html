About this work

Nagai Kafū's central collection of essays, serialized in Mita Bungaku from August 1914 into the following year, subtitled "A Record of Walks in Tokyo." Kafū, unusually tall, puts on fair-weather clogs, takes a western umbrella and walks the city without a destination, setting down under subject headings — obscure shrines, trees, maps, temples, water in rivers and canals, alleys, cliffs, slopes, sunsets, views of Fuji — the traces of a vanishing Edo and the townscape being torn up by modernization. The summit of his walking literature, which finds beauty in back streets and the life of the outskirts rather than in famous sights, and a classic that greatly influenced later writing on the city.

Summary

"Being unusually tall, I always walk in fair-weather clogs and carry a bat umbrella" — Kafū, who trusts nothing of Tokyo's changeable sky, carries his clogs and umbrella against a sudden shower and wanders the city with no destination in mind. Roadside shrines, trees, maps, temples, water in rivers and canals, back alleys, cliffs, slopes, sunsets, and the view of Fuji — Kafū sets a theme for his rambles chapter by chapter, cherishing the traces of Edo left in back streets and the outskirts, and mourning the cityscape daily broken up and set in iron bridges, asphalt and cement. Not the sightseeing that prizes famous places, but Kafū's eye, which finds beauty precisely in shabby side streets and life at the foot of a cliff, sets down in fine detail the vanishing face of Tokyo. At once a piece of civilization criticism turning its back on the modern city, and running through with an inexhaustible affection for the everyday townscape, it is the summit of Tokyo rambling literature.

The opening (original text)

Being unusually tall, I always walk in fair-weather clogs and carry a bat umbrella. However fine the day, I cannot feel at ease without my clogs and umbrella. This is because I place no trust whatsoever in Tokyo's weather, damp as it is throughout the year.

Hiyori geta by Nagai Kafū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

From the view of Fuji I chance to recall a parting verse of Sozan, and, filled with sorrow, cannot stop thinking of him.

Hiyori geta by Nagai Kafū (Aozora Bunko)
