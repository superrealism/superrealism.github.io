What is Yukar?

Category
Form / Genre

Oral literature handed down and told by the Ainu people. In a culture without writing, it was passed down chanted to a melody, and divides into the kamuy yukar (sacred narrative songs), in which a god narrates its own experience in the first person; the yukar (heroic epics), singing of a hero's adventures; and the uwepeker, prose folk tales. The kamuy yukar has a refrain called the "sakehe," and the distinctive rhythm of the form arises from repeating this refrain with the main text interspersed between its recurrences. The spelling "yūkara" spread through the work of the linguist Kindaichi Kyōsuke and others who introduced it, though in recent years it is also written closer to the original pronunciation as "yukar." Chiri Yukie's "Ainu Shin'yōshū" ("A Collection of Ainu Kamuy Yukar"), published in 1923, transcribed in Roman letters thirteen sacred narrative songs told by her grandmother and others and translated them herself into Japanese; it is celebrated as the first book of Ainu literature compiled by an Ainu hand. Together with its preface, beginning "Long ago, this vast Hokkaido was a free land for our ancestors," it conveys to us today a vanishing language and way of life. Yukie died at nineteen, the day after she completed the manuscript.
