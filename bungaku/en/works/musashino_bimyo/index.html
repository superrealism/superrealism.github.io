About this work

A historical novel by Yamada Bimyō, a pioneer of the unified spoken-written style, serialized in the Yomiuri Shimbun in 1887. In the disorder of the Northern and Southern Courts, on the Musashino plain, it draws the tragedy of a family caught up in the defeat and death of Nitta Yoshioki. It made a pioneering trial of the colloquial style in the narrative passages, using the plain copula, while giving the characters' dialogue an archaic diction mixed with the slang of the Ashikaga and Keichō eras, and survives in literary history as a landmark that helped establish the modern Japanese colloquial style. Another feature is the fine prose in which the desolate withered fields and battle sites of Musashino are drawn in richly coloured figures.

Summary

In the turbulent age of the Northern and Southern Courts, two warriors of the Nitta faction hurry across the desolate winter moors of Musashino, bound for Kamakura: the elder Chichibu Minbu, and his son-in-law, the young warrior Serada Saburō. Unaware that their lord, Nitta Yoshioki, has already been struck down through Ashikaga treachery at the Yaguchi Ferry, the two press on — until what had looked like horses turned loose on the moor reveals itself to be a troop of mounted Ashikaga warriors. Told of Yoshioki's death and surrounded on every side, the two men are cut down in a rain of blades. Back home, in a mountain village of Chichibu, Minbu's wife and her daughter Oshimo — who is also Saburō's wife — wait anxiously for word of the father and husband who have gone off to war. Rumor reaches them that Yoshioki has been killed and that not one of his men survived, and Oshimo's heart grows uneasy. Her mother urges her not to weep like a woman, as befits a warrior's wife, but Oshimo cannot hold back her tears as she gazes at the dagger her husband left behind as a keepsake. That night, unable to bear it any longer, Oshimo secretly slips from the house, halberd, armor, and dagger in hand. The next morning, when her mother discovers her gone, an unfamiliar warrior named Ōuchi Heita comes calling. Heita tells of how Minbu and Saburō were cut down on the moor of Musashino — and further, of a young woman he came upon in the mountains, run down by beasts, her legs torn open, lying dead. It was, of course, Oshimo, who had gone out in search of her father and husband. Having lost husband, father, and daughter within so short a span, the mother can only stare, wide-eyed, unable to speak a single word. And then, from beyond the gate, a clamor of voices draws near: "Lady Oshimo has been eaten by a bear!" A tragedy of an unknown family swallowed without mercy by the chaos of war, sung out in Bimyō's pioneering genbun'itchi — the unified language of speech and writing.

The opening (original text)

Part One Since this Musashino is a tale of a bygone age, without precedent as yet, I have written the speech of its characters in a particular style of my own. This manner of speech mixes the common language of the Keichō era with that of the Ashikaga era, and so it should, on the whole, suit that age well enough. Ah, the Tokyo of today — the Musashino of old! Today, so crowded that not even an awl could find room to stand; in the old days, so vast that no barrier gate could enclose it. The crow that today gets glared at by idle pleasure-seekers in Naka-no-chō once scraped out its living, in those days, in a fishing village a mere four or five blocks from the shore. The moon that today is worshipped by beauties at Yanagibashi once had, in the words of the old poem, "no mountain left to enter behind" — utterly, hopelessly destitute.

Musashino by Yamada Bimyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The mother went on staring, her eyes wide. Heita took up his words again. "It was on my way here that I chanced to come upon her, though I never dreamed it then. A strange bond of fate it must have been — for at the time I had no notion at all that she was my lady's own daughter... A pitiful thing it has come to, that in so short a space three should be lost." The mother's eyes remained wide with shock. Her lips moved as though to speak, but not a single word came out. Just then a clamor of footsteps rose at the gate. "Lady Oshimo has been eaten by a bear!"
