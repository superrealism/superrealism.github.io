What is Han'an (Adaptation)?

Category
Style & Technique

A method of creative writing that borrows the plot of a foreign work or a classic and remakes it by transposing its characters and setting to one's own country or era. It played a major role in the Meiji era, when the reception of Western literature was just beginning; Kuroiwa Ruikō, above all, boldly Japanized a stream of Western detective and adventure novels — "Ganchichō" ("The Count of Monte Cristo"), "Ā Mujō" ("Les Misérables"), "Yūreitō" ("The Ghost Tower"), "Hakuhatsuki" ("The White-Haired Demon") — often in the pages of the newspaper "Yorozu Chōhō," which he had himself founded, and, in plain prose that thrilled readers, laid the groundwork for popular fiction in Japan. His influence reached deep into later writers: Edogawa Ranpo, who had been an avid reader of Ruikō as a boy, later rewrote Ruikō's own "Hakuhatsuki" as his own work. Adaptation was not limited to Western works: it also became established in modern literature as a way of breathing new life into the classics, as when Dazai Osamu retold several of Ihara Saikaku's works as modern fiction in "Shinshaku Shokoku Banashi" ("New Tales of the Provinces, Newly Told"), or when Yoshikawa Eiji based his "Sangokushi" ("Romance of the Three Kingdoms") on the Chinese "Romance of the Three Kingdoms."
