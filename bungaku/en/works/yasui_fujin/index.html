About this work

A historical story Mori Ōgai published in Taiyō in 1914, drawing Yasui Sokken — Nakahei — a great Confucian scholar at the end of the shogunate, and his wife Osayo. To Nakahei, ugly in appearance but brilliant, the girl Osayo, reputed a beauty, comes as a bride at her own wish. In poverty she asked no reward and gave her whole life to her husband's learning and advancement — but what was it that she wanted? A fine piece in which Ōgai drew, in a concise historical style full of resonance, one woman's silent devotion and the riddle at the bottom of her heart.

Summary

Alongside the reputation that 'young Nakahei will surely rise to greatness,' there also spread through the village of Kiyotake in Hyūga province the whispered gossip that 'young Nakahei is an ugly man.' Yasui Nakahei — later to be known as Yasui Sokken, one of the great Confucian scholars of the closing years of the shogunate — was a man of brilliant, quick intelligence, yet his face bore the scars left by smallpox. It is to this Nakahei that a girl reputed for her beauty, Osayo, comes to marry, choosing him herself over her own elder sister. Though the world around them marvels at it, Osayo, with nothing to rely on but her talented husband's future, remains utterly unshaken even in the depths of poverty, and devotes herself wholly to supporting Nakahei. When her husband travels to Edo to study, she keeps the household in his absence; however much she suffers in their finances, she never utters a word of complaint, resigns herself to the plainest of clothing, and gives herself entirely to her husband's scholarship and advancement. But by the time Nakahei has finally made a name for himself as a great Confucian scholar, and their life has at last gained some ease, Osayo has already left the world, without ever fully witnessing his success. What was it she wished for, what did she dream of, that she gave her whole life to that ugly man? The wise of this world will say she wished only for her husband's success — but were not her beautiful eyes, until the moment they closed forever, gazing at some far, far distant place? A masterpiece in which Ōgai, in a chronicle-like style rich with lingering emotion, depicts one woman's silent devotion and the mystery hidden deep within her heart.

Characters

  • Osayo　A girl reputed for her beauty. Marries the ugly but talented Nakahei of her own choosing, and devotes her whole life, seeking no reward, to her husband's scholarship and advancement, but dies before witnessing his success
  • Yasui Nakahei (Sokken)　A scholar of classical Chinese learning, ugly of face but brilliant of mind. Later makes a name for himself as one of the great Confucian scholars of the closing years of the shogunate

The opening (original text)

Alongside the reputation that "young Nakahei will surely rise to greatness," there spreads through the whole village of Kiyotake the whispered word that "young Nakahei is an ugly man." Nakahei's father held a plot of house-land of about two tan and eight se in Kiyotake village, Miyazaki district, Hyūga province, and had built three houses there to live in. As for property, he held fields and paddies a short distance from the house-land, and for years had taught the classical Chinese learning to the children of others in his home, all the while never giving up his farming.

Yasui fujin by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

What was it that Osayo wished for? The wise of this world will simply say she wished for her husband's success. I, who write this, cannot deny that either. But if one were to say that Osayo, like a merchant investing capital to seek a profit, offered up her labor and endurance to her husband and died before ever receiving her due reward — that, I confess my own dullness, I cannot bring myself to agree with. Osayo must surely have wished for something, somewhere in the future. And until the moment her eyes closed forever, the gaze of those beautiful eyes was fixed on some far, far distant place — so far, perhaps, that she had no room left even to feel her own death as a misfortune. And perhaps she herself could not clearly say just what that object of her longing had been.
