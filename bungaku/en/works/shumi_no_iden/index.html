About this work

A novella opening on a triumphal procession from the Russo-Japanese War, published in the magazine Teikoku Bungaku in January 1906 and later collected in Sōseki's early volume Yōkyoshū. At the grave of a friend killed at Port Arthur, an unknown and beautiful woman has laid white chrysanthemums — and the narrator sets out to explain the mystery by heredity, then a new science. An unusual early work in which a satire on the horror of war stands beside a detective-story turn.

Summary

The narrator, a scholar living at Nishikatamachi, goes to Shinbashi station to see the returning soldiers, and weeps at the sun-blackened face of the general and at the sight of a tiny old mother hanging on the neck of a sergeant come home. And at the same moment he remembers his friend Kawakami Kōichi, who leaped into a trench at Port Arthur and never came up again. Going to the Jakkōin at Komagome to visit the grave by way of mourning, he meets a beautiful woman he has never seen, who lays white chrysanthemums before Kō-san's stone and presses her palms together. He had never heard that Kō-san had any acquaintance among women. Puzzled, he borrows the field diary from Kō-san's mother, finds in it the line "dreamed of the woman I met at the post office," and concludes that this strange connection can be explained by an "inheritance of taste" — the memory of an ancestor's love reappearing in a descendant; and he sets out to trace the bloodlines of the two families.

Characters

  • I　A scholar living at Nishikatamachi who studies heredity. The narrator
  • Kawakami Kōichi　The narrator's close friend, killed as an infantry lieutenant on Shōjusan at Port Arthur
  • Kō-san's mother　An old woman who lived alone with her only son, and who entrusts his field diary to the narrator
  • The Onoda young lady　The beautiful woman laying white chrysanthemums on Kō-san's grave at the Jakkōin. The sister of a doctor of engineering
  • The old man of Azabu　A man of nearly eighty, steward of the old domain, who tells the narrator of the unhappy love between the ancestors of the two families

The opening (original text)

The season is enough to send even the gods mad. When the voice cried out from within the clouds, "Slaughter men and save the starving dogs," and the cry shook the Japan Sea upside down and rang to the far end of Manchuria, the Japanese and the Russians answered with a start and opened one great slaughterhouse, forty leagues across, on the northern plain. And then, from below the end of the boundless flatland, came a swarm of dogs beyond counting, cutting the reeking wind sideways and tearing it lengthwise, flying as if four-legged bullets had all been fired at once. At the signal of the maddened god, who danced and cried "Drink the blood," their lolling tongues lit up the dark earth, and one heard the sound of blood boiling up over their throats.

Shumi no iden by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I saw the general with his blackened face. I saw the sergeant with the old woman hanging on him. I heard the roar of the welcome. And I shed tears. Kō-san leaped into the trench and never came up. No one went out to meet Kō-san. In all the world there are probably only these two, this mother and this young lady, who think of him. Every time I see how well the two of them get on together, I shed clearer and cooler tears than when I saw the general, or when I saw the sergeant. The doctor, it seems, knows nothing about it.

Shumi no iden by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)
