About this work

Kitahara Hakushū's second collection, following Jashūmon, subtitled "Lyrical Pieces." An autobiographical book of poems setting down, as blocks of sensation, his memories of a boyhood spent in the waterways of his native Yanagawa. The long prose piece at the head, "How I Grew Up," is famous as a fine example of modern Japanese autobiographical prose. Tonka John is Hakushū himself.

Summary

"I dedicate this small collection of lyric songs to my mother, so frail and lovely in those days gone by, and to my beloved younger brother, Tinka John" — after this dedication comes the long prose piece "My Upbringing." A childhood spent lavishing an unaccountable, mysterious affection on the warm rustle of harvested barley, on the red-necked firefly, on the eyes of the blue dragonfly, on the beautiful coat of a black cat — memories of sensation too elusive to grasp, Hakushū writes, that even now irritate his heart, frighten it, make it grieve, make it suffer. "I want this small collection of poems to be read as nothing other than a kind of history of sensation, or a history of sexual awakening." His family home was a sake brewery in Yanagawa. The watery town threaded with canals, the lingering traces of the old Southern Barbarian trade, festivals and traveling theater troupes, and a sensuality awakened early — all of this is sung out across chapters titled "My Upbringing," "Memories," "The Budding of Life," "The Sorrow of Tonka John," and "Fragments." The language of the poems is extremely plain, and yet color, scent, and sound rise up from it in dense profusion: the scene of a country play accompanied by the refrain "se, se, mizuguruma," the straw mat on the earthen floor where leek shoots poke through, the parting of traveling actors. And near the close of the book, in "Furusato" ("My Home Town"), a contradictory love for his native place is sung: he hated the people there, hated even his parents, loathed the little town enough to leave home in the dead of night, and yet — there was nothing else to be done. The collection closes with a few final lines: light spilling from the crack of a door not yet gone to sleep, and, clinging to the budding water-caltrop, the damp, nostalgic scent of the oil press, fading softly away.

The opening (original text)

I dedicate this small collection of lyric songs to my mother, so frail and lovely in those days gone by, and to my beloved younger brother, Tinka John. Tonka John. My Upbringing One Time passed. And, before I knew it, the affection I had once lavished — for no reason I could name, mysteriously — on the warm rustle of harvested barley, on the red-necked firefly, on the eyes of the blue dragonfly, on the beautiful coat of a black cat, that childhood of mine turned, before I knew it, into the joys and sorrows of a beloved thing called "memory."

Omoide by Kitahara Hakushū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

My Home Town I hate the people, I hate even my parents, I loathed this little town so much, that I left home in the dead of night — but there was nothing else to be done. The mist falls, the moon shines, and against the whiteness of the wall the crickets are singing, the water in the canal is grieving. On thin toes, lonesome, faintly, as I hurry along the road, from the crack of a door not yet gone to sleep light spills out too, and, clinging to the budding water-caltrop, how dear, how it seeps in and fades away — the damp scent of the oil press.

Omoide by Kitahara Hakushū (Aozora Bunko)
