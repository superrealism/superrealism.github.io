What is Yūgen?

Category
Other

An aesthetic principle running through the medieval waka, renga, and noh, describing a deep, ineffable feeling that drifts beyond the surface meaning of words. Originally a term used in Buddhism and Chinese classical texts to mean "profoundly deep, beyond fathoming," it became a term of poetic criticism from the late Heian period, when Fujiwara no Shunzei held it up as a refined, elegant beauty steeped in lingering suggestion, the ideal of waka, and it crystallized, at the hands of his son Teika and others, in the mysterious, sensuous poetic style of the "Shin Kokin Wakashū." In the medieval period it was carried on as well by Shinkei and other renga poets, and Zeami, further, named "yūgen" the state, in noh, of a deep, unfathomable quality dwelling within a graceful, gentle form. In treatises such as "Fūshikaden," Zeami called the moving response that arises in the audience's heart "hana" ("flower"), and set yūgen at its root. This aesthetic sensibility, which entrusts itself to margin and lingering suggestion rather than depicting everything fully, is, together with the court's "mono no aware," the medieval sense of impermanence, and the early-modern "wabi" and "sabi," a key term for speaking of Japanese beauty. As a distinctively Japanese reticence, set against a Western beauty that prizes clarity, it has been repeatedly taken up in discussions of Japanese culture from the modern era on.
