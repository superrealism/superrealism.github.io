About this work

A transcript of a lecture Sōseki gave for about an hour, without even fixing a title, when the literary society of the Tokyo Higher Technical School invited him. It begins with the self-mocking remark that, after refusing dozens of invitations, he ran out of endurance in the contest of patience and came; he goes on to say that he too once meant to be an architect and was headed for a technical career, and to describe how he moved from that to literature. A record in a relaxed voice, speaking to students of another discipline about literature as a profession and about a person's proper work.

Summary

It is the first time I have come to this school, but it is not the first time I have been asked — so Sōseki begins. Since a professor asked him two or three years ago he has refused every one of some dozens of invitations. He refuses not because it amuses him but because he cannot help it; and since being unable to help it so many times over began to seem hard on them, he has come today. Since it is a sort of contest of endurance in which his endurance has given out, he can hardly give an amusing talk. He has no subject either. ... With that preamble, Sōseki begins from the distance between himself and his hearers. His field is altogether different from theirs, and but for such an occasion they would never meet. And yet he too once meant to become a specialist in a branch of engineering. He meant to be an architect — why is not the question, but since it comes up in the course of the talk, and so on. The subject moves in time to his own calling and to the state of literature and art, and arrives at the single point that, though there may be need also for men of letters and artists who conduct as it were, one must be conscious of one's proper part as a human being. This is the important place, which ought to be explained fully; but since there is no time today he will stop here, says Sōseki; and he closes by saying that though he has spoken in detail about his own side because their callings are different, it should hold, to some degree, for their side as well.

The opening (original text)

It is the first time I have been to this school — er, the first time I have come, but by no means the first time I have received an invitation. Two or three years ago I was asked by Mr. Tanaka (Tanaka Kiichi, professor at the Tokyo Higher Technical School). The gentleman who came then to ask me will have graduated by now. Since that time I have received some dozens of invitations, and have refused them all.

Ohanashi by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

What I have said I have said in detail about our side, because of the difference between your calling and ours; but it will hold to a certain extent for your side too, and there will be something in it that may be of use in the field of your calling. Since this is a meeting of the literary society, however, I have the right to say so proudly even if it is of no use. But if it is of any use to you, whether as individuals or in your calling, I shall be very glad. That is all.

Ohanashi by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)
