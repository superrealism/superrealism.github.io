About this work

The novella that continues "Spring Daytime," published in Shinshōsetsu in December 1906. The stroller, who heard about the dead guest from the abbot in the first part, now speaks with Mio, the wife of the Tamawaki house who was the other party to it. Told that he resembles the man she loved, he is entrusted with a poem for her by a pair of passing street performers — and where that message goes converges into a quiet tragedy on the spring shore. With the first part it makes one work, and it is a central piece of Kyōka's fantastic writing.

Summary

Having left the hermitage, the Stroller comes upon a beautiful woman sketching on the bank of a rape-blossom field. It is Miwo, wife of Tamawaki. Telling him he resembles someone she once loved, she calls over a pair of traveling Kakubei-jishi lion-dancer brothers passing by on their rounds, and has them carry off a note bearing a poem by Izumi Shikibu, addressed to no one she will name. The younger of the two boys seals the paper inside his lion mask and departs. Who is the message meant to reach — or will it reach no one at all? Before long, out on the sea before the Stroller's eyes, the younger brother is swept away by the waves. The body is not found that day, but the next morning, on the ebbing tide at first light, it washes ashore on the rocks of Meikaku Point — the very place where the hermitage's lodger drowned the summer before — and there, on the rocks, two figures are found together.

Characters

  • The Stroller　The narrator, continuing from the first part. Exchanges words with Tamawaki's wife
  • Tamawaki Miwo　Wife of Tamawaki Sainosuke. Sketches on the embankment and entrusts a poem to the Kakubei-jishi dancers
  • The Kakubei-jishi Brothers　Children who go door to door performing lion dances. The younger seals the note inside his lion mask
  • The Priest　The head of the hermitage at Ganshō-ji. Told of the hermitage lodger's fate in the first part

The opening (original text)

Before long the rain cleared, and over garden and hill alike fell a haze embroidered with butterflies and blossoms upon blue velvet. By whatever chance, the scent of rape blossoms had soaked into the hermitage, into the very seat where they sat, into their sleeves. The priest, in his earnest way, urged that once the sun was out he would guide him to the Serpent's Turret on the hill behind. But the Stroller found he had no heart, just then, to pass beyond the rear of the Kannon hall, and so, promising to call again another day, he presently took his leave of the hermitage.

Shunchū gokoku by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The body could not be found all that day, but the next morning, on the ebbing tide at first light, it washed up on the rocks of Meikaku Point — the same rocks where the hermitage's lodger had drowned the summer before — and this time there were two figures there. One face lay pressed against a breast like polished jade, a scarlet cloak soaked through and wound about snow-white arms — a form of such beauty and grace. Tamawaki's wife must have known, then, where that spirit had gone. Farewell, she had said, at the foot of the embankment, as they parted; and when he glanced back after walking some way off, he could not forget the sight of her — the deep purple of her paper umbrella tilted sideways at her waist, her head bowed a little beneath the weight of her black hair, watching him go. How the tide must have tangled that hair. The sand of the shore, however it crumbles, gathers again; however it hollows, fills again, without a sound. Only beautiful bones emerge from it. The colors of the shells: the crimson of the sun, the snow of the shore, the green of the waves.

Shunchū gokoku by Izumi Kyōka (Aozora Bunko)
