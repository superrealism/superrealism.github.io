About this work

A short story Tokuda Shūsei published in Keizai Ōrai in 1933, a late work by the master of naturalism. It sets the narrator's journey home at the news that his sister in the country is dying, and his recollection of her seventy years, against the pleasures of the dance hall he frequents in the city. Age and death, the memory of family, and the loneliness of a modern person without roots, set down in a dry, spare hand.

Summary

"I," who dislikes formal crested kimono and goes to funerals in Western dress even, receives a telegram saying that my sister in the country is dying, and, wanting to exchange a few words with her while she still draws breath, sets out for my hometown, to which I had had scarcely any occasion to return in a long while. But by the time I arrive at the station, my sister has already, in the middle of the night, somewhere around when the train was passing through Nagano or Komoro, come to the end of her seventy years. Past Karuizawa, in the now-quiet train car, I find myself calling up my sister's life, image after image, like clouds scattered by the wind — her days as a beautiful young bride, celebrated throughout the town; the thirty years she spent keeping house while her husband worked at a mine in some distant province, raising several children while running a small leaf-tea shop; and, at last, her husband's death the year before. Back in my hometown, I stop at a restaurant hoping to eat ayu, only to be told there is none to be had, and I find myself irritated in some indefinable way. My journey home, bound up with my sister's death, comes to intersect quietly with the pleasures of the dance hall I frequent back in the city. Moving my body on the dance floor clears, for a while, my gloomy mind — and once home, I fall into a sweet sleep beneath the wide blue mosquito net in the parlor. A piece in which Shūsei, the great master of Japanese naturalism, sets down, in a spare and weathered style, the loneliness of the modern person cut loose from his roots, amid old age, death, and the memory of family.

Characters

  • I　An aging man who dislikes formal crested kimono. He returns to his hometown on news that his sister in the country is dying, but she has already passed; he recalls her life while continuing to frequent a dance hall in the city
  • My sister　The narrator's sister. After a beautiful youth as a bride, she keeps house through her husband's long absence working at a mine, raises children while running a leaf-tea shop, and comes to the end of her seventy years

The opening (original text)

Since it was summer, I ought to have prepared something cool to wear, but I dislike formal crested kimono, and so I had made it my habit to go to funerals and the like mostly in Western dress. A crested kimono somehow brings to mind spears and bows, ancestors out of the feudal age. Besides, Japanese clothing is loose and flapping, never quite fits the body, leaves one's very state of mind feeling slack, and on top of all that is an extremely troublesome thing to wear. I had received a telegram saying that my sister in the country was dying, and, wanting to exchange a few words with her while she still drew breath, I had set out. If not for that, my hometown was one to which I had had scarcely any occasion to return.

The ending (original text)

Leaving a couple of tickets behind in my pocket, I left the studio. The exercise cleared my gloomy mind for a while. The moment I got home, I fell into a sweet sleep beneath the wide blue mosquito net hung in the parlor.
