About this work

An early long work by Akutagawa, set in a ruined Heian capital, drawing a band of thieves, the two brothers caught up with them, and the relation of both to the bewitching woman Shakin. Though it belongs with "Rashōmon" and "In a Grove" among his court pieces, here blood and desire swirl thickly. Akutagawa himself called it a failure, but its rendering of passion is unlike anything else in his work.

Summary

On a sweltering July day at noon, at the crossing of Suzaku and Ayanokōji in the ruined capital, Jirō — an ugly, one-eyed samurai — hails an old woman known as Granny of Inokuma. Both belong to a band of thieves who prey upon the city. Jirō has an elder brother, Tarō, a huge man scarred faintly by smallpox who serves as the band's leader in all but name — yet between these two brothers lies a woman named Sakin. Sakin, Granny of Inokuma's daughter, is Tarō's mistress, yet she has given herself to Jirō as well. Torn between his debt to his brother and his obsession with the woman, Jirō is driven to the point of even contemplating his brother's murder as a way out. The old woman, in her aged greed, sells her own daughter; Tarō, with a beast's simplicity, drowns himself in the woman; and Sakin, playing every man for her own ends, lives faithful to nothing but her own desire. In time the band plots a great raid on the mansion of the Governor Fujiwara, and as that night approaches, each of their private calculations comes to a boil. In the ensuing melee the brothers cross blades, and the hatred simmering over the woman, together with the blood-tie neither can quite sever, erupts to the surface. The tale then shifts to a scene after the affair, in which the Imperial Police investigate what happened. Through the testimony of a girl named Anō, who had lived in Tarō's household, fragments of that night come to light, and she, found innocent of any crime, is released. More than ten years later, Anō, now a nun raising a child, sees a man of great renown for his valor pass among the retinue of a certain Governor of Tango, and tells those around her that it is Tarō — the man, indeed, bears the same faint smallpox scars and the same blind eye. But whether it is truly Tarō, no one can ever know for certain.

Characters

  • Tarō　The elder brother, leader of the band of thieves. Bears faint smallpox scars and is blind in one eye
  • Jirō　His younger brother. Torn between his brother and Sakin
  • Sakin　A woman who plays both brothers against each other
  • Granny of Inokuma　Sakin's mother
  • Anō　A girl who lived in Tarō's household

The opening (original text)

"Granny — Granny of Inokuma." At the crossing of Suzaku and Ayanokōji, a young samurai of about twenty, ugly and one-eyed, dressed in a plain indigo suikan with a crumpled eboshi on his head, raised a flat-ribbed fan and called out to an old woman passing by. — It was the height of a sweltering July day, the sky trailing summer haze, hanging low and hushed over the rooftops as though holding its breath.

Chūtō by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Then, more than ten years later, Anō, who had become a nun and was raising a child, once saw a man of great renown for his valor pass by among the retinue of a certain Governor of Tango, and told those around her that this was Tarō. Indeed, that man too bore the faint scars of smallpox, and was blind in one eye. "If it were Jirō-san, I'd run to meet him at once — but that man frightens me..." Anō said this with a girlish air. But whether it was truly Tarō or not, no one can say.
