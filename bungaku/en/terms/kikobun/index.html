What is Kikōbun (Travel Writing)?

Category
Form / Genre

Prose recording the scenery and human affairs a writer sees along the road, together with the feelings that come and go. Its origin goes back as far as Ki no Tsurayuki's "Tosa Nikki" ("The Tosa Diary," around 935), written in kana on the journey home from Tosa to the capital, and Matsuo Bashō's "Oku no Hosomichi" ("The Narrow Road to the Deep North"), depicting a wandering journey woven through with haikai, stands as the classical peak of the genre. In the modern era, too, many writers have taken up the pen on the road: Mori Ōgai's "Michi no Ki"; Shimazaki Tōson's "Chikuma-gawa no Suketchi" ("Sketches of the Chikuma River," 1911), depicting the nature and daily life of Shinshū in a sketch-based style; Watsuji Tetsurō's fresh "Koji Junrei" ("Pilgrimage to Ancient Temples," 1919), touring the old temples and Buddhist statues of Nara; the travel-loving tanka poet Wakayama Bokusui's "Minakami Kikō" (1924), crossing the mountains from Kōzuke to Echigo; and Akutagawa Ryūnosuke's "Shanhai Yūki" ("Shanghai Travels," 1921), walking through various parts of China as a special correspondent. Travel writing is not a mere record of an itinerary; its particular savor lies in the way a writer's thought and aesthetic sensibility seep through the unfamiliar sights of a foreign place.
