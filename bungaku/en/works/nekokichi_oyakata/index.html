About this work

Kusuyama Masao's version of the tale by Charles Perrault known as Puss in Boots (the Japanese title carries the subtitle "otherwise called the Cat in Boots"). The youngest son, who gets nothing in the division of the estate but a single cat, is raised to a marquis by that cat's wits alone. It goes forward on a rising heap of splendid lies — presenting game to the king to make his master's name, having him pretend to be drowning in the river, and at the last getting the ogre to turn himself into a mouse and eating him. The closing lesson is added too: better for a young man to set up on his own wits and his own arm than on what his parents leave him.

Summary

A poor miller dies, and all he leaves his three sons is a mill, a donkey and a cat. The youngest son's share is a single cat, and he laments that once he has eaten it and made gloves of its skin there will be nothing left but to starve. But the cat asks for a bag and a pair of boots, and having got them catches rabbits and partridges one after another and begins carrying them to the king's palace: "a gift from my lord the Marquis of Carabas." Presently the cat has his master bathe in the river, hides his clothes, and raises an outcry that robbers have taken the marquis's clothes. Dressed in the fine suit brought down from the king's coach, the young man is liked at once by the princess who rides with him. Running ahead, the cat frightens the reapers and the mowers along the road into answering that the land belongs to the Marquis of Carabas, and at last goes into the castle of the ogre who really owns it. He flatters the ogre into turning himself into a lion, and then into trying something small, like a mouse — and eats the mouse that springs out, at one bite. Master of the castle, his master marries the princess that very day, and Puss is raised to be a great lord.

Characters

  • Puss　The cat the miller's youngest son inherits, who by his wits alone makes his master a marquis
  • The youngest son　He gets nothing but the cat in the division, and comes to be called the Marquis of Carabas
  • The king　Pleased with the gifts, he takes the marquis up into his coach
  • The princess　The king's daughter, whose heart goes out to the marquis at first sight
  • The ogre　The true owner of the land and the castle

The opening (original text)

1 Once upon a time there was a miller who had three sons. He had been poor to begin with, so that the property he had to divide among his children when he died was nothing but the windmill that turned his millstone, a donkey, and a single cat. When the time came to divide it, neither notary nor clerk of the court was called in; it was done in the plainest way, the eldest son taking the mill, the second the donkey, and the youngest the cat. The youngest son, having been given so poor a share, was altogether cast down by it.

The ending (original text)

— "you are in every way a suitable match for us," he said. The marquis made a low bow and gladly accepted the honor the king offered him; and that same day he married the princess. As for Puss, he was raised to be a great lord, and from then on he never chased mice except for his own amusement, and passed his days at his ease. Better than sitting snug and warm on what one's parents leave, a young man does best to set up on his own wits and his own arm.
