About this work

Kasai Zenzō's representative work, published in "Waseda Bungaku" in 1918. Four months behind with the rent and pressed to quit the house, "he" is living with his two children, having heard nothing from the wife he sent to her home province to raise money. He goes again and again to his friend K to beg, is called in by the police, and at last, the appointed day having come, has no choice but to leave the house. A single day of wandering the night streets with nowhere to go, a child by the hand, drawn exactly as the author's own life was lived. It was this piece that fixed the name of a writer who made his poverty and his ruin into his work and was called "the god of the I-novel."

Summary

The deposit is used up and the rent four months in arrears. Relations with the landlord are broken off, and the "three-hundred" — the man who does nothing but demand that tenants quit — appears again and again. "He" has been made to sign a bond giving him ten days, and by desperate contrivance has sent his wife off to her family in a distant province to raise money; but she has not come back, and their youngest girl is with her. Next to the garden is a graveyard, and the morning glories he tended carefully have put out nothing but vine, no flowers. If he goes to his friend K he can borrow a yen. But K asks: "Well, for today I will give you a yen, certainly I will — but what then?" Does it never frighten you, going on like this? It is not some vague dread; it is a clear and terrible fact. He still cannot make out what this terrible thing really is. He knows only that he is sinking moment by moment, as though his legs were caught in a bog. The day comes, his household goods are carried out, and he leaves the house with his eldest son and daughter. Drinking in a bar, he mutters to himself that the life of an artist who has lost the power of being moved is a bad life. Past ten at night he asks at K's lodging house for two or three days' shelter, but the old couple who keep it see that something is not right and refuse. His daughter begins to cry aloud.

Characters

  • He　A novelist behind with the rent and pressed to quit his house. He leaves it with his two children
  • The eldest son and daughter　The children who wander the night streets led by their father
  • Wife　She went back to her family to raise money, taking the youngest girl, and has not been heard from since
  • K　A friend lodging in Shibuya. He answers the begging, and questions severely the way his friend is living
  • The "three-hundred"　A man of forty-two or three who comes to demand that they quit the house

The opening (original text)

1 He had swept, and boiled the vegetables, and taken out the pickles, and seen the children through their supper; and then at last, near the veranda from which the western sun had gone, he set down his tray and sat sipping his evening cup with a desolate feeling. Just then, without so much as a by-your-leave, the lattice door at the front slid softly open, and the "three-hundred" who came about the eviction thrust his face through the gap of the open paper door at the entrance.

The ending (original text)

Along the dark moat where the damp wind of the late night blew pleasantly, a streetcar with few passengers ran by at speed. You will not be able to go on living! — K's face as he said it, the police inspector's face; and yet was it really so very great a matter? "…But to drag even the children in with me?" Yes! That was surely a terrible thing. But now his head and his body, like his children's, wanted only rest.
