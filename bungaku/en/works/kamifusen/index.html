About this work

A fine Sunday afternoon, a room facing the garden. A young couple with nothing to do act out, against their boredom, an imaginary journey — what if we went to Kamakura — in Kishida Kunio's central work and a fine piece of modern Japanese drama. Not one thing happens, and yet the distance between the two and their loneliness rise out of the breathing of the dialogue alone.

Summary

A fine Sunday afternoon, a room facing the garden. The husband, in a rattan chair on the veranda, reads an article in the paper about a cultural-village development; the wife is knitting. "Say, shall we go for a walk or something?" "Never mind that — go on over to the Kawakamis'." Conversation after conversation fails to connect and spins its wheels. To the husband, who says he doesn't want to go, that he'd rather be by her side, the wife answers, "I know, I know, thanks all the same." Time with nothing to fill it passes, and between them settles something less like boredom than an unspeakable irritation and dissatisfaction with each other. Eventually the wife suddenly brings it up: what if they had gone to Kamakura today? From there the two of them begin to talk about the trip they never took, as though they really had taken it. Riding the train, seeing the sea, checking into an inn, the people they meet there. The husband and wife of their imagining talk far more vividly than the two of them do in reality, drawn to each other, even playing at jealousy. The more the fancy carries them along, the more starkly it throws into relief the flatness of the everyday in front of them. Eventually a paper balloon, blown over from next door, falls into the garden, and the two are pulled back to reality. The wife picks it up, blows it up, and gives it a light tap. "So that did happen too" — with this short line from the husband, the curtain falls.
