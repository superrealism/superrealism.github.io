About this work

Shimazaki Tōson's first collection, published in 1897. From "First Love," which opens with the girl's hair newly put up, it sings the feeling of young love and the longing of youth in a fresh seven-five measure. Poems celebrating a freeing of emotion from feudal habit, they were received with enthusiasm as the arrival of Meiji romanticism, and the book stands as the starting point of modern Japanese poetry.

Summary

This first collection, which Tōson set before the world in his middle twenties, holds fifty-one poems. It opens with a preface poem in which he likens his own verses to a single cluster of grapes, and goes on with works that sing the flutter and the sorrow of a young soul — "Autumn Thoughts," "First Love," "O-Efu," "The Six Maidens." "First Love" above all, which begins "When you appeared beneath the apple tree, / your forelock newly put up," draws with great delicacy a boy's faint love for the girl who hands him an apple, and is still widely loved and recited as the representative Japanese love poem. The literary language keeps its dignity, and yet what overflows in it is the free feeling of young people unbound by the old morality; with this one collection Tōson set loose the heart of Meiji youth in a new poetic language. It may fairly be said that the modern Japanese lyric begins here.

The opening (original text)

The strains of these heartless songs are like a single cluster of grapes; plucked by a kindly hand they may yet turn to warm wine. Deep on the trellis hang not those purple ones, it is true; yet by the kindness of the tender-hearted I leave three or four clusters in the shade. It is because the songs are young: their taste and their color are shallow, and most of them should be chewed and spat away — the empty words of a dozing dream.

The ending (original text)

Far too is the way by boat to Zuiganji; with no heart for a winter's ramble I lean myself against the old door, and where the famed craftsman of Hida carved his relief I come and look in the shadow of the grapes: in the winter sun of this temple of the dead the blade grieves and the chisel sorrows. Under the thin carved leaves of the vine, O squirrel hiding in their shade, though you hide your shape from me there is no hiding the scent of the chisel. Though this day should close with the bell of the shore temple sounding over the tide, if I linger on into the dusk — how I long for you, Jingorō.

Famous passages

When first I saw you, your bangs newly put up, beneath the apple tree, I took you for a flower yourself, with the flowered comb set in your hair.

The first stanza of “First Love.” Capturing a girl's age in the single detail of hair just put up, it seals a faint first love within a single scene in an apple orchard — the wellspring of modern Japanese lyric poetry.
