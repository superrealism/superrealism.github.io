About this work

An essay Masaoka Shiki published in the haiku magazine Hototogisu in 1901. From the sickbed where he suffered from tuberculosis of the spine, he sets down in a free hand his inexhaustible delight in the fruit he loved beyond everything. From the meaning of the word and a study of the kinds, to memories of the silverberry and the persimmons he tasted on his travels, the talk widens as it pleases. The ending in particular — a beautiful maidservant at an inn in Nara peeling a Gosho persimmon for him as the bell of Tōdaiji begins to sound — recalls the mood of his famous haiku about eating a persimmon and hearing the bell at Hōryūji, and is known as one of the finest scenes in his essays.

Summary

Not fruit as seen through the lens of botany, nor of the study of local products, but simply an account of the flavors he has tasted from his sickbed — with that disclaimer, Shiki sets off to write as he pleases about the fruit he loves so well. He begins with a bit of etymological digging: the origin of the word kudamono, the line separating it from konomi ("tree fruits" such as chestnuts and acorns), and where melons and muskmelons fit in — before turning, before long, to memories of fruit he has actually eaten. Traveling the roads of Shinshū, he once came upon wild jujube-cherries (natashi-gumi) ripened bright red along the roadside, and, unable to resist, had some picked for him at a teahouse on the mountain pass and ate them, delighted, from horseback. And once, visiting Nara, the pretty maid at his inn peeled him a great heaping bowlful of Goshō persimmons; as he sat gazing at them, entranced, a bell tolled out — boom — and he was told it was the great bell of Tōdai-ji striking the first watch of the night; sliding open the shutter, he found Tōdai-ji standing almost directly overhead, and was told that at night the cry of the deer could be heard from there as well. A fine piece of Shiki's essay-writing, drawing together a fresh, vivid appetite and the memory of his travels even as he lies ill in bed — closing, in a scene of persimmons and Nara that recalls his famous haiku, "I bite into a persimmon, and the bell tolls — Hōryū-ji."

Characters

  • I　The sickbed narrator, recounting his love of fruit and his memories of travel
  • The maid at the Nara inn　A fair, pretty woman who peeled Goshō persimmons for him in a large bowl. Said to hail from Tsukigase

The opening (original text)

This is not fruit as seen from the standpoint of botany, nor fruit as seen from the standpoint of the study of local products — it is simply an account of the good and bad flavors of the fruits I have eaten here on my sickbed. Where I go wrong, put it down to the roughened tongue of an invalid, and forgive me.

Kudamono by Masaoka Shiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

When I asked whose bell that was, I was told that it was the great bell of Tōdai-ji, striking the first watch of the night. When I asked whether Tōdai-ji was right above my head, she said it was, just there. Seeing me look doubtful, the maid went out onto the wooden floor beyond the room and slid open the inner shutter there to show me. And indeed, Tōdai-ji stood almost directly overhead. Whatever night's moon it was, it shone, faint and lonely, over the ragged trees round about. The maid pointed further on and told me that at night, behind the hall of the great Buddha, over that way, the deer could be heard crying, quite clearly.

Kudamono by Masaoka Shiki (Aozora Bunko)
