About this work

A long detective novel serialized in the magazine Hinode in 1934. The beautiful thief known as the Black Lizard — by day the bewitching Madame Midorikawa, by night a queen of the underworld — sets her sights on a great jewel kept by an Osaka dealer, and on his daughter. Facing her is the detective Akechi Kogorō. Through disguises and stratagems, and through the Black Lizard's perverse taste for turning living people into "dolls" for her collection, the two match minds at close quarters. One of Ranpo's central novels, on a woman thief who embodies the attraction of evil, and later famously made into a play by Mishima Yukio.

Summary

The Black Lizard — a woman with a black lizard tattooed on her upper arm — passes by day as the beautiful Madame Midorikawa in society, and by night reigns as the queen of the underworld. What she is after is the great diamond called the Star of Egypt, kept by the Osaka jeweler Iwase Shōbei, and the beautiful body of his daughter Sanae as well. Sensing the danger to his daughter, Iwase asks the great detective Akechi Kogorō to guard her. The Black Lizard carries Sanae off by an ingenious stratagem, and Akechi follows hard after with disguises and devices of his own. Knowing each other for what they are, the two carry on a keen exchange of wits and daring, and between these sworn enemies something oddly like respect begins to grow. The Black Lizard's hideout proves to be a beautiful and horrible secret house, where living people have been made into stuffed "dolls" and set out on display. Her ambition to add Sanae to that collection is broken to pieces by Akechi's counterattack. At the end, driven into a corner, the Black Lizard drinks poison and dies with her head on the great detective's knee and a look of contentment on her face. Only the black lizard tattooed on her bared arm seems to stir faintly, as though grieving to be parted from its mistress. It is the finest of Ranpo's romances, a contest between the aesthetics of evil and a great detective.

The opening (original text)

It happened on a certain Christmas Eve — the night when, they say, several thousand turkeys are killed even in this country. Step one pace off the main thoroughfare of G Street, the busiest quarter of the imperial capital, where the rainbow of neon in the dark night dyes tens of thousands of passers-by in five colors, and there lies the underworld of this city.

The Black Lizard by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

So ended the life of the Black Lizard, the extraordinary woman thief who had shaken the age. She left this world with her head pillowed on the knee of the great detective Akechi Kogorō, and a smile on her face as though she were glad. Glancing down, one saw that the sleeve of her black dress must have torn when she shook off the detective's hand and ran a moment before. Her beautiful arm was bared, and there, the origin of the name she went by, the tattooed black lizard — this alone still seeming to have life in it — appeared to be stirring, faintly, very faintly, as though it grieved to be parted from its mistress.

The Black Lizard by Edogawa Ranpo (Aozora Bunko)
