About this work

A short essay Akutagawa contributed to the magazine Bunshō Kurabu in 1920. Tracing the books he had loved since boyhood, he speaks frankly of his own reading and of how his tastes changed. It begins with his affection for Journey to the West and The Water Margin, read as a child, and moves to Tokutomi Roka and Takayama Chogyū, Natsume Sōseki and Izumi Kyōka, and on to Wilde, Gautier and Strindberg. In his remark that of late he is drawn most of all to quiet books with strength in them, the change in a writer moving toward maturity shows. Brief, but with a great deal of flavour.

Summary

"As a child, my favourite book above all was Journey to the West" — Akutagawa Ryūnosuke begins with these words, and traces his own reading history frankly. He speaks of his unchanging attachment to Journey to the West and The Water Margin, which he devoured as a child, and confesses that at one time he had all one hundred and eight heroes of The Water Margin memorized by name. Around the time he entered middle school, he says, he was fond of Tokutomi Roka's Nature and Life and Takayama Chogyū's Miscellaneous Thoughts on the Heike, and also read avidly Natsume Sōseki's I Am a Cat, Izumi Kyōka's Fūryūsen, and Saitō Ryokuu. As he grew older he came to prefer showy, ornate novels like those of Wilde and Gautier, which was also a reaction against the naturalist literature then dominant in Japan. Then, around the time he left higher school, a great turn came over his tastes and way of seeing things, and all art lacking in force came to feel to him like so much rubble, leading him to fall under Strindberg's spell. But even that "worship of a burning-like force" gradually faded, and now, he concludes, it is "quiet books with strength in them" — Stendhal, Mérimée, Saikaku and the like — that draw him most. A piece of unadorned self-examination, offering a glimpse of what shaped the spirit of one writer.

The opening (original text)

As a child, my favourite book above all was Journey to the West. It remains a favourite of mine even today. I do not believe the West has produced a single masterpiece of allegory to match it. Even the famous Pilgrim's Progress of Bunyan is, in the end, no match for this Journey to the West. The Water Margin, too, is one of my favourite books. I go on reading it with pleasure even now.

Aidokusho no inshō by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Let me add, while I am at it, that I recently took out Jean-Christophe and read it again, but it did not stir me the way it once did. I began to wonder whether the books of that period no longer held up — but when I took out Anna Karenina and read two or three chapters, it felt every bit as precious as it once had.

Aidokusho no inshō by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke (Aozora Bunko)
