About this work

An epistolary novel serialized in the Kahoku Shinpō in 1945 and 1946. Hibari, a young man of twenty with a diseased lung, enters an unusual sanatorium that calls itself a health dōjō, and writes his days there in letters to a close friend. The strict but cheerful life of the place, the give and take with a vivid company of fellow patients and assistant nurses, a faint first love — and, in the very time just after the defeat, a young man refusing despair and setting himself to live a new life, drawn in a light and fresh hand. Unusually for Dazai, a bright and healthy piece full of hope.

Summary

A young man of twenty called Hibari, whose lungs are diseased, enters an unusual sanatorium that calls itself the Health Dōjō, and writes his days there in the form of letters to a close friend, "you." At this dōjō the patients are called "students" and the nurses "assistants." In a life at once well ordered and somehow briskly cheerful, Hibari mixes with a strongly individual set of people: the taciturn and richly flavored Mr. Hanayoi, nicknamed the Echigo Lion; the lively assistant Mā-bō; and the assistant Take-san, to whom he is drawn. In the letters, with humor, he sets down vividly the dōjō's odd customs, his light exchanges with the other students, his faint love for Take-san, and the reflections of a young man facing his illness. Though the time is the confusion just after the defeat, Hibari does not sink into despair or sentimentality, but tries to live brightly as a "new man." At the end of the story, after hearing Mr. Hanayoi's talk on "devotion," Hibari puts aside his straining and resolves to go straight forward at an altogether ordinary pace. "It seems that the sun shines in the direction of growth" — and toward that hope the young man lays down his pen with a "good-bye." A long work, rare in Dazai, filled with a bright and healthy will to begin again.

Characters

  • Hibari　A young man of twenty with diseased lungs who enters the Health Dōjō. The writer of the letters
  • Mr. Hanayoi (the Echigo Lion)　The taciturn, richly flavored master of the dōjō
  • Mā-bō　A lively assistant (nurse)
  • Take-san　The assistant to whom Hibari is drawn

The opening (original text)

This novel takes the form of letters from a boy of twenty, fighting his illness at a sanatorium called the Health Dōjō, to his close friend. There have not, I think, been many precedents for a novel in the form of letters in the newspaper fiction we have had so far. So the reader too may feel out of his element and be a little at a loss for the first four or five installments; but the letter form has, on the other hand, a strong sense of actuality, and has been attempted from of old by many writers abroad and in Japan alike.

Pandora no hako by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Where does this road lead? Better to ask the tendril of a growing plant. The tendril will answer: "I know nothing at all. But it seems that the sun shines in the direction of growth." Good-bye.

Pandora no hako by Dazai Osamu (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

“I know nothing at all. But it seems the sun shines in the direction I grow.”

The ending. Saying that how one should live is best asked of a plant's vine, the work sets these words as the vine's answer—a line of faint hope addressed to postwar youth.

Even when thrown into the depths of misfortune and rolling about there, a person somehow gropes his way to a single thread of hope.

A passage from the letters written to a friend from the Health Dōjō, a tuberculosis sanatorium, just after the defeat. Likening it to the hope left at the bottom of Pandora's box, it speaks brightly of the human strength to find a thread of hope even at rock bottom.
