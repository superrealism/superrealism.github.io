About this work

The concluding part of Chikamatsu Shūkō's trilogy, after "Black Hair" and "Frenzy." The obsession of a man pursuing a woman in Kyoto gives way entirely, and he ends by hiding behind a board fence to follow her mother — wretched and absurd. And yet his pen never prettifies him: he sets down his own figure walking the frozen streets of Kyoto as if it were someone else's. With this sequence, which draws the swamp of infatuation to the end, Shūkō marked out one limit of the I-novel.

Summary

"I," who have been passing my days in anguish and disappointment, went out again aimlessly into the streets on a cold morning in the middle of January. Drawn by the neighborhood where the woman used to be, I was walking a side street when I saw her mother coming toward me from the back gate of the Kenninji, wrapped in a thick muffler. How long I had waited for this chance — I hid behind a board fence and followed to see where she was going. After several turns the mother went into the house at the back of an alley which I had found the month before last. So the woman too must be in that house after all. Everything I have been told was a lie. Nodding to myself, my suspicion and my lingering attachment grow deeper still. I call, I wait, I peer in, I press her, I abuse her, and I apologize again. In the deep cold of a Kyoto winter, the man's obsession goes almost over the edge of sanity. In time the mistress of a teahouse and the young servants speak of the rumor of one who has died. Before these women renewing their memories, my own present state seemed to me far more pitiable.

Characters

  • I　A man clinging to a woman in Kyoto, fallen so far as to shadow her mother
  • The woman　The one who has hidden herself
  • The mother　The woman's mother, who seems to be hiding her daughter's whereabouts
  • The mistress and the young servants　The people of the teahouse, who gossip about what is past

The opening (original text)

I After that I went on brooding day after day in anguish and disappointment, doing nothing; and thinking that if the woman was no longer in Kyoto I might go back once to Tokyo, where I had not been since last spring, and yet not doing that either, I passed the days until it was a certain day after the middle of January.

Shimo koru yoi by Chikamatsu Shūkō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

When the young servant said so, the mistress said, "Hm, was it so? I don't know how I was at the time; I wasn't in the house, and heard of it afterward.... When she died it was truly a pity." And she seemed to be renewing her memories again; but to me my own present state was far more pitiable than that.
