About this work

The last book Yanagita Kunio published at eighty-six, the year before his death. Where did the Japanese come from? — he sets out the grand hypothesis that they came riding the Kuroshio in search of cowrie shells, island by island from the south, carrying rice with them, drawing on the folklore and the language of Okinawa. Beyond the question of whether it holds academically, it is the high point of Japanese folklore studies and its author's testament.

Summary

"Some thirty years ago I published my thoughts on the question of how the Japanese people came to cross over to these islands, and ever since, the problem of ships and seafaring has never once left my mind" — so Yanagita writes in his foreword. The sea route linking Japan and Okinawa runs today along the western coast, he notes, but in ancient times it may well have run chiefly along the eastern coast instead, since its many shoals and shallow beaches allowed sailors to advance only a short distance before beaching their boats and finding lodging for the night. The title essay, "The Road over the Sea," advances a bold hypothesis: that peoples who rode the Kuroshio Current northward from the south, in search of cowrie shells, moved island by island, and eventually reached the Japanese archipelago bringing rice cultivation with them. Traces of this passage, Yanagita argues, survive in words shared between Okinawa and the mainland, in beliefs about the Dragon Palace and the eternal land beyond the sea, in the veneration of drift objects and driftwood, and in the customs surrounding the birthing huts of the rice harvest. In "Thoughts on the Sea God's Palace" he traces the lineage of Dragon Palace legends; in "The Ship of Miroku," the belief that happiness arrives from beyond the sea, tied to faith in the Buddha of the Future; in "The Tale of the Land of Roots," the conception of the land where the dead go; and in "The Paradise of Mice," "On Cowrie Shells," and "People and Job's Tears," the bonds between small objects and the traditions attached to them, each followed out with painstaking care. Coconuts washed up on shore, stranded whales, songs of world-renewal, records from Yonaguni — in everything the southern sea brings drifting to Japan's coast, Yanagita seeks a clue to the origins of the Japanese people. Marked less by rigor of proof than by sheer breadth of vision, this is the culminating achievement of Yanagita Kunio's very last years.

The opening (original text)

Foreword Some thirty years ago I published my thoughts on the question of how the Japanese people came to cross over to these islands, and ever since, the problem of ships and seafaring has never once left my mind. Among these questions, one I feel I must set down here above all concerns the route of travel that once linked Japan and Okinawa.

Kaijō no michi by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Might it not be that, in the traffic running north and south through Japan, the eastern coast — later abandoned — was in fact once used more heavily than the west? For the old style of seafaring, the eastern coast was the more convenient of the two: its many shoals and shallow beaches let sailors advance only a short distance at a time, beach their boats on land, and take lodging there — and, once terms were settled, even remain a while in the place where they had come ashore.

Kaijō no michi by Yanagita Kunio (Aozora Bunko)
