What is Hon'yaku (Translation)?

Category
Style & Technique

The act of rendering a work written in a foreign language into one's own language, faithful to the original's meaning and expression. It is distinguished from adaptation, which borrows a work's plot while transplanting its characters and setting to one's own country, and in the Meiji era, as the full-scale importation of Western literature advanced, translation greatly enriched the style and poetic sensibility of modern literature. Futabatei Shimei's translation of a Turgenev short story, "Aibiki" ("The Rendezvous," 1888), with its precise colloquial prose capturing nature, deeply influenced the writers of genbun'itchi and Naturalism, and Mori Ōgai's translation of Andersen's novel into dignified classical prose, "Sokkyō Shijin" ("The Improvisatore," from 1892), was celebrated as finer even than the original. Above all, Ueda Bin's collection of translated poetry "Kaichōon" ("The Sound of the Tide," 1905) introduced Western European modern poets such as Verlaine and Carl Busse in flowing, musical cadences, and had a decisive influence on the Japanese Symbolist poetry movement. Nagai Kafū's translated-poetry collection "Sangoshū" ("The Coral Collection," 1913) followed in its wake. Translation was never merely a means of introducing foreign works; it remained, in its own right, an endeavor by which translators forged and expanded the expressive powers of the Japanese language itself.
