About this work

Nakahara Chūya's first collection of poems, published by Bunpodō in 1934. Steeped in French symbolism and Dada, it established a manner of his own that boldly fused colloquial and literary Japanese with the rhythms of popular song. In two parts, "Early Poems" and "Poems of the Goat," it distils the inner landscape of his boyhood and of his drifting years in Tokyo. It holds many pieces still recited today — the nonsense refrain of "Circus," the aching repetitions of "Upon my soiled sorrow" — and marks the point of departure for Nakahara as a poet.

Summary

The collection is in two parts, "Early Poems" and "Poems of the Goat." The first half sets out longing for home and memories of boyhood, the night of the city, and experimental pieces marked by Dada; in "Circus" the melancholy that hangs about a swing in a sideshow tent is drawn in pure sound. In the second half a lyricism sharpened by the death of a friend and by the disorder of his life in Tokyo comes through, and the images return again and again to a clear beauty held even within defilement and resignation, as in the lines "Upon my soiled sorrow / today again the light snow falls." Across the whole book one figure emerges: the poet as a boy's innocent soul, smeared with the world and yet never lost.

Famous passages

On this sorrow that has grown so soiled light snow falls again today On this sorrow that has grown so soiled even the wind blows past again today

Chūya's representative poem, piling up as it repeats the same line. A nameless sorrow is left described only as having “grown so soiled.”

Head hanging upside down, hands dangling free, beneath a canopy of soiled cotton cloth, yuahn, yuyohn, yuyayuyon

A line from his representative poem, “The Circus.” A refrain that turns the swing of a trapeze directly into sound became the single best-known cadence in Chūya's poetry.

The snow that fell upon me was like floss silk.

The opening of “Song of My Upbringing.” By changing the image of the snow that falls on him from childhood to the present, Chūya sings of the changes in his own life.
