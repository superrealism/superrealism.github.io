About this work

The first of Sōseki's late trilogy, serialized in the Asahi Shimbun from January to April 1912. It was his first newspaper novel after his near-fatal illness at Shuzenji, and its title comes simply from his plan to write from New Year's Day until the equinox. Several short pieces are linked through what Tagawa Keitarō, just out of university, sees and hears; the second half digs into Sunaga Ichizō's inward self-regard and his twisted feeling for his cousin Chiyoko. The theme of self-consciousness and distrust that runs on into The Wayfarer and Kokoro first appears here.

Summary

Out of university but with no position, Keitarō idles in his lodgings and longs for an adventure out of a detective story. He hears the wandering life of Morimoto, who lodges in the same house; and then, introduced through the lodging, he takes a commission from the businessman Taguchi to follow a certain man from a streetcar stop. The man proves to be Taguchi's brother-in-law, Matsumoto Tsunezō, who calls himself a man of leisure and education. Keitarō finds a post in the Taguchi house and becomes friendly with Taguchi's nephew, Sunaga Ichizō. Everyone expects Sunaga to marry his cousin Chiyoko, whom he has known since childhood; but his inward-turning self-consciousness will not let him be straightforward with her. On the trip to Kamakura, where another suitor appears for Chiyoko, he is torn between jealousy and self-contempt, and Chiyoko tells him to his face that he is a coward. In "Sunaga's story" and "Matsumoto's story" the secret of his birth comes out — that he is not in fact his mother's own child, but the son of his father and a servant — and Sunaga goes off traveling to look at himself again. By gathering up the long talk of one person after another, Keitarō has come to stand at the entrance of the drama called the world.

Characters

  • Tagawa Keitarō　A young man just out of university. The listener through whom the whole story is told
  • Sunaga Ichizō　Keitarō's friend, tormented by his own inward self-consciousness
  • Taguchi Chiyoko　Sunaga's cousin. A cheerful, straightforward girl
  • Matsumoto Tsunezō　Sunaga's uncle, who calls himself a man of leisure and education
  • Morimoto　A fellow lodger of Keitarō's, with the tale of a wandering life to tell

The opening (original text)

Keitarō was beginning to grow a little sick of the running about and exertion of these last days, which had shown so little result. His body was strongly built to begin with, and he knew well enough that mere hurrying on foot would hardly trouble him; but as the times multiplied when the thing he was after caught somewhere and sat there and would not move, or slipped clean away just as he put out his hand for it, it was his head rather than his body that grew less and less willing to obey him.

Higan sugimade by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

In short, all the knowledge and feeling about human life that he had lately acquired came through the working of his eardrums. The several long talks, beginning with Morimoto and ending with Matsumoto, had moved him at first broadly and thinly, and then more and more deeply and narrowly, and then had stopped all at once. But he had never been able to get inside them. That is what leaves him unsatisfied, and at the same time what makes him fortunate. In the unsatisfied sense he cursed the snake's head, and in the fortunate sense he blessed it. And looking up at the wide sky, he wondered how this drama, which seemed to have stopped so abruptly before him, would go on changing for ever hereafter.

Higan sugimade by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Each time I compare myself with Chiyoko, I feel the urge to repeat the words “the woman who does not fear and the man who fears.”

Words spoken by Sunaga in “Sunaga's Story,” comparing himself with his cousin Chiyoko. The contrast between a woman who acts without hesitation and a man who thinks too much to take a step hints at a theme the late Sōseki pursued to the end.
