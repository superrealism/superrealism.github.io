About this work

A short story Okamoto Kanoko published in Chūō Kōron in 1939. The narrator, a writer struggling to catch the character of a girl in a story she cannot get on with, moves into a house by the Sumida where the tide can be felt rising and falling. There she meets the real daughter of the house and the strange love affair the girl once had, and, led by images of water and the river, comes back to her own writing. A fine late work in which Kanoko set down, in a dense style, a sensuous feeling for water and a deep insight into life.

Summary

"I," a writer, had been feeling, try as I might, that something was lacking in the character of the girl in the story I was continuing to write. Thinking that a change of surroundings might change the very mood of the piece, I moved into a house along the river, where the tide of the Ōkawa came lapping in close to the window. It was a move made in obedience to an irrepressible longing to place myself at the water's edge. In that house there actually lived a girl. She had once, it was said, fallen in love with a young folk-geographer, and had traced that love as though tracing a river back to its source, until at last, just as a river settles into its course toward the sea, she had married away to a distant land at the sea's far edge. As I came to know, intimately, the strange story of that girl's love, my own way of feeling toward water gradually and deeply changed. A river holds within it an infinite wellspring, like a mother's breast, and at its end lies the boundless, all-embracing ocean — surely, then, within the river that flows between that source and that end, an infinity must lie hidden. Gazing at the faint "river-light" that lit the paper screens, "I" was led toward a new resolve: to rewrite, from the very beginning, the character of the girl in my own story. A fine late work in which Kanoko, in a dense and richly wrought style, crystallizes her sensuous insight into water and into life itself.

Characters

  • I　A writer who moves to a house along the river, hoping to grasp the character of a girl in a story she cannot finish writing
  • The girl of this house　Once fell in love with a young folk-geographer, traced her love up the river, and eventually married away to a distant land by the sea
  • The folk-geographer　The girl's former lover. A young man who once explored the upper reaches of the river together with her

The opening (original text)

Feeling that there was something lacking in the character of the girl who was the central figure of the story I was continuing to write, it occurred to me that I might as well change my surroundings entirely and see whether that would change the very mood of the piece — and so my heart came, again and again, to long for a house along the river, where the rising and falling tide of the Ōkawa could be felt lapping close against the window.

Kawākari by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

If that were so, then this faint river-light too must hold, beyond the pitiful, fragile thing I had once so longed for, some firm and certain substance of its own — and once I began to feel this clearly, in the end, my courage was brought to the point of resolving to reform the character of the girl in my story by rewriting the whole of it, from the very beginning.
