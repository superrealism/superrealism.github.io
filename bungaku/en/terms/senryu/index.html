What is Senryū?

Category
Form / Genre

A colloquial short poem in seventeen syllables (5-7-5), requiring neither a seasonal word nor a cutting word. It grew out of "maekuzuke," a game in the mid-Edo period of appending a 5-7-5 verse to a 7-7 opening verse, and its name comes directly from that of the celebrated judge of these compositions, Karai Senryū (1718–1790). In 1765, Ryōryōken Kakei compiled "Haifū Yanagidaru," the first collection of the form, taking senryū's own selected verses and stripping away the opening verse to stand alone as independent poems; it was published in 167 installments up to 1840 and beloved by the common people of Edo. Prizing "uchi" (a keen eye that sees through to the hidden truth of human feeling and social conditions), humor, and lightness of touch, it captures with wit the vanity of samurai and townspeople, the subtleties of husband and wife or parent and child, and the manners of the pleasure quarters — all the very human subject matter that haiku, which honors nature, does not directly address. This is why it is said that, against haiku, which sings of nature, senryū sings of humanity. From the Meiji era on it was carried forward in the reader-submission columns of newspapers and magazines, and today, as "jiji senryū," satirizing current events, and "salaryman senryū," singing of the sorrows of the office worker, it remains one of the most widely beloved short verse forms.
