What is Shokantai Shōsetsu (Epistolary Novel)?

Category
Form / Genre

Fiction that unfolds its story through a series of letters written by its characters. It has the confessional power of a writer laying bare his or her heart directly, and misunderstandings between sender and recipient, as well as the gaps left by what the letters do not say, are effects peculiar to the form. It was hugely popular in eighteenth-century Europe, producing masterpieces such as Richardson's "Pamela" (1740), Rousseau's "Julie, or the New Heloise" (1761), Goethe's "The Sorrows of Young Werther" (1774), and Laclos's "Dangerous Liaisons" (1782). In modern Japanese fiction, "Sensei and His Testament," the final part of Natsume Sōseki's "Kokoro" (1914), is written as Sensei's long letter to the young narrator and forms the heart of the work. Kajii Motojirō's "K no Shōten" ("The Ascension of K," 1926) recounts a fantastic death on a moonlit night in the form of a reply to a letter inquiring about the drowning of the narrator's friend K. Yumeno Kyūsaku's "Binzume Jigoku" ("Hell in Bottles," 1928) brings to light the fate of a brother and sister cast away on a remote island through three letters found sealed in three beer bottles. Dazai Osamu was fond of the form: besides "Kyokō no Haru" (1936), composed entirely of letters addressed to himself, he wrote "Kaze no Tayori," made up of an exchange of letters between two writers, and the postwar novel "Pandora no Hako," told through the letters a young man in a sanatorium sends to his close friend.
