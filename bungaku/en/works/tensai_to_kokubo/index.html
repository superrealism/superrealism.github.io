About this work

The physicist Terada Torahiko's best-known essay, written as a warning about disaster preparedness and published in the magazine Keizai Ōrai in 1934, just after the Muroto typhoon devastated the Kansai. The more civilization advances, Terada argues, the greater the damage that disasters do. People forget disaster easily and let their preparations lapse in the good years, and so it comes when they have forgotten it. If national defence means readiness against a foreign enemy, then meeting the strong enemies of nature — earthquake, typhoon, flood — with united scientific measures in peacetime would be the fitting modern evolution of the Japanese spirit. A pioneering piece of criticism that treated disaster preparedness as a national question, and still read today.

Summary

In 1934, when the word "emergency" was everywhere and unease about the international situation trailed through the minds of the people, Terada Torahiko turned his eyes to the natural disasters that had come one after another that year: the great fire at Hakodate, the floods in Hokuriku, the great wind and flood in the Kansai. When misfortunes follow one another in a family or a country, people look for mysterious causes and turn to rites of purification; but statistically these are no more than chance "natural variation," and it is an iron law of nature that a bad run of years must come round sooner or later. And yet human beings forget entirely the obvious duty of preparing thoroughly while the good years last. Terada goes on to point out that the more civilization advances and the more complicated society's machinery becomes, the greater the damage an earthquake or a typhoon will do — and that it is in this paradox that the danger of the modern age lies. Just as a nation prepares its defenses against a foreign enemy, so, against the strong enemy that is nature, the people should unite in peacetime to take scientific measures: that, he argues quietly but urgently, would be a fitting evolution of patriotism, of the Japanese spirit, for the present age.

The opening (original text)

I cannot remember when the word "emergency" — a word somehow ominous, and yet with a meaning hard to make out clearly — began to be fashionable; but it is a fact that lately a shadow of unease, like a formless nightmare, has been trailing through the deepest layer of the consciousness of the whole nation: the sense that something like a black cloud threatening the peace of all Japan is peeping quietly at us from beyond a distant horizon. And the center about which that whirlpool of unease turns is, of course, the difficulties of international negotiation that are expected in the near future. As though to fan that unease further, this year one natural disaster after another has struck our country and seems to have carried off a great number of lives and a great deal of property. While the memory of that terrible fire at Hakodate, and more recently of the floods in the Hokuriku region, was still fresh, the great wind and flood of the twenty-first of September fell upon the Kinki region, and the damage appears to be so enormous that it can hardly be assessed. The so-called international "emergency" is, at present at least, something formless and unproved; but these "emergencies" of natural disaster expose their misery as the most concrete of facts before our eyes.

Tensai to kokubō by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

According to the newspapers, America has a plan to set up floating airfields on the Pacific as stepping stones for a transoceanic flight. It may be untrue, but for Americans it is perfectly possible. And if that is possible, then the setting up of floating observation stations on the ocean can hardly be dismissed as a castle in the air drawn by scholars. I imagine it is the sort of thing that will probably be common sense fifty or a hundred years from now. As mankind advances, I think patriotism and the Japanese spirit ought to advance as well. To stake one's life and charge the enemy's camp through smoke and shellfire is certainly a noble Japanese spirit; but to unite as a people in ordinary times and take proper scientific measures against a natural enemy stronger than this country or that is also, it seems to me, something we might well hope for as one aspect of an evolution of the Japanese spirit suited to the present age. It is all very well to display that sort of patriotism in a great hurry only when a disaster has already happened; but for the patriotism of a scientific and civilized people of the twentieth century, who are not insects or birds or beasts, there ought, I think, to be a somewhat different and somewhat more rational form of expression.

Tensai to kokubō by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

The fact that the more civilization advances, the more violent the disasters wrought by the fury of nature become.

A passage in which Torahiko, who was also a physicist, sets out a principle of disaster preparedness. It points out that the more civilization advances, the greater the damage from natural disasters, and it is still widely quoted in discussions of disaster prevention.
