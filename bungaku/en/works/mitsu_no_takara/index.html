About this work

One of the few plays for children that Akutagawa wrote. It is a piece in three acts turning on three treasures — boots that fly a thousand ri, a cloak that makes the wearer invisible, and a sword that will cut through iron — in which a prince, neatly palmed off with counterfeits by a band of thieves, goes to rescue the princess all the same. The plot turns on the idea that it is not the power of the treasures but a man with no treasures at all that moves another's heart, and at the end the prince declares to the audience that they cannot go on living in a country inside a fairy tale. Borrowing the form of a fairy tale, Akutagawa sets in it his own device for stepping out of one.

Summary

In a forest three thieves are quarreling over some treasures. Boots that fly a thousand ri at a bound, a cloak that makes the wearer invisible, a sword that will cut through iron — all of them, to look at, no better than old junk. A prince passing by offers to settle the quarrel, hands over his own fine boots and cloak and sword in exchange for the three treasures, and goes on his way. The thieves jeer that they have made an excellent bargain. In the taproom of an inn the prince hears a rumor: the princess of that country is to be married off to the Black King. The Black King has presented the three genuine treasures, and the greedy king has promised his daughter in exchange. The prince announces that he will rescue the princess, tries at once to fly in the boots, lands on his backside, and is laughed at by the company. Even so he gets into the castle disguised as a servant and stands before the princess. The boots have holes in them and will not fly a single chō; but with the princess beside him, a thousand ri or two thousand would not surprise him. The cloak does not hide his figure, but it has at least hidden the prince in him. When the Black King draws his sword at last and cuts the prince's blade in two with ease, he finds his opponent laughing still, and throws his own sword away — it was a mistake, he says, to think that with three treasures he could win the princess.

Characters

  • The prince　He buys the three treasures from the three thieves and sets out to rescue the princess
  • The three thieves　They are quarreling over the three treasures in the forest
  • The Black King　The king who has carried off the princess, and who was to have been the villain of the story
  • The princess　The princess who has been carried off

The opening (original text)

1 A forest. Three thieves are quarreling over some treasures. The treasures are boots that fly a thousand ri at a bound, a cloak that makes the wearer invisible, and a sword that will cut iron clean in two — though to look at, every one of them seems no more than an old piece of junk. FIRST THIEF. Hand over that cloak. SECOND THIEF. Never mind the cloak. It's that sword you had better hand over. — Hello, you've stolen my boots. THIRD THIEF. Aren't these boots mine? It's you who have stolen what belongs to me. FIRST THIEF. Very well, then; I shall keep this cloak myself. SECOND THIEF. Curse you! As if I would let a fellow like you have it. FIRST THIEF. So you have struck me, have you? — Hello, and now you've stolen my sword as well? THIRD THIEF. What is this, you cloak-thief! The three of them fall to fighting in earnest.

The ending (original text)

PRINCE. Just so. (Turning to the audience.) Ladies and gentlemen! The three of us have woken up. A Black King like a devil, and a prince who owns three treasures — such things exist only in fairy tales. And since we have woken up, we cannot go on living in a country inside a fairy tale. Out of the mist before us a wider world is rising. Let us go out together from this world of roses and fountains into that one. A wider world! An uglier, a more beautiful — a greater world of fairy tale! What waits for us in that world, suffering or delight, we do not know at all. We know only that we are going forward into it, like a gallant company of soldiers. (December 1922)
