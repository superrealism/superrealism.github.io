About this work

The work that made Takayama Chogyū's name: a historical novel submitted anonymously to a prize competition of the Yomiuri Shimbun in 1894, which won and was serialized. Drawing on "Yokobue" in the tenth book of The Tale of the Heike, it turns on the doomed love of Saitō Takiguchi Tokiyori, a warrior of the Taira, and Yokobue, a serving woman of the Kenreimon'in. Forbidden the match by his father, Tokiyori leaves the world and becomes the monk Takiguchi Nyūdō on Mount Kōya, and with the fall of the Taira at hand tries, in keeping with the dying wish of his lord Shigemori, to admonish the fugitive Lord Koremori and send him back to Yashima. The conflict of love and loyalty, sung in a dignified elegant style with the seven-five metre woven in: one of the central historical novels of the Meiji period, which raised the young Chogyū's name at a stroke.

Summary

In the spring of Jishō, at a flower-viewing banquet of the arrogant Heike, Saitō Takiguchi Tokiyori, a bluntly soldierly warrior in the service of Komatsu-dono (Taira no Shigemori), falls, at a single glance, into the first love of his life — for Yokobue, a serving-woman of Kenreimon-in who performs a dance at the banquet. Staking his honor as a warrior, he sends her letter after letter, but never once does an answer come from Yokobue. In truth, Ashige Jirō Shigekage, another retainer of the same Komatsu-dono, has secretly set his heart on Yokobue as well, and through an old woman spreads malicious slander against Tokiyori, even carrying his lies to Tokiyori's own father. Realizing that to be lost in love is a warrior's shame, Tokiyori resolves instead to make Yokobue his wife, and begs his father, Saemon Shigeyori, for his blessing — but is fiercely refused for the difference in their stations, and, despairing of the world, resolves to renounce it and become a monk, whereupon his father disowns him. Before casting aside the world, Tokiyori takes his leave of his lord Shigemori. Shigemori, sensing the coming fall of the Heike, leaves Tokiyori these last words: "Koremori is weak of heart; should some crisis come, he may lose himself in love for wife and child and lay bare the shame of our whole house. You alone are the one I can trust." Meanwhile Yokobue, learning that it was her own coldness that drove Tokiyori to renounce the world, dies steeped in deep remorse. Passing through Saga, Tokiyori climbs Mount Kōya and becomes "Takiguchi Nyūdō." Three years later, Shigemori's eldest son, Lord Koremori, having slipped away from Yashima, comes to him accompanied by Ashige Shigekage. To Koremori, who has fled his post driven only by the wish to see his wife and child once more, Tokiyori, his heart hardened by his late lord's dying charge, tearfully admonishes him — "You must share life and death with your own house" — and turns him away without so much as a night's lodging. Koremori and Shigekage drown themselves at Waka-no-ura, and Tokiyori, as though following after them, disembowels himself where he sits, upright, at the foot of a pine. A man who cast aside the world for love, scattering at last in martyrdom to loyalty toward his lord's house — against the backdrop of the fall of the Heike, this masterwork of Meiji historical fiction sings, in an elevated classical style, of the tragic fates of Tokiyori, Yokobue, and Koremori alike.

The opening (original text)

One With no way to know, amid the pleasures of this spring of Jishō, of the sorrow that the autumn of Juei would soon bring — those who, six years hence, tracing back this dream of days now passing, would wring with tears the sleeves of their court robes, must surely find that tonight's revelry, too, becomes a tear shed in troubled, unforgetting sleep.

Takiguchi nyūdō by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The next morning, when a fisherman of Waka-no-ura came to the shore, he found, at the foot of a pine, a single monk who had cut open his own belly and died. Unable, it seems, even in death, to bear soiling it, he had hung his black-dyed robe upon a nearby pine branch, and wore beneath it only a white robe of bleached cloth; at his feet lay the deep waters of the sea god's realm, and in the hand that held the blade there was not so much as a hairsbreadth of disorder — gripping reverse-handed the scabbard-knife, crimson-grooved and smeared with the paste of his own blood, he sat upright, his knees never once giving way, in the posture, surely, of some warrior of renown, come at last to his end. Ah — this was none other than Saitō Takiguchi Tokiyori: a man who, having lost all hope in love, cast aside the world, only to find the world would not cast him aside in turn, who bore upon his shadow the fate of his lord's house through twenty-six years adrift upon the waves of its rise and fall — and this, in truth, was his very last moment in this fleeting world.

Takiguchi nyūdō by Takayama Chogyū (Aozora Bunko)
