About this work

A full-scale essay on civilization written by Terada Torahiko late in life, published in Tōyō Shichō in 1935, just before his death. It argues widely and deeply, from the standpoint of a geophysicist, how the Japanese have seen and responded to the nature of Japan. Noting that the country has two faces — rich and mild, and at the same time productive of harsh disasters such as earthquakes, typhoons and floods — he argues that long association with that nature has nurtured a distinctive view of it in every aspect of Japanese art, religion and daily life. A central essay compressing Terada's thought, which does not separate human beings from nature but grasps them as one organism.

Summary

The topic assigned to him, "the Japanese view of nature," Terada Torahiko begins, seems on the surface a perfectly plain and simple one, but upon closer reflection proves surprisingly ambiguous. The face of nature varies astonishingly from place to place across the earth, and "the nature of Japan" itself looks entirely different in Kyūshū than it does in the Tōhoku region. In the same way, the content of the word "Japanese" is far from uniform. Even so, Terada attempts to consider the nature-view of the "Japanese in general," taken as a summation of these regional particularities. The argument rests, at bottom, on the premise that human beings have been nurtured, over long ages, within the bosom of nature, and shaped to adapt to their environment — and that man and nature are therefore, by their very origin, one inseparable organism. Terada fixes his attention on the two-sided character of Japan's nature: rich in seasonal change, bestowing abundant blessings, and yet at the same time visiting fierce calamities upon the land — earthquakes, typhoons, floods — and argues, ranging freely over concrete particulars, how this two-sidedness has shaped Japanese art, religion, language, and daily life in every respect. It is careless, he says, to imagine that merely copying Western dress, food, housing, and thought could change the very character and climate of the Japanese people; rather, it is the mission of the Japanese to recognize every one of Japan's own particularities, to put them to use, and to adapt them to their surrounding environment. "If the cherry blossoms were to vanish from the world, the world would grow that much lonelier for it" — a piece in which Terada's thinking on nature, in his final years, crystallizes quietly into its finished form.

The opening (original text)

Foreword The meaning of the topic assigned to me, "the Japanese view of nature," seems at first glance a perfectly plain one, but upon closer reflection proves, in fact, a surprisingly ambiguous matter. It seems to require a certain amount of preliminary examination and analysis before I can even take up my pen. What this topic seems, in the main, to signify is how the Japanese see and respond to their own environment, "the nature of Japan," and what particular character this bears when set beside the ways in which foreigners, not Japanese, see and respond to the nature of their own respective countries. And, as a secondary matter, the question of how foreigners' views of Japan's nature differ from those of the Japanese themselves may also come into play.

Nihonjin no shizenkan by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

This is something of a digression, but to distinguish races by skin color alone is a classification that comes very close to meaninglessness. From the standpoint that regards man and nature together as a single organism, it seems to me that the Chinese and the Japanese are, in fact, by no means so closely related a people as is often assumed. And to lump them together in a single phrase, "Orientals," is likewise a rather empty way of speaking. Within that broad region called the Orient, the climate of Japan and its people form, after all, an "island" entirely set apart from what surrounds it. I believe that it is the mission of the Japanese, and their very reason for existing, and their contribution to the healthy progress of humanity as a whole, to recognize every one of Japan's own particularities, to put them to use, and to adapt them to the surrounding environment. If the cherry blossoms were to vanish from the world, the world would grow that much lonelier for it.
