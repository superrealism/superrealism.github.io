About this work

Mori Ōgai's translation of Andersen's autobiographical novel of 1834, a central work of Meiji translated literature. He worked at it for nine years, from 1892 to 1901, a little at a time on nights and holidays between military duties. The gorgeous literary style, weaving Chinese diction into an elegant classical Japanese, was said even to surpass the original, and it became the rare case of a translation more famous than the book it came from. It draws a poor boy born in Rome making his way in the world as an improvisatore, together with the light and landscape of Italy. The longing for southern Europe recorded here held Ueda Bin, Nagai Kafū, Kinoshita Mokutarō and other later writers for a long time.

Summary

Antonio, born in a house facing the Piazza Barberini in Rome, grows up with his poor widowed mother. The disposition of pleasing others and taking pleasure in it — the seed of what was to follow him all his life — is already visible in his childhood memories. Losing his mother in an accident, the boy is taken in by the noble house of Borghese as they pass by, and is educated under the protection of an uncle. His native gift for improvised verse astonishes people; but at the same time he is worn thin between the shame of one who receives charity and the constraint of noble society. From Bernardo, the friend of his boyhood, he is at last parted for good over a woman. Several women appear before Antonio. The singer Annunziata takes his heart, but that love is torn apart in the quarrel with his friend and becomes the cause of his being driven out of Rome. The blind girl Lara, a dancer of Venice, a nobleman's daughter — through the sunlight and the festivals of the south, the noise of Naples, the eruption of Vesuvius, the ruins of Pompeii, the canals of Venice, he wanders without a settled place, winning a name as a poet. Fame does not slake the thirst of his heart, and the memory of a lost love comes back again and again. At the end of his long wandering Antonio comes to the Blue Grotto on Capri. Into that place where the two of them once came, he rows in a small boat with Lara. Passing under a cleft in the rock scarcely above the water, the light comes up from the bottom of the sea and dyes the whole cave blue, and at every stroke of the oar the spray scatters like the petals of a red rose. Lara puts her palms together and is lost in thought. Before the tide rises and closes the mouth of the cave the boat comes out, and before them lay the boundless sea and behind them the stone gate of the grotto growing narrower — with that scene the story closes.

Characters

  • Antonio　An improvisatore born in a poor house in Rome. The narrator of the story
  • My mother　Who brings Antonio up and dies early
  • Bernardo　The friend of his boyhood, who becomes his enemy over a woman
  • Annunziata　The singer whom Antonio loves
  • Lara　A blind girl, who visits the Blue Grotto with him at the close
  • Fra Martino　The monk who hears his mother's confession

The opening (original text)

My earliest surroundings Everyone who has been in Rome knows the Piazza Barberini — the great square with its beautiful fountain, where the figure of the Triton with his conch is made to blow the water several feet into the air. Those who have never been in Rome will have seen the square in engravings. It is a pity only that in such pictures the house at the corner of the Via Felice is not to be seen. From the wall of the house I speak of, three spouts pour their water into a stone basin. That house has for me an uncommon interest; and why is that? Because it was there that I was born.

Sokkyō shijin by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The one point of light at the mouth of the cave was suddenly gone, and a second boat came in. It seemed to rise out of the bottom of the water. A third and a fourth followed. And of all who gathered there, whatever the faith they held, whether old or new, there was not one who, meeting this marvel of nature, did not praise the goodness of the Father in heaven. The boatmen cried out suddenly that the tide was rising, and began to ply their oars in haste; for they feared that the flood would close the mouth of the cave. The boats of the visitors came out of the grotto one after another, and we saw before us the boundless sea, and behind us the stone gate of the Blue Grotto growing narrower and narrower.

Sokkyō shijin by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)
