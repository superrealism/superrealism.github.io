About this work

A novella from Sōseki's early period, published in the magazine Hototogisu in April 1906. A blunt, hot-headed young man from Tokyo takes a post teaching mathematics at a middle school in Shikoku and finds himself squaring off against his scheming colleagues, in a story carried by sharp talk and headlong action. Its clear-cut, good-against-bad plotting and humorous voice have kept it among the most popular of all Sōseki's works since the day it appeared.

Summary

Botchan, reckless since childhood and looked at askance by his family, was doted on by one person only: Kiyo, the old maidservant, who looked forward to great things for him. Graduating from a physics academy, he takes a post teaching mathematics at a middle school in Shikoku. Unable to settle to country ways, he is at once plagued by the pupils' pranks, and drawn as well into the schemes of crafty colleagues — Redshirt, the head teacher, and his hanger-on, the Clown. Falling in with the blunt and upright Porcupine, Botchan grows angrier and angrier at Redshirt's underhanded conduct, until at last the two of them lie in wait for Redshirt and the Clown and give them a thrashing; then he resigns his post and goes back to Tokyo. He takes work as a technician on the city tramway and begins living with Kiyo again — but she dies soon afterwards of pneumonia, and is buried at Botchan's family temple.

Characters

  • Botchan　The narrator, reckless and straightforward. He goes from Tokyo to a middle school in Shikoku to teach mathematics
  • Kiyo　The old maidservant of Botchan's family, who dotes on him and never stops believing in his future
  • Porcupine (Hotta)　A fellow mathematics teacher. Blunt and upright, he and Botchan take to each other at once
  • Redshirt　The crafty head teacher, who affects literary tastes and works behind the scenes for his own advantage
  • The Clown (Yoshikawa)　The drawing master, who fawns on Redshirt

The opening (original text)

Reckless by inheritance, I have been getting the worst of it ever since I was a child. While I was in elementary school I jumped from the second storey of the school building and could not stand for a week. Someone may ask why I did anything so foolish. There was no great reason. I was putting my head out of a window of the newly built second storey when a classmate, joking, called out that for all my swagger I could never jump down from there — coward, coward. That was why.

Botchan by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I have forgotten to speak of Kiyo. — When I reached Tokyo I did not even go to my lodgings but burst in with my bag still in my hand, calling, "Kiyo, I'm back!" — and she let the tears fall in great drops and said, "Oh, Botchan, how good of you to come home so soon." I was so glad myself that I told her I would not go to the country again, that I would keep house in Tokyo with Kiyo. Afterwards, through someone's good offices, I became a technician on the city tramway. The salary is twenty-five yen and the rent six. Kiyo seemed perfectly content though the house had no proper entrance hall; but the pity of it is that this February she caught pneumonia and died. The day before she died she called me and said, "Botchan, I beg of you — when Kiyo dies, bury her in your temple. I shall be waiting in the grave and looking forward to your coming." And so Kiyo's grave is at the Yōgenji in Kobinata.

Botchan by Natsume Sōseki (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Ever since I was a child, I have been getting myself into trouble through the reckless streak I inherited from my parents.

One of the most famous opening lines in Japan, in which the narrator introduces himself through his inherited recklessness. In a single breath, the Edokko-spirited protagonist springs to life.

And that is why Kiyo's grave is at Yōgenji Temple in Kobinata.

The final line. A narrative voice that has pushed through in true Edokko fashion closes quietly, leaving behind only his affection for Kiyo, the old family maid.

“You have a straight and honest nature.”

The words with which Kiyo, the family's maid, praises the young narrator, whom his family dislikes. Kiyo's steadfast faith in a boy no one else appreciates becomes the warmth running through the whole story.

When I was in primary school, I jumped from the second floor of the school building and couldn't stand up for a week. Some may ask why I did such a reckless thing. There was no deep reason. I was sticking my head out of a second-floor window of the new building when one of my classmates said as a joke, “You can brag all you like, but you can't jump down from there. Coward!”

The episode that follows the opening line about the recklessness Botchan inherited from his parents. The boyhood leap from a second-floor window, made because he was teased, tells his straightforward character in a single breath.
