What is Mujōkan (The Sense of Impermanence)?

Category
Other

The Buddhist-derived sense that everything in this world is ceaselessly changing and nothing remains still — this feeling of "mujō" (impermanence) forms the basic tone running beneath Japanese literature, above all medieval literature. For the people of the medieval period, who witnessed repeated warfare, natural disaster, and the turnover of power, impermanence was a pressing reality. Kamo no Chōmei opens his essay "Hōjōki" with, "The flowing river never stops, and yet the water never stays the same," contemplating the fleeting nature of people and their dwellings, and the war tale "The Tale of the Heike" sings the fall of the once-glorious Taira clan to the melody of impermanence: "The sound of the bell of Gion Shōja echoes the impermanence of all things." A gaze that stares straight at the transience of this world runs through the musings of Yoshida Kenkō's "Tsurezuregusa" ("Essays in Idleness") as well. This sense of impermanence, which finds beauty precisely in the things that pass away, went on to develop into the yūgen (mysterious depth) of medieval renga and noh and the wabi and sabi of early-modern haikai, deeply shaping the Japanese aesthetic sensibility and view of life and death.
