What is Tenkō Bungaku (Literature of Ideological Conversion)?

Category
Form / Genre

A body of work, from 1933 on, written by writers who, under suppression, abandoned their Communist convictions, taking their own experience of conversion (tenkō) as its subject. The trigger came that year when Sano Manabu and Nabeyama Sadachika, leaders of the Japan Communist Party held in prison, issued a statement, "To Our Fellow Defendants," repudiating the movement's course, and many of the writers who had been arrested likewise declared their conversion, one after another. Murayama Tomoyoshi's "Byakuya" ("White Night"), Shimaki Kensaku's "Rai" ("Leprosy"), and Takami Jun's "Kokyū Wasureubeki" ("Old Friends Not to Be Forgotten") belong to this body of work, and Nakano Shigeharu's "Mura no Ie" ("The Village House," 1935), depicting a protagonist who falls silent before a father urging him to lay down his pen, stands as its representative achievement. This writing, staring fixedly at the shame of capitulation, the gap between thought and life, and relations with family, came, as a result, to draw close to the tradition of the I-novel, digging into the author's own self. Even so, writing the reality of the suppression directly was not permitted under censorship, and these works were often forced into oblique and contorted forms of expression. It spread as if to fill the vacuum left after the organized destruction of proletarian literature, and, together with the Nihon Roman-ha that rose to prominence around the same time, it shapes one facet of the literature of the 1930s as it moved toward the war.
