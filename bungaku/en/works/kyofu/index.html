About this work

A short story drawing a man who suffers from a phobia of railways, published in the Osaka Mainichi Shimbun in January 1913. Get on a train or a streetcar and the blood rushes to his head until he feels he will die — carrying the malady a friend has named Eisenbahnkrankheit, the narrator must nevertheless get from Kyoto to Osaka for his conscription examination. An unusual early work by Tanizaki, in which the comedy of trying to board with the help of whisky lives together with real urgency.

Summary

Lodging in Kiyamachi and keeping up a round of drinking and late nights, "I" realize that an old ailment of mine has come back. The moment I board a train, the blood in my whole body surges up into my skull, a cold sweat breaks out, and it feels as though my head is about to split open — a nervous complaint my friend N had named "railway sickness." But to sit my conscription physical I must reach a village office along the Hanshin line and transfer my registration there before the day is out. Telling myself the streetcar will surely be all right, I sit on a bench at Gojō Station and cannot bring myself to rise. I gulp down whiskey, trying to numb my nerves with drink, press a handkerchief packed with ice to my forehead, and make it through the ticket gate — but at the sound of the train's whistle I bolt back out of the station once more. The clock reaches two.

Characters

  • I (T)　A man afflicted with railway sickness, trying to travel to Osaka for his conscription physical
  • Mr. K　A friend who speaks to me at Gojō Station and becomes my travelling companion
  • Mr. A　A doctor of about thirty-two or three whom Mr. K has brought along

The opening (original text)

It was, I believe, around the beginning of June, while I was lodging in Kiyamachi and keeping up drink and late nights nearly every day, that I was seized once again by that malady. — Mind you, I recall being struck by it more than once before as well, back when I lived in Tokyo, but by giving up drink, rubbing myself down with cold water, and taking brain tonics, I had at last managed to recover fully. Then, coming to Kyoto and slipping back into that same disorderly life, I found, before I quite knew it, that it had returned.

Kyōfu by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The whistle shrieked, and the train at last began to move. "So this is it — I'm going to die," I murmured to myself. The feelings of a condemned man mounting the scaffold, I thought, could be no different from this. "Well, Mr. A, what do you think — will Mr. T pass his physical?" Mr. K put the question. "Hmm, let's see. You look likely to be taken, I'd say. Plump as you are, with such a fine build." Outside the windows on either side, the streets of Kyoto fell away, and the green leaves, the trees, the roads, the hills of the outskirts went rushing past. And it was only then that a faint relief began to stir in my breast — the thought that I might, after all, reach Osaka safely.
