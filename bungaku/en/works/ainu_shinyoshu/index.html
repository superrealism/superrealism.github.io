About this work

Chiri Yukie, a young Ainu woman, wrote down in roman letters thirteen kamuy yukar — songs of the gods — as her grandmother's generation told them, and translated them into Japanese herself: the first book of Ainu literature compiled by an Ainu. The preface, beginning with the words that long ago this wide Hokkaido was a land of freedom for our ancestors, is famous as a piercing appeal for a people's language and life as they vanish. The day after she finished the manuscript, Yukie died at nineteen.

Summary

"Long ago this wide Hokkaido was the free country of our ancestors" — the preface begins by drawing the free life of forebears who hunted bears and fished and lived singing with the seasons. But that peace is long past. Decades have gone by since the dream was broken; the hills and fields have become villages and the villages towns, and the kinsmen who remain are burdened with the sad name of "a vanishing people." If nothing else, she wished to leave in writing some of the stories her ancestors told and delighted in — and with that wish Chiri Yukie set down thirteen divine songs. Included are the owl god's own song, "Silver droplets fall, fall all around"; the fox god's "Towatowato"; the hare god's "Sanpaya terekay"; the tale of the pond-mussel god, and more — first-person songs in which the gods of animals and of nature become "I" and tell their own experience. The owl god takes upon himself the single arrow loosed by a poor boy, comes down to his house, and brings the house wealth. Those with an evil heart are punished and the merciful are rewarded — and in these simple tales of cause and effect there lives the Ainu view of a world in which everything has a soul. Refrains such as "Shirokanipe ranran pishkan" run through the whole book, and the original text in roman letters is set beside the translation, so that the sound of the oral telling comes through as it was.

The opening (original text)

Long ago this wide Hokkaido was the free country of our ancestors. Embraced by beautiful nature, like guileless infants, they lived easily and happily; and truly they were nature's favorites — what happy people they must have been. In winter they kicked through the deep snow that covered the woods and fields and, making nothing of a cold that froze heaven and earth, crossed mountain after mountain to hunt bears; in summer, on green waves where a cool wind swam, with the song of the white gulls for a friend, they floated a boat like a leaf and fished the whole day long.

Ainu shin'yōshū by Chiri Yukie (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"Oh, the poor things — it has been so hot that the beds of the pond mussels have dried up and they are wanting water. What can have happened? It looks almost as if they had been trodden on...." — saying so, she gathered every one of us up and put us in a butterbur leaf, and put us into a clean lake. In the clear cold water we recovered our strength completely and grew very strong. So one of the pond mussels told the story.

Ainu shin'yōshū by Chiri Yukie (Aozora Bunko)
