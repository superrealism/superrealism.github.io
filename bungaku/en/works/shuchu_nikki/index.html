About this work

A novella taking the form of a diary written over drink by Ōkawa Imazō, who keeps a private school on a small island in the Inland Sea. Five years before, living quietly with his wife and child as a primary-school headmaster, he was worn down by the degradation of his mother and sister and their constant demands for money, until at last his mother stole a hundred yen of subscription funds from him. Driven to it, he put his hand to the money in a wallet he had picked up, and from there rolled down to the irreparable end of his wife's suicide. Among Doppo's short works this one has a dark force, tracing in a first person mixed with drunkenness how a weak, well-meaning man is destroyed.

Summary

Ōkawa Imazō, who keeps a private school on the island of Umashima in the Inland Sea, begins over his drink to write of what happened five years ago. As a primary-school headmaster he had the trust of the district, took his wife Omasa through the good offices of the old man of the Masuya, had a child, and lived at peace. But about the time of the war with China his mother and sister, who had opened a private lodging house in Akasaka, went to the bad with soldiers for customers and began coming to him for money again and again. One Sunday he cannot refuse a demand for five yen, and his wife pawns her sash to raise three; but his mother is angry that it is not enough, declares that she disowns him, and goes away. Afterward the hundred yen of subscription money for the rebuilding of the school, which he had put in his desk drawer, is gone. Understanding that his mother has taken it, Imazō goes to Shinmachi to have it out with her, only to be browbeaten and to come home without a word. On the way back, at his wits' end, he picks up a handbag on the moor at Aoyama with three hundred yen in it, tells himself he is only "borrowing for the present," takes out a hundred, and fills the hole in the accounts. Days of remorse follow, and then one night his wife, having found the bag, throws herself into the well with the child on her back. Imazō resigns, holds the funeral with his gratuity, and leaves Tokyo with what is left for traveling money. Drifting west and west, he washes up on the island, where the girl Otsuyu comes to love him, and where he writes his diary while driving off the past with drink. According to the compiler's note, the day after the diary ends Imazō fell from a boat and drowned, and Otsuyu bore his child four months later.

Characters

  • Ōkawa Imazō　A man who keeps a private school on the island of Umashima in the Inland Sea; formerly a primary-school headmaster
  • Omasa　Imazō's wife, sickly and quiet
  • Imazō's mother　She keeps a private lodging house in Akasaka with soldiers for customers, and presses her son for money
  • Omitsu　Imazō's younger sister
  • The old man of the Masuya　A leading man of the town who trusted Imazō and even found him his wife
  • Otsuyu　The girl on Umashima who comes to love Imazō

The opening (original text)

3 May (1897) "I wonder what became of that man" — it is a thing often said; when four or five people get together and talk of old times, the talk slips of itself to someone who has dropped out of sight. This Ōkawa Imazō is very likely being spoken of in just that way even now. "And what has become of Ōkawa?" the old man of the Masuya begins. "He may be dead by now," someone answers without much interest. "There was never a man so much to be pitied as that one," says the old man in his usual mournful voice. That they should pity me is more than I deserve. But, fortunately or unfortunately, this man Ōkawa is alive yet, and remarkably well at that, and there is no telling for how many decades more he may go on breathing in this world. I am, if you please, no more than thirty-two.

The ending (original text)

Otsuyu lived for the child, the child was held by every islander in turn, and Ōkawa attained what he had wished for and came to rest in the dark, wooded graveyard deep in the island. It seems to me, as compiler, that the unhappy Ōkawa cannot be known in the whole from his diary; for the diary was written by Ōkawa himself, and in it he sets down little of his life on Umashima. Therefore, in coming to know him, we must of course know his past through his diary, but must also guess at his daily life on the island. The compiler will call him an unhappy man, and cannot bring himself to say more; and he too pitied himself, and would say at the slightest thing, "Ah, what an unhappy man." It was Ōkawa himself who gave it the title "A Diary Written in Drink." That it should be so titled is pitiful enough, and more so when one considers that in fact he never took up his pen except after drinking. In reading this diary, that is the fact above all to be kept in mind. Omasa went before him with the child on her back; Otsuyu was left behind him with a child on hers. Which of them is the more unhappy? Which the more pitiful?
