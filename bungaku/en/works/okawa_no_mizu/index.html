About this work

A short piece Akutagawa contributed to "Kokoro no Hana" while a student at Tokyo Imperial University, one of the earliest of his writings to appear in print. The author, who grew up near the Hyappongui on the Ryōgoku side, sets down his attachment to the water of the Sumida — the Ōkawa. The color of the water, the sound of the sculls of the ferryboats, the lamps of the houses along the bank, the smell of the water. In the closing sentence — that if he were asked the smell of "Tokyo" he would answer, the smell of the water of the Ōkawa — the sensibility of his later work is already there. The one line added at the end about a ferry that has since been discontinued is not easily forgotten.

Summary

I was born in a district near the bank of the Ōkawa. Going out of the house and through the lanes of Yokoami with their many black fences, one comes out at once on the bank at the Hyappongui. From childhood until I finished middle school I looked at that river almost every day. I saw the water and the boats and the bridges and the sandbars, and the lives of the hurried people who are born on the water and live on the water. Even the smell of the river that I caught without meaning to on a midsummer afternoon, treading the burning sand on my way to a swimming lesson, comes back to me now, with the years, as something familiar. Why I should love that river so, why I should feel a boundless tenderness for its muddy, tepid water, I find hard to explain even to myself. I only know that from long ago, whenever I looked at that water, I felt a consolation and a desolation that made me want to shed tears, as though I had gone far from the world I live in and entered a country of longing and remembrance. Ryōgoku, Azumabashi, Eitaibashi — the color and the sound of the water as I went up and down the river looking at it. I shall never forget the day when, leaning on the gunwale of a ferryboat and gazing over the water at dusk, I shed tears in spite of myself at a great red moon rising over the dark houses. Every city, they say, has its own smell. If I am asked the smell of "Tokyo," I answer without hesitation: the smell of the water of the Ōkawa.

The opening (original text)

I was born in a district near the bank of the Ōkawa. Going out of the house and through the lanes of Yokoami, covered over with the young leaves of the chinquapins and lined with black fences, one comes out at once on the bank at the Hyappongui, from which the whole breadth of the river can be seen. From my childhood until I finished middle school I looked at that river almost every day. I saw the water and the boats and the bridges and the sandbars, and the lives of the hurried people who are born on the water and live on the water. Even the smell of the river's water, which I caught without meaning to catch it as I went by on a midsummer afternoon, treading the burning sand on my way to a swimming lesson, now seems to me, with the years, something familiar to remember. Why is it that I love that river so? Why do I feel a boundless tenderness for that tepid, muddy water of the Ōkawa?

The ending (original text)

"Every city has a smell peculiar to itself. The smell of Florence is the smell of white iris and dust and mist and the varnish of old paintings." (Merezhkovsky) If there were anyone to ask me the smell of "Tokyo," I should have no hesitation at all in answering: the smell of the water of the Ōkawa. And not the smell alone. The color of the water of the Ōkawa, the sound of the water of the Ōkawa, must be the color and the voice of the "Tokyo" that I love. It is because the Ōkawa exists that I love "Tokyo," and because "Tokyo" exists that I love living. (January 1912) Afterward I heard that the Ichinohashi ferry had been discontinued. It cannot be long before the Mikurabashi ferry goes the same way.
