What is Bōken Shōsetsu (Adventure Fiction)?

Category
Form / Genre

Popular fiction whose themes are journeys to unknown lands, castaways, exploration, and struggles against danger, depicting the exploits of a hero through courage and ingenuity. With Defoe's "Robinson Crusoe" (1719) as one of its sources, it became a major genre, above all for young readers, in the nineteenth century, when Stevenson's "Treasure Island" (1883) and the works of Jules Verne were widely read. In Japan it was introduced through translations and adaptations from the middle of the Meiji era, and Morita Shiken's "Jūgo Shōnen" ("Fifteen Boys," 1896), translated from an English version of Verne's "Two Years' Vacation," won acclaim. In original writing, Oshikawa Shunrō thrilled boys with "Kaitō Bōken Kitan: Kaitei Gunkan" (1900), the story of an undersea battleship secretly built on a remote island, and became the pioneer of Japanese adventure fiction. Shunrō served as editor-in-chief of Hakubunkan's magazine "Bōken Sekai" and later founded "Bukyō Sekai," widening the stage for adventure fiction. In the Shōwa era the works of Yamanaka Minetarō and Minami Yōichirō won popularity in boys' magazines such as "Shōnen Kurabu," and Edogawa Ranpo serialized in that magazine "Shin Takarajima" (1940), depicting the exploits of boys cast away on an island in the South Seas. Many works, such as Unno Jūza's "Kasei Heidan" (1939), were bound up with science fiction, and in wartime military adventure stories in line with national policy increased as well. It is a genre that leads on to postwar reading for young people and to science fiction and action novels.
