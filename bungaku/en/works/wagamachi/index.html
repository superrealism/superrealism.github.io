About this work

A short story Oda Sakunosuke published in Bungei in 1942. Set in downtown Osaka, it draws Taayan, a man whose lifelong pride is that he took part in the punishing construction of the Benguet road on Luzon in the Philippines, and those who come after him. The life of the stubborn, single-minded Takichi, called Tarzan, and a deep attachment to the life of ordinary Osaka people, set down in Oda's own light and feeling hand: a central piece of his Osaka writing.

Summary

At the end of the Meiji era, in the mountains of Luzon in the Philippines, an Osaka man named Takichi — known by everyone as "Ta-ayan" — took part in the perilous, backbreaking construction of the Benguet Road linking Manila and Baguio. As laborer after laborer fell to his death in the ravines below or was struck down by tropical disease, Takichi lived out the rest of his life taking unwavering pride in having seen that brutal work through to the end. Back in the working-class quarter of Osaka, Takichi, a stubborn, single-minded, but warm-hearted man, makes his living alongside the people of the town. As the years pass and his blood carries on through his daughter and her children, down to his grandchildren, Takichi's pet phrases and his way of living are passed on to his descendants without their even realizing it. The story follows, across the years, the figure of Takichi — known even in his old age by the nickname "Tarzan" — and the joys and sorrows of the ordinary people of Osaka around him. In time his grandson Jirō comes to feel, in the shape of Takichi's own life, that it is now his own fate to cross the sea to Manila in his turn. A representative piece in which Oda, with a light touch and deep feeling, depicts his profound attachment to the town of Osaka and the people who live there.

Characters

  • Takichi (Ta-ayan; Tarzan)　A man of Osaka's working-class quarter. A stubborn, single-minded man who lives out his life taking pride in having seen through the brutal construction of the Benguet Road in the Philippines in his youth
  • Jirō　Takichi's grandson, carrying on his blood. Comes to feel, in Ta-ayan's own life, his own fate to cross to Manila
  • Kimie　Jirō's wife. Bears some faint resemblance to Ta-ayan

The opening (original text)

One: Meiji Of the Benguet Road linking Manila and Baguio, the eighty-kilometer stretch cut through the mountains between Tagpan and the summit of Baguio was so brutal an undertaking that Major Kennon, the officer supervising the work, was made a general the very day it was completed. The laborers had to work their way in zigzags up a five-thousand-foot cliff face on the flank of Mount Benguet, and whenever a squall came, landslides and rockfalls broke loose in an instant, crushing men against the rocks in the ravine below like so many geckos underfoot.

Wagamachi by Oda Sakunosuke (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Glancing suddenly at Kimie's profile, Jirō started, thinking, ah, how like Ta-ayan she looks — and in that same instant, the thought that it was now their turn to go to Manila swept unbidden through his chest. Ta-ayan had never once, in so many words, told him to go to Manila and salvage an American ship. But seeing the way the old man had died was, without any need for reasoning, the same as being ordered to do it; no — it seemed to come to him almost as a physical certainty that this had already been decided the moment he took Kimie for his wife — and Jirō stood for a while, gazing up at the framed photograph.
