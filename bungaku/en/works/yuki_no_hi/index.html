About this work

A short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the magazine Bungakukai in March 1893. A girl born into a great house in a mountain village and raised in the affection of the aunt who brought her up tells in the first person, in remorse, how at fifteen, on a day of falling snow, she ran in secret to her teacher at the school and came to grief. The first stirring of love and the gossip of the world, the aunt's severity in trying to cut it off, and one irrecoverable day, laid over the falling snow. A lyrical piece with strong autobiographical colour.

Summary

Otama, the only child born into the distinguished Usui family of a mountain village, loses her parents early and is raised by her aunt — who herself had returned to the family home after losing her husband — with a devotion greater even than that for a child of her own. At fifteen, in the winter, Otama comes to be doted upon like a younger sister by the kind schoolteacher Katsuragi Ichirō, who has come from Tokyo, and before long rumors spread through the village that the two are in love. Hearing the rumors, her aunt tells Otama sternly that the Usui family is fated never to marry into a family from another region, and that she cannot be allowed to wed someone of unknown origin like Katsuragi — and forbids her from any further contact or lessons with him. Feeling that even her own foster mother has come to doubt her innocence, Otama weeps bitterly out of mortification. On the seventh day of the new year, while her aunt is away visiting relatives for the New Year, amid a heavy snowfall, Otama, unable to bear her longing, borrows an umbrella and leaves the house to go to her teacher — and that becomes the irrevocable mistake from which there is no turning back. Having abandoned both her hometown and her aunt to go up to the capital, Otama receives word that her aunt, grieving over her, passed away that autumn. Now she can only quietly relate her regret over that day of snow, saying that she keeps faith with an unkind husband, preserving a fidelity known to no one.

Characters

  • Otama (Usui Tama)　The only child born into the distinguished Usui family of a mountain village. Defies the aunt who raised her and, on a day of snow, runs to her teacher, making the mistake that becomes the narrator's lifelong regret
  • The Aunt　A woman who, widowed and returned to her family home, raised the three-year-old Otama with a devotion greater than that for her own child. Worried by the rumors, she sternly warns Otama, and later grieves over Otama's flight from home until her own death
  • Katsuragi Ichirō　A schoolteacher from Tokyo. Thirty-three years old. Dotes on Otama like a younger sister, and becomes the subject of the village rumors

The opening (original text)

As far as the eye can see, the earth lies spread with silver sand; the snowflakes dance light as butterfly wings and sleeves, and even the withered trees take on the look of spring's six-petaled blossoms — a sight that people of this world set down in poem and verse, praising it alongside the moon and the flowers. How enviable, that they should be free to praise it so! For when I, alas, call to mind that unforgettable past, this endlessly falling snow becomes to me only a thing of bitterness and sorrow — eight thousand times over I have repented, though repentance now is useless — that I, unworthy as I am, should have abandoned the ancestral burial ground of my forefathers, and turned my back on the aunt to whose deep kindness I owed my very upbringing.

Yuki no hi by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Never once had I dreamed of such a thing — yet, lost and with nowhere to turn, my heart broke beneath the falling snow against the bamboo at the window, and what had been, for both myself and others, no true wrongdoing became, in that moment, a real and true transgression. That I left my hometown, that I abandoned my aunt — all of it was but a dream born of that day of snow. To resent my husband now, unkind as he is, would be idle and useless; the capital is a lovely place to look upon its blossoms, and yet I, a tree of the deep mountains, have no place to stand among them, knowing myself alone to be in a winter of bare grass and trees, and when I ask my sleeve, wet with tears, about the past, I find that everything, everything, was a mistake. When word reached me on the wind from my hometown, I learned that my aunt had grieved and grieved over what became of me, until, that very autumn, she too was numbered among the sorrowful dead. Regret, they say, is the end of all things; now nothing more remains to be cut off in this fleeting world — I have only to keep faith with an unkind man, and preserve a fidelity known to no one. When I think of it, how true are the words of Lady Murasaki's poem — that the more one dwells upon this world, the more its sorrows only increase. I wonder if I even know, this year again, whether the snow still falls the same; mending my own broken hedge, as if to show it off to someone, I find myself only longing for what is gone.

Yuki no hi by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)
