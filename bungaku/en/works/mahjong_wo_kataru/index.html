About this work

An essay by Nanbu Shūtarō, beginning from the clacking of tiles he heard in a teahouse in Shanghai and going on to how mahjong spread through the Japanese literary world. Kikuchi Kan, Sasaki Mosaku, Hirotsu Kazuo, Furukawa Roppa — a roster set out by name, with their ranks attached, that conveys vividly how the writers of the early Shōwa years amused themselves.

Summary

In the autumn of the eleventh year of Taishō, on the night before he was at last to set sail for his beloved homeland after a three-month journey through north and south China, Nanbu wandered the streets of Shanghai. He savored Cantonese cooking at a restaurant on Sima Road, sipped liqueurs at a foreigners' bar, listened to a geisha's Nagasaki dialect at a Japanese restaurant, and even smoked opium in a secret den in the French Concession. Past one in the morning, stepping idly into a teahouse on a back street, he opened the window — and from the second floor of the house next door, across an alley scarcely a yard wide, came an unfamiliar clattering sound, chara-chara-chara-chara. That was his first encounter with mahjong. From then on, Nanbu carried the game back to Japan and grew steadily absorbed in it. He picked it up on his own, taught by no one in particular, from the autumn of the fourteenth year of Taishō, and wryly admits that just the year before this writing he had been in the grip of something like a mild fever for it. He writes of the pull of the tiles, the relation between luck and skill, the disgust that comes with a losing streak and the itch that soon creeps back all the same. And then the roster of his opponents is set down under their own names: sixth-dan Sasaki Mosaku, third-dan Wagi Seizaburō, third-dan Iketani Shinzaburō, and, at times, sixth-dan Kikuchi Kan, fifth-dan Hirotsu Kazuo, seventh-dan Kawasaki Bikan, sixth-dan Hamao Shirō, fourth-dan Furukawa Roppa, fifth-dan Suga Tadao — it sounds like a tournament of masters, he writes, but in truth there are hands that leave one's palms sweating and hands that dissolve everyone into helpless laughter besides. Since the company runs more to sharp tongues than sharp play, he begs the reader's indulgence for the racket that results. He had meant to describe the mahjong style of each of these worthies in turn, but — the piece closes — he has already reached the number of pages allotted him, and the retribution that would follow is a fearful thing to risk.

The opening (original text)

It is an old story by now, but it happened on a certain night in the autumn of the eleventh year of Taishō. Having just finished some three months of travel through north and south China, on the eve of the night before I was at last to take ship for my dear homeland — an evening thick with a milky night mist that left the town's lamps glowing softly, an evening that felt, in every way, like autumn in a foreign land — I set out wandering through the streets of Shanghai with a well-informed friend.

Mājan o kataru by Nanbu Shūtarō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

My opponents, too, were of course mostly men from within our own circle — those I faced time and again being sixth-dan Sasaki Mosaku, third-dan Wagi Seizaburō, third-dan Iketani Shinzaburō, and, on occasion, sixth-dan Kikuchi Kan, fifth-dan Hirotsu Kazuo, seventh-dan Kawasaki Bikan, sixth-dan Hamao Shirō, fourth-dan Furukawa Roppa, fifth-dan Suga Tadao, and the like...I had meant, for instance, to introduce the mahjong style of each of these worthies in turn, but I have just reached the number of pages I was allotted, and the retribution that would follow is a fearful thing to risk.
