About this work

A short story published in 1947, after the war, in the form of a letter from a young man working at a post office in Aomori to a certain novelist. Whenever he is moved by something, or tries to take anything seriously, a hammering sound — tokatonton — comes from somewhere and breaks the mood. The emptiness of a young man whose every enthusiasm is smashed by that hallucinated noise.

Summary

Dear sir. Tell me just one thing; I am at my wits' end — so the letter begins. The writer is a young man of twenty-six. Born the second son of a florist in Aomori, he passed through a munitions factory and the army, came home with the unconditional surrender, and now works at a third-class post office in a village on the coast. What torments him is a strange hallucination of sound. Just after he had listened to the Emperor's broadcast and been filled with a solemn feeling, from somewhere came a faint sound of a hammer: tokatonton. Ever since, whenever he is moved by anything, throws himself into his work, feels his heart beat over a love, or burns with anger and dreams of revolution — every time, that tokatonton sounds, and all his passion crumbles into something hollow and absurd. At a sports meeting, at the loss of a love, in a labor dispute, even when he had made up his mind to kill himself — that sound. The young man asks the writer how he may escape this hallucination. And the writer who receives this strange letter answers him briefly, quoting a verse from the Gospel of Matthew. A famous short story of Dazai's, catching sharply the emptiness of a young man living in the postwar collapse of every value.

The opening (original text)

Dear sir. Tell me just one thing. I am at my wits' end. I am twenty-six this year. I was born in the temple quarter of the city of Aomori. (...) I finished middle school in Aomori, then became a clerk at a certain munitions factory in Yokohama and served there three years, then lived four years in the army, and with the unconditional surrender I came back to the place where I was born; but the house had already been burned, and my father and my elder brother and his wife, the three of them, were living in a wretched hut they had put up on the burned ground.

The ending (original text)

In reply. That is an affected sort of anguish. I do not feel very much sympathy for it, you know. (...) Matthew ten, twenty-eight: "And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell." "Fear" here seems to come near to meaning "hold in awe." If you can feel these words of Jesus as a thunderclap, your hallucination ought to stop. That is all.

Famous passages

From the barracks behind me came the faint sound of someone driving nails with a hammer: tokatonton.

The sound of hammering that reaches the ears of a young man resolved to die, on the day of Japan's surrender. From then on, each time he hears it, all his passion drains away into blankness—a scene symbolizing postwar listlessness.
