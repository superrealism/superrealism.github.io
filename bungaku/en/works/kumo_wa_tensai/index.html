About this work

An autobiographical novel Ishikawa Takuboku wrote in 1906, published posthumously in his collected works in 1919, drawn from his time as a substitute teacher at Shibutami village in his home country. The narrator, a substitute teacher on eight yen a month, pours his whole soul into extra classes and sets fire to the boys' hearts, while nearly suffocating in the mediocrity and the conventions of a country school. The ideals and the rebellion of a young man with high ambitions, entrusted to the cloud of the title, which crosses the sky as it pleases.

Summary

On the thirtieth of June, in the staff room of the S—— Village Ordinary Higher Elementary School, even the wall clock seems to have caught the monotony of a schoolteacher's life, and announces, in its customary listless voice, the third hour of the afternoon. "I," a substitute teacher earning a mere eight yen a month, count the after-hours English and history lessons I began at my students' own request — once the regular curriculum is done — as the single happiest hour of my day, an hour into which I pour every last scrap of my knowledge, my grievances, my experience, my thought, like arrows loosed from a bow. Setting fire to the young hearts of some fifty boys and girls between thirteen and sixteen, the classroom becomes something like "a living tableau of life in revolt," charged with an excitement worthy of the Jacobins of revolutionary France. But on this particular day, a single remark from Principal Tajima — a man henpecked by his wife and the very picture of mediocrity — forces me to give up my beloved after-hours lessons, and I am cast down instead into the "hell" of meaningless clerical work, my resentment mounting by the hour. In the latter half of the story, this portrait of a young substitute teacher — carrying lofty ambitions, yet nearly suffocated by the mediocrity and stale custom of a country school — is taken up and answered by a tale my colleague Ishimoto tells me: the story of his parting, amid deep poverty, from Amano Shu'un, an "eccentric beyond the world." Amano had sold off everything he owned to pay for Ishimoto's travel expenses, and left him with the words, "Live, and fight" — before disappearing from his life. "The clouds are a genius" — in the free, ever-changing shapes of clouds drifting across the sky, Takuboku entrusts the soul of a young man's ideals and rebellion, in this autobiographical piece of fiction.

Characters

  • I　A substitute teacher earning a monthly wage of eight yen. Pours his whole soul into his after-hours lessons and inspires his students, but is nearly suffocated by the mediocrity and stale custom of his country school, despite his lofty ambitions
  • Principal Tajima　A man the very picture of mediocrity, henpecked by his wife. Forces "I" to give up his beloved after-hours lessons
  • Ishimoto　A fellow teacher. Tells the story of his parting from Amano Shu'un, an "eccentric beyond the world" whom he met amid deep poverty
  • Amano Shu'un　An eccentric man who sells off all he owns to pay for Ishimoto's travel expenses, and departs with the words "Live, and fight"

The opening (original text)

On the thirtieth of June, in the staff room of the S—— Village Ordinary Higher Elementary School, the clock on the wall gave, just then, its customary listless, lifeless shriek — no doubt even this clock had been infected by the monotonous life of a schoolteacher — and announced the third hour of the afternoon. It was probably, in fact, already close to four. For this is a common enough failing of clocks in country schools: in the three months I have now been employed at this school, this clock has never once, not a single time, agreed exactly with the great clock at K Station.

Kumo wa tensai de aru by Ishikawa Takuboku (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"...Life, to the very end, is nothing but suffering. That I was treated by Amano-kun as a true younger brother — I think that was the one and only happiness of my entire life." Having told his story to the end, Ishimoto wiped away his tears with the back of his thin hand and looked at me with a grief-stricken face. I too let out a long breath and wiped my own tears away. The woman teacher lay collapsed, face down, upon the desk.
