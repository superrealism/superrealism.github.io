About this work

One of the nine pieces in "The Restaurant of Many Orders," the only collection of children's stories published in Miyazawa Kenji's lifetime. It goes forward as the great boulder in the middle of Kurosaka Forest tells the origin of the strange names of the four forests standing north of the Koiwai farm — Wolf Forest, Basket Forest, Kurosaka Forest and Thief Forest. Grass and trees grow over a plain buried by an eruption of Mount Iwate, and in time four farmers come in and begin to break the ground. In the way the people and the forests call to each other — "let us stay here," "you may" — and come to terms, Kenji's sense of the natural world comes out clearly.

Summary

North of the Koiwai farm stand four black forests of pine: Wolf Forest, Basket Forest, Kurosaka Forest and Thief Forest. The great boulder in the middle of Kurosaka Forest begins to tell how it alone knows where those names came from. Long ago Mount Iwate erupted again and again, and after the land had been buried in ash the grass came up and the trees came up and the four forests were made. On an autumn day four farmers in straw capes come out onto the plain with axes and hatchets and shout, "May we break a field here?" The forests answer, "You may!" — and so the clearing begins. In time houses are built and children are born. Then one day two children go missing, and when everyone goes to look in Wolf Forest, the wolves are there playing with them around a fire. Next the tools disappear and they go to Basket Forest; and then, one winter morning, the whole store of millet is gone from the barn and they set out for Thief Forest. The master of Thief Forest insists he knows nothing about it, but the big man of Mount Iwate appears and smooths it over — Thief Forest only wanted to try making millet cakes — and the millet is back in the barn. Ever since, the people of the village have made millet cakes at the beginning of every winter and carried them to the four forests.

Characters

  • The four farmers　The men in straw capes who come onto the plain and begin to break the ground
  • The boulder of Kurosaka Forest　It tells the origin of the four forests
  • The wolves of Wolf Forest　They were playing with the missing children around a fire
  • The master of Thief Forest　He is suspected of stealing the millet
  • Mount Iwate　He appears in the shape of a big man and speaks for Thief Forest

The opening (original text)

North of the Koiwai farm there are four black forests of pine. The southernmost is Wolf Forest, the next Basket Forest, the next Kurosaka Forest, and the one at the northern end is Thief Forest. How these forests came to be, and when, and how they came by such queer names — I am the only one who knows it all from the very beginning: so the great boulder in the middle of Kurosaka Forest told me this story one day, with a good deal of pride. Long, long ago Mount Iwate erupted a great many times. Everything about there was buried in the ash. This huge black boulder too, they say, was flung out of the mountain and came down where it is now.

Wolf Forest, Basket Forest, Thief Forest by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"—" And Mount Iwate turned his face up to the sky again with an innocent air. The big man was nowhere to be seen any more. They all went home talking at once, quite taken aback; and when they looked, the millet was back in the barn, sure enough. So everyone laughed and made millet cakes and carried them to the four forests. To Thief Forest above all they carried the most of them. In exchange, they say, there was a little sand in those, but there was no help for that. And from then on the forests were friends with everyone. And every year, at the beginning of winter, they were sure to be given their millet cakes. But even those millet cakes, what with the times, have grown a good deal smaller; and there is no help for that either — so the great black boulder in the middle of Kurosaka Forest said at the end.
