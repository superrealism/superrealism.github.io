About this work

The work that made Hayama Yoshiki's name, published in Bungei Sensen in 1925. Written during his imprisonment in Nagoya, it is counted with "Letter Found in a Cement Barrel" and The People Who Live on the Sea among his central works. A young seaman loafing about the Yokohama wharf in work clothes is stopped by a rough, and taken, in exchange for everything he has, into a dim room in a warehouse — where a sick, wasted woman lies naked. A novella drawing, in a recollective style poised between fact and vision, the bottom of a society in which the exploited have no one to lean on but the exploited.

Summary

A memory from some ten years ago — whether it was fact or fantasy, 'I' still cannot say for certain, even now. As a young sailor just back from the European run, 'I' had been wandering, still in my work clothes, along the Meriken Wharf in Yokohama. At dusk, a man with a face like a slug's called out to me: 'Won't you buy, for two bits, the kind of fun a young fellow's after?' Two of his companions seized my arms and stripped me of what little money I had, then made me climb a warped staircase that clung to the side of a warehouse, deep in the back streets of Nankin-machi. A bare, hollow room reeked of mold and rot. Behind a stack of beer crates lay a woman of twenty-two or three, stretched out naked on her back. Wasted to skin and bone, her hair caked stiff with filth and blood, only her eyes remained open, watching 'me.' 'Do as you like with her,' the man said, and vanished downstairs. Torn between righteous anger and desire, 'I' spoke to the woman: 'I won't do anything too awful to you.' Determined to save her, I knocked the man down — and the woman told me something I never expected: that these very men had been keeping her alive all this time. Late that night, 'I' visited the room once more. The man too was a laborer stricken with the miners' palsy, and the two of them were nothing but scraps squeezed dry by others, huddling together to survive. Before the sleeping woman, now covered with a yukata, 'I' came to understand how mistaken I had been.

Characters

  • I (Minpei)　The narrator. A young sailor just back from the European run, wandering the wharf still in his work clothes
  • The woman　A woman of twenty-two or three lying in a warehouse room. Stricken with tuberculosis and cancer, she awaits death
  • The slug　The man who leads 'me' into the room. A former laborer suffering from the miners' palsy, who supports the woman together with his companions

The opening (original text)

One Even if someone were to ask me, of what I am about to set down here, 'Is that fact, or is it fantasy — which is it, really?' — I could not say for certain which it is. For ten years now, this same question, this same incident, has troubled me. At times I have suddenly thought, 'So I too have lived through something terrible.' At other times I have thought, 'But isn't this nothing more than my own fantasy — merely a feeling that it happened that way — or else...' And so, never quite knowing myself which it is, this strange memory has only grown more vivid, more detailed, carving itself ever deeper into some part of me.

Inbaifu by Hayama Yoshiki (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

I saw a martyr, not a prostitute. She seemed to me the very symbol of the whole fate of the exploited class. My eyes brimmed with tears. I walked softly, so as to make no sound, and pressed a one-yen note into the hand of the slug-faced man standing by the door. As I handed it to him, I gripped his withered hand with all my strength. Then I stepped out into the street. As I set my foot on the first step of the stairway, the tears that had gathered fell, dropping from my eyes.

Inbaifu by Hayama Yoshiki (Aozora Bunko)
