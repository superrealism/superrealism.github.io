Famous passages by Shimazaki Tōson

Well-known lines from the works of Shimazaki Tōson.

9 passages

Seedlings

When first I saw you, your bangs newly put up, beneath the apple tree, I took you for a flower yourself, with the flowered comb set in your hair.

The first stanza of “First Love.” Capturing a girl's age in the single detail of hair just put up, it seals a faint first love within a single scene in an apple orchard — the wellspring of modern Japanese lyric poetry.

Tōson shishō

By the old castle at Komoro, the clouds are white, and the traveler grieves.

The opening of “Song of Travel on the Chikuma River.” Setting the solitude of a traveler standing on the ruins of an old castle to a seven-five meter, it became a representative poem of Tōson as a poet.

From a distant island whose name I do not know a single coconut has drifted here. Leaving the shore of your homeland, how many months have you spent on the waves? Does the tree you came from still grow thick? Do its branches still cast their shade? I too, with the beach for my pillow, am a lone traveler sleeping adrift. Taking the nut and holding it to my breast, I feel anew the sorrow of wandering. When I watch the sun sink into the sea, the tears of a strange land come pouring down. I think of the eightfold tides between — on what day shall I return to my own country?

The whole of the poem “Coconut” (first collected in Rakubaishū). Written from Yanagita Kunio's account of seeing a coconut washed ashore at Cape Irago, it was later set to music and has long been loved as a song.

At last, the time of new poetry has come. It was like a beautiful dawn.

The opening of the author's preface to the one-volume edition gathering his four collections. Proclaiming the arrival of the age of new-style poetry through the image of the dawn, it is known as the words that symbolize the rise of modern Japanese poetry.

Life is power. Power is voice. Voice is words. New words are, in themselves, a new life.

A passage from the same preface. Linking life to power, to voice, to words, it holds that finding new words is itself a new way of living — the creed of Tōson's poetry.

The Broken Commandment

At Renge Temple, lodgers were also taken in.

A spare first line that offers only a setting, no explanation. It opens a monumental novel of Japanese Naturalism, moving toward the confession of the young teacher Segawa Ushimatsu, who lives concealing his origins.

The secret of his whole life had been as simple as this: “Hide it.” His father's commandment was contained, in full, in this single word.

A scene in which Ushimatsu recalls his father's commandment never to reveal his origins. The struggle over whether to keep or break this single word runs through the entire story, moving it toward confession.

Before the Dawn

The whole of the Kiso road lies deep in the mountains.

A sweeping opening that takes in the mountains of Kiso at a glance. It begins an epic novel that layers the upheaval of the late shogunate and the Meiji Restoration over a highway, a mountain range, and the life of one village headman.

A single highway ran straight through this deep forest region.

A line following the celebrated opening. This very highway, running through the Kiso valley, becomes the stage that carries the upheaval of the late shogunate and Restoration deep into the mountains.
