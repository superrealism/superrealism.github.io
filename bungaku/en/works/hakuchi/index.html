About this work

One of Sakaguchi Ango's central works and a starting point for postwar literature, published in the magazine Shinchō in June 1946. In a shabby corner of Tokyo at the end of the Pacific War, a film director becomes entangled with the idiot woman living next door. A representative work of the "decadents," looking for the truth of the human soul inside the confusion and destruction of the air raids.

Summary

Izawa, an apprentice director at a film company living in a back street of Kamata, finds one night that the idiot woman, wife of the madman next door, is hiding in his closet. He does not touch her but strokes her hair, and she attaches herself to him and stays on in his shed. In this woman, an unselfconscious existence of flesh released from every social tie, Izawa finds instead a kind of peace. In time a great air raid strikes the town, and through a sea of fire Izawa runs with her, telling her that when they die it will be like this, the two of them together. Reaching a barley field at last, with the woman asleep and snoring in front of him, Izawa can think neither of abandoning her nor of how he is to live from now on, and only waits for the day to break.

Characters

  • Izawa　An apprentice director at a film company living in a back street of Kamata. The protagonist, who keeps strange house with the idiot woman
  • The idiot woman　The wife of the madman next door, who attaches herself to Izawa without any understanding

The opening (original text)

In that house there lived human beings and pigs and dogs and chickens and ducks, and in truth neither the buildings they lived in nor the food they ate differed much. There was a crooked building like a storehouse, with the master and his wife downstairs and, renting the loft, a mother and daughter, the daughter pregnant by a man no one could name. The room Izawa rented was a shed set apart from the main house, where the consumptive son of the family had lain in the old days, they said; it was no shed too luxurious for a consumptive pig. Still, it had a closet and a lavatory and a cupboard.

The ending (original text)

Toward dawn it began to grow cold, and although Izawa was wearing his winter overcoat and a thick jersey, the chill was hard to bear. Below, at the edges of the barley field, whole fields of fire were still burning in several places. He wanted to go and warm himself there, but it would be awkward if the woman woke, and Izawa could not move. Somehow it was unbearable to him that the woman should wake. He thought of leaving her while she slept and going away, but even that had come to seem too much trouble. When a man throws a thing away — even scrap paper — he must have at least a certain resolve and a certain fastidiousness for the throwing. It was only that the resolve and the fastidiousness for throwing this woman away were both gone. He had not a particle of affection and not a particle of regret, but he had not the resolve for it either. Because there was no hope of a tomorrow to live for. Suppose tomorrow he did throw off the shape of the woman; would there be any hope somewhere for him? What would he live by? He did not even know where there was a house to live in, or a hole to sleep in. The American army would land, every kind of destruction would come upon heaven and earth, and the enormous love of that destruction of war would judge it all. There was nothing left to think about. When the sky whitened, he would wake the woman and, without so much as a glance toward the burnt ruins, would go looking for somewhere to sleep, setting out toward the furthest station he could. So Izawa was thinking. Would the trams and the trains be running? Leaning against the sleeper fence around the station and resting, would the sky clear this morning, he wondered, and would the sunlight pour down on his back and on the back of the pig beside him? For it was too cold this morning.

Famous passages

In that house lived humans, pigs, dogs, chickens, and ducks, and, truth be told, the buildings they lived in and the food they ate were scarcely different from one another.

A coldly detached opening that sets humans and livestock on the same level. A representative postwar work of Ango's, depicting a man fleeing through a city under air raid together with a feeble-minded woman.
