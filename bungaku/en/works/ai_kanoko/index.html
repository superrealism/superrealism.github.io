About this work

A very short piece, written in the first person, setting down the mind of a woman who swings between oppression and an aching elation every time she meets a certain man. Within barely seventeen hundred characters it places, through a fine accumulation of images, the level ordinary days when she is not seeing him, the unsettledness of the moment it is decided that she will, and the strange fullness of being with him. It is a piece in which Okamoto Kanoko's characteristic sensuality and feeling for life are concentrated, and the last line reveals from an unexpected angle who that person is.

Summary

Until she sees him again he is a person so oppressive, so wearing to the spirit, that she thinks she will hardly ever see him again — and thinking so she feels clear and goes back to ordinary months and days. She can hardly wait for the three-o'clock tea, the thread goes easily through the eye of the needle, the mats are pleasant under her bare feet, and she buys yellow chrysanthemums and arranges them. But when she is to see him again there is something like a presentiment. A discontent buds in her — only these months and days, only this much of herself? — and she begins to feel it absurd, and then puts the feeling down again. As the neon in the dark night sky reads now "little red pellets" and now "Jintan," her breast flickers busily on and off. As she sets out she is thoroughly tired of it; and once they meet, everything changes. A vigorous strength fills her, and she feels she could flow anywhere she liked over a field from which a bright, strong scent is rising. The hardships of marrying, what waits for her in old age, no longer enter her thoughts. If any man were to touch her now she would burn away with a hiss and be ash — and in the midst of such a rising, that person goes on playing his instrument quietly, unchanged. And when it turns out that this person, miraculous as he is to her, is in the world's eyes a customer of her mother's cheap sewing, and a junior employee of a confectionery company who goes about the city delivering biscuits every day, she can make no sense of it at all.

Characters

  • I　A woman whose heart is shaken every time she sees that person
  • That person　A junior employee of a confectionery company, and also a customer of my mother's sewing

The opening (original text)

Until I see that person again he is so oppressive, so wearing to the spirit, that I think I shall hardly ever see him again. Thinking so, I feel clear, nothing troubles me, and I go back to ordinary months and days, and every day I can hardly wait for the three-o'clock tea and take it eagerly. I think steadily about the things that suit a human lifetime — marrying, bringing up children, the order of my old age — and I do not feel, even to myself, that this is growing old before my time. The thread goes easily through the eye of the needle. The mats touch the soles of my bare feet smoothly and pleasantly. I buy yellow chrysanthemums and such things and arrange them in a vase. When I am to see that person again there is something like a presentiment. A discontent comes over me — only these months and days, only this much of myself — and I begin to feel it all absurd.

Love by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

If any man at all were to touch me now, I should burn away with a hiss and be ash. I want to let every one of the men know it. And yet that person, unchanged, goes on quietly playing his instrument. Only this time I am like a sightseer who has gone up inside the great Buddha, and can look at him from within. For the first time the sound of the instrument is loud in my ears. It is a happy sound, like the purling of water. To come back from there to my ordinary self again is another labor. I must cross loneliness like seas and mountains. And yet that this person, so miraculous an existence to me, should in the world's eyes be one of my mother's customers for cheap sewing, and at present a junior employee of a confectionery company who goes about the city delivering biscuits every day at a salary of so many yen a month — that I can make no sense of at all.

Love by Okamoto Kanoko (Aozora Bunko)
