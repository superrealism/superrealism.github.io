About this work

A historical romance Tanizaki serialized in the detective magazine Shin Seinen from 1931. It unravels the sexual secret, absent from the official histories, of the lord of Bushū — Musashi no Kami Terukatsu, celebrated as a great general of the warring age — in the guise of documentary work on invented sources such as a nun's memoir and a tea-priest's notes. The perverse desire that grew in a boy held hostage from the sight of severed heads being dressed night after night, and the conspiracy and revenge it brings about, are drawn in the formal beauty of a storyteller's manner. The book edition carried an afterword by Masamune Hakuchō.

Summary

The lord known as Bushū-kō, whose childhood name was Hōshimaru, grew up as a hostage at Oshika Castle, held by the Chikuma house. In the autumn of his twelfth year, during a siege, the boy caught a glimpse, night after night, of the ritual in which the castle's women washed and applied makeup to the severed heads of the enemy dead — the "head dressing." The sight of the women's white hands against the severed heads, and above all the smile of a beautiful girl bestowed upon a headless, noseless "woman's head," planted in him a strange rapture that would govern him for the rest of his life. Driven by that obsession, Hōshimaru steals into the enemy camp and, in an act of reckless daring, slices the nose from the sleeping enemy general — a feat that breaks the siege of the castle. Years pass, and the daughter of the general who died disfigured and noseless, Lady Kikyō, becomes the principal wife of Chikuma Norishige, the castle's lord. Her dark scheme to avenge her father's grievance upon her husband's house becomes entangled with Bushū-kō's own desire to witness that scene once more; Norishige's lips are slit, and at last, reduced to the same disfigured, noseless state, Oshika Castle falls. With the castle's fall, Lady Kikyō's heart returns to its natural womanly self, and her illicit bond with the lord comes to an end as well — and the author, for the sake of the lord's honor, lays down his brush, choosing to reveal no more than this.

Characters

  • Bushū-kō (Hōshimaru; Kawachi-no-suke Terukatsu)　A celebrated general of the Warring States era, held captive for life by the sight of the "head dressing" he witnessed as a boy
  • Lady Kikyō　Principal wife of Chikuma Norishige. Schemes to avenge upon her husband's house the grievance of her father, who died disfigured and noseless
  • Chikuma Norishige　Lord of Oshika Castle. His lips are slit and his nose lost through a conspiracy
  • Dōami　A tea attendant serving Bushū-kō. Leaves behind a memorandum bearing witness to the secret history
  • Myōkaku-ni　A lady-in-waiting who served in the inner quarters of Bushū-kō. Author of the memoir "The Dream of a Night Once Seen"

The opening (original text)

There is no way now of knowing in detail just what manner of person the nun Myōkaku-ni, author of "The Dream of a Night Once Seen," might have been, nor exactly when she came to write such a work as this — but from the sense of the passages before and after, it is clear enough that this woman had once served as a lady-in-waiting in the inner quarters of Bushū-kō. After the fall of the Bushū house she took the tonsure and became a nun, and records of herself that she built a grass hermitage "in some remote mountain village, with no occupation morning or evening but the recitation of the nembutsu." This memoir would seem, then, to be something she wrote down in the tedium of her old age, calling to mind the events of her past life — and yet, one wonders, for what purpose would a nun with "no occupation but the recitation of the nembutsu" have taken it into her head to write such a thing at all?

Bushūkō hiwa by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Thus the illicit relations between Bushū-kō and Lady Kikyō came to a complete end once she had moved to the Sannotani residence. As for the story of all that followed — how, until the lord's life came to its close at forty-three, he went on seeking one new woman after another, indulging in ever stranger stimulations and uglier misdeeds — to tell it would only grow overlong, and would do great injury to the lord's honor and the virtue of his memory; it is surely the wiser course, then, to lay down the brush after this much disclosure. And yet, if the reader, bearing in mind that such a secret lay hidden within one facet of the lord's sexual life, should afterward turn to the "Chikuma Gunki" and other official histories, he will surely find much there that comes as an unexpected discovery. In just this lies the quiet purpose of the author who set down this book.

Bushūkō hiwa by Tanizaki Jun'ichirō (Aozora Bunko)
