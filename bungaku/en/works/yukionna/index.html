About this work

One of the central pieces in Lafcadio Hearn's 1904 collection Kwaidan, drawn from tales of the snow spirit. On a night of heavy snow the young woodcutter Minokichi is spared by the snow woman, on the strict condition that he never tell anyone what he has seen, or she will kill him. He later marries a beautiful woman called O-Yuki and builds a happy household — until one evening he lets the secret of his youth slip. A quiet study of taboo and loss, and one of the best-loved Japanese tales of the uncanny.

Summary

In a village of Musashi Province live two woodcutters, Mosaku and Minokichi. Coming home from the forest one bitterly cold evening, they are caught in a great snowstorm and take shelter in the ferryman's hut, where they lie down to sleep. In the night Minokichi wakes to see a beautiful woman all in white breathing a white breath upon the old man Mosaku and taking his life. Her eye falls on Minokichi, young and handsome, and she tells him that for his beauty she will spare him — but warns him sternly that if he ever tells anyone, even his own mother, what he has seen that night, she will kill him. Then she is gone into the snow. About a year later Minokichi meets a fair-skinned, beautiful girl traveling alone, named O-Yuki, and takes her for his wife. She proves an excellent wife and bears him ten children, and the years pass without dimming her beauty in the least. One snowy evening, watching her face as she sews by the lamp, Minokichi lets slip the story of that night of the blizzard in his youth. At once his wife throws down her sewing, rises, and cries that the snow woman he saw was herself. Were it not for the children, she says, she would kill him where he stands; and with that O-Yuki turns to a white mist and passes out through the smoke-hole, never to be seen again.

Characters

  • Minokichi　A young woodcutter of Musashi, who meets the snow woman on the night of the blizzard and is spared on condition that he never speak of it
  • O-Yuki　The fair, beautiful woman who becomes Minokichi's wife. She is the snow woman herself, and leaves him when he breaks his word
  • Mosaku　The old master woodcutter Minokichi serves, whose life the snow woman takes on the night of the blizzard

The opening (original text)

In a village of Musashi Province, there lived two woodcutters: Mosaku and Minokichi. At the time of which I am speaking, Mosaku was an old man; and Minokichi, his apprentice, was a lad of eighteen years. Every day they went together to a forest situated about five miles from their village. On the way to that forest there is a wide river to cross; and there is a ferry-boat. Several times a bridge was built where the ferry is; but the bridge was each time carried away by a flood. No common bridge can resist the current there when the river rises. Mosaku and Minokichi were on their way home, one very cold evening, when a great snowstorm overtook them. They reached the ferry; and they found that the boatman had gone away, leaving his boat on the other side of the river. It was no day for swimming; and the woodcutters took shelter in the ferryman's hut — thinking themselves lucky to find any shelter at all. There was no brazier in the hut, nor any place in which to make a fire: it was only a two-mat hut, with a single door, but no window. Mosaku and Minokichi fastened the door, and lay down to rest, with their straw rain-coats over them. At first they did not feel very cold; and they thought that the storm would soon be over.

Yuki-Onna by Lafcadio Hearn (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

Even as she cried, her voice became thin, like a crying of wind — then she melted into a bright white mist that spired to the roof-beam, and shuddered away through the smoke-hole.... Never again was she seen.

Yuki-Onna by Lafcadio Hearn (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

But, if you ever tell anybody — even your own mother — about what you have seen this night, I shall know it; and then I will kill you.

The words the snow woman leaves behind after sparing only Minokichi's life in the blizzard-bound hut. The story holds its quiet tension until the day this promise is broken.
