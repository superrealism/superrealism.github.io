About this work

Kasamori Osen, the daughter of the teahouse at the Kasamori Inari shrine in Yanaka and the talk of all Edo. A long novel in which Kunieda Kanji drew in a gorgeous hand the love and the life of the girl painted in the colour prints of Suzuki Harunobu and adored by the young men of the city. Serialized in a newspaper with illustrations by Komura Settai, and a central historical novel of the Shōwa era, successful in bringing back the feeling of Edo.

Summary

North of Kannō-ji temple in Yanaka stands a thatched-roof house; the moonflowers twining its fence show white, and the garden, overrun with autumn grasses, is as beautiful as any print by the celebrated ukiyo-e artist Suzuki Harunobu. Outside that fence, the young master and Matsugorō lie in hiding, waiting for a glimpse of "the Goddess Benten" — so the story opens, on just such a night. Osen, daughter of the Kagiya, a teahouse at the Kasamori Inari shrine, has become the talk of all Edo ever since Harunobu's print of her began to circulate, and customers flock daily to the teahouse for no more than a glimpse of the girl. Yet Osen's own heart belongs to Kikunojō (Kicchan), a young actor she has known since childhood. Around Osen swirl the designs of those who would make her their own — among them O-Ren, younger sister of Honda Shinano-no-kami — entangling schemes over Harunobu's print, the romantic entanglements of young men of means, and the gossip of the town. The customs and human warmth of Edo, the bustle of the teahouse, the inner workings of the ukiyo-e publishing trade are all rendered in fine detail. In time Kikunojō falls ill and dies young. As he lies with his head to the north, his face, clear enough to be envied even by a woman, Osen takes up a powder-brush and, with the meticulous care of a master painter tracing fine lines, applies his makeup one last time. When crimson rouge touches his lips, Kikunojō's face is restored almost to the point of seeming about to speak. "Though a thousand men, ten thousand men, should come courting me, I will never stir again as long as I live" — and just as her tears wet her cheeks, the visitor who arrives to offer incense is none other than O-Ren, her face caked in makeup like some demon.

Characters

  • Osen　Daughter of the Kagiya teahouse at Kasamori Inari. The talk of all Edo
  • Kikunojō (Kicchan)　The young actor Osen loves
  • Suzuki Harunobu　The ukiyo-e artist who depicted Osen in a print
  • O-Ren　Younger sister of Honda Shinano-no-kami. Harbors hostility toward Osen

The opening (original text)

"Whoa, whoa, whoa. Don't lean out so far. The fence won't hold. Steady, now, steady." "Heh heh. Look who's flustered, young master — more than me, I'd say." "Shh — quiet—" A little more than two blocks north of Kannō-ji in Yanaka stands a modest dwelling, its thatched eaves grown over with moss; yet the moonflowers twining its fence show white, and the four or five tsubo of garden, left to grow wild with autumn grasses, its maiden-flowers half-hidden among the plume grass, wears a dreaming, otherworldly air — beauty exactly like a print by Suzuki Harunobu, the ukiyo-e artist so much talked of these days.

The ending (original text)

"—I shall surely go on living with you, from now on, always. Do stay by my side too, in spirit, forever. Though a thousand men, ten thousand men, should come courting me, I will never stir again as long as I live. — Ah, Kicchan..." A single tear fell from Osen's eyes and wet Kikunojō's cheek.
