About this work

A conversation between Nomura Kodō, author of the Zenigata Heiji casebooks, and Edogawa Ranpo. It begins from Kodō's prolific output — more than three hundred detective stories in period dress — and goes on to the secret of never running out of tricks, to a manner that shifted from popular fiction toward Conan Doyle and then toward psychological description, and to the remark that Zenigata Heiji stays thirty however many years pass. A valuable record of the detective-fiction world of the time, in which two masters speak frankly about each other's work. Collected in Essays on Zenigata Heiji.

Summary

You have written a great deal — how many of the casebook stories has it come to? — At Ranpo's question, Kodō answers that he does not know exactly, but that it must be over three hundred. That is for the two together, "Zenigata Heiji" and "The Ikeda Daisuke Casebook"; of the Heiji stories it must be two hundred and forty or fifty. He began putting one in every number from the first number of Ōru Yomimono, and when he rested for half a year in the fifth year the readers would not have it and the shops complained, so he began again. No one has written so many short stories even abroad, says Ranpo: sixty for Conan Doyle, and eighty even for Chesterton. ... The talk moves to tricks. Asked whether among three hundred stories there are none that use the same trick, Kodō answers that he may forget and use something similar, but never exactly the same. As to manner, he says that in the old days he wrote in the popular style with swordplay, and then gradually in the manner of Conan Doyle, and lately has come to want to write psychologically and to put some strength into the drawing of character — but that in a length of thirty sheets it cannot be done. In thirty sheets of manuscript paper one cannot get the interest of a detective story, Ranpo agrees; in Doyle's day it was generally a hundred. Toward the end the two speak of their own heroes. We grow older, but the heroes of our works stay young forever — Zenigata Heiji is thirty however many years pass, and it is a mistake to let him age, laughs Kodō. Ranpo discloses that he means to bring out an English translation of his own short stories at his own expense, and Kodō closes with the story of an American who tried to translate his casebook and wrote that the word oyabun could not be put into English.

The opening (original text)

A writer of world-class output with "Zenigata Heiji" EDOGAWA RANPO: You have written a great deal. How many of the casebook stories has it come to? NOMURA KODŌ: I do not know exactly, but it must be over three hundred. Though my casebooks are the two together, "Zenigata Heiji" and "The Ikeda Daisuke Casebook."

Tantei shōsetsu konogoro by Nomura Kodō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

EDOGAWA: I mean to bring out an English translation of my own short stories at my own expense. A man named Harris has translated "The Psychological Test," "The Human Chair," "The Twins" and so on. I think it can be brought out before long. NOMURA: An American once took my casebook away saying he would translate it, and then wrote that it was quite impossible. It seems it will not go into English. For one thing there is no word for oyabun. "Boss" will not do.... Ha, ha, ha.
