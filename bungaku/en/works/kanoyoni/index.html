About this work

Hidemaro, heir to a viscount's house and home from studying in Germany, comes up against the hard question of how to handle Japanese myth and history — a piece in which Mori Ōgai made a problem of thought squarely into fiction. Taking Vaihinger's philosophy of the as if for his clue, he offers the attitude of treating something as if it were so while knowing it is not fact, and sent great ripples through the intellectual situation of his day.

Summary

Gojō Hidemaro, heir to a viscount's house, has stayed up reading nearly every night since returning from his studies in Berlin. His mother watches him with uneasy concern, while his father the viscount, having no wish to force any particular profession on him, thinks it enough to let him choose whatever subject he pleases. Hidemaro's field is history. During his years abroad, what he wrote home to his father again and again was of the theologian Adolf Harnack — a man who, through rigorous historical research, could criticize the doctrines of Christianity even as he went on devoting himself to faith and to the state. In Harnack's position Hidemaro saw an answer to the problem that troubled him. That problem was this: to write the history of Japan as a genuine scholarly discipline, one cannot treat the accounts of the age of the gods as historical fact. But to deny them outright would shake the very foundations of the state. How was he to escape this impasse on every side? What Hidemaro arrives at is Vaihinger's "philosophy of as if." Duty, rights, God — none of these exist as plain facts, and yet people go on living "as if" they did exist. Might not myth, too, be treated the same way — known not to be fact, yet handled as though it were? When he confides this thought to his friend Ayanokōji, Ayanokōji presses him: "If you've hit a wall on every side, why not simply charge straight through it?" "Is there really no hope of coming to some compromise with my father, in the end?" Hidemaro says, low-voiced. "No use. No use at all," Ayanokōji answers. Beside the stove the two men sit looking into each other's eyes, saying nothing for a long while. The loneliness of a Sunday in the Yamanote district still holds sway over them both.

The opening (original text)

When the maid Yuki came in that morning to lay the fire in the hibachi, her mistress said, with an anxious look on her face, "The light was on again in Hidemaro's room last night, wasn't it." "Oh — was it, ma'am? Just now, when I went in to light the gas stove, his lamp was already out, and he was lying in bed smoking."

Kanoyōni by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

For an instant Ayanokōji's eyes flashed like steel. "If you've hit a wall on every side, why not simply charge straight through it? Why not do it?" Hidemaro's eyes reddened again at the rims. And, in a low voice, with almost the air of a child standing before some grown-up, he said, "Is there really no hope of coming to some compromise with my father, in the end?" "No use. No use at all," said Ayanokōji. The two of them sat looking into each other's eyes, saying nothing for a long while. The loneliness of a Sunday in the Yamanote district still held sway over them both.

Kanoyōni by Mori Ōgai (Aozora Bunko)
