About this work

A short story Higuchi Ichiyō published in the Kōyō Shinpō in October 1892. A childish orphan girl who has stubbornly kept at a distance the young doctor who looks after her realizes her love only when she loses him to illness, and refuses to remarry, keeping his ring and his sutra desk. It draws with an ironic eye the sadness of a love understood only when it is gone. The sutra desk of the title is where she keeps incense burning for the dead man.

Summary

Matsushima Tadao, a renowned young physician at the medical university, hailing from a samurai family of Shizuoka, is a man of impeccable character and learning, and receives countless proposals of marriage from noble families and high officials — yet he refuses every one, to the puzzlement of those around him. On his way home from making his rounds, Matsushima regularly calls at a house in Morikawa-chō, Hongō, where a beautiful girl named Kōzuki Sono (Osono) lives alone. Osono is the sole survivor of the former shogunal retainer Kōzuki Samon, having lost her father in the Battle of Ueno and her mother at fourteen. Matsushima had first taken on the care of Osono through the connection of having once been called to her late mother's sickbed, and on his way home he would teach her to read and write, treating her with great affection — but the extremely childish Osono finds him a nuisance, and dislikes him so much that she hides whenever she hears the sound of his rickshaw. The elderly maidservant Otami, who has long served the household, lectures Osono on her ingratitude toward Matsushima, who does so much for her. In time Matsushima is appointed director of a hospital in Sapporo, and bids Osono farewell, saying they will be parted for three or five years. At that moment he quietly leaves a gold ring tucked in Osono's sleeve. Only after Matsushima's departure does Osono suddenly seem to grow up — she puts on the ring and begins poring over a map of Hokkaido. But that autumn, Matsushima contracts typhoid fever from a patient and dies before reaching thirty, in the soil of Hokkaido. Hearing the news, Osono lives as though she has renounced the world, and when a second marriage is proposed to her, this time to a university professor, she answers that although she made no promise to Matsushima, and so has no vow of fidelity to keep, the feeling that has sunk into her can never be forgotten as long as she lives — and so, if the professor truly insists on taking her as his wife, she says, her body might go through the form of it, but her heart could never be so easily given. The sutra desk is the relic connected to all this.

Characters

  • Kōzuki Sono (Osono)　The only surviving child of the former shogunal retainer Kōzuki Samon. An extremely childish orphaned girl who is annoyed by Matsushima's attentions, but comes to realize her love for him only after his death
  • Matsushima Tadao　A renowned young physician at the medical university, from a samurai family of Shizuoka. Cares for and dotes on Osono, but contracts typhoid fever and dies after being posted to Sapporo
  • Otami　An elderly maidservant who has served the Kōzuki household for over ten years. Worries over Osono as if she were her own child, and lectures her on her debt to Matsushima
  • Kōzuki Samon　Osono's father. A former shogunal retainer who died in the Battle of Ueno

The opening (original text)

A single flower offered in mourning, a vow of a thousand years, a devotion of ten thousand — for whose sake does she keep her body chaste, living alone, turning her rare beauty away from moon and blossom both, wearing a face that seems not to know what season it is in this world, telling her beads on their cord, again and again, as though lost in the very wheel of transmigration before the Buddha himself? On what Tanabata night long past did she make her vow, that she should resent now being but one wing of the paired birds, and rage at the wind of impermanence that has struck down her twined branch? Ask who it is that keeps this ceaseless incense burning in the censer upon the desk, here within this quiet, secluded window —

Kyōzukue by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

There are those, no doubt, who would cast aside a bond without true love and remarry. To be sure, she says, she made no promise to that man, and has all the less right to speak of keeping faith with him — and yet the feeling that has sunk into her, from she knows not where, can never be forgotten as long as she draws breath; so if that professor should truly insist upon making her his wife, tell him this: that in form only might she consent to go to him, but her heart is not so easily surrendered. She says it all quite calmly, showing no sign of relenting, and Otami, seeing it is hopeless to press her further, gives up and presses her no more. Such is the origin of the sutra desk. A certain sharp-tongued person, hearing this story, remarked with a bitter laugh: "What a perverse woman — if the doctor were still alive, and had never gone to Sapporo at all, and came and went as gently as before, no doubt she'd loathe the sight of him enough to raise gooseflesh." And yet — "while he lived, his very presence sometimes wearied me; it is only now he is gone that I find myself longing for him" — however one looks at it, what a spiteful world, what a spiteful world this is.

Kyōzukue by Higuchi Ichiyō (Aozora Bunko)
