About this work

An unusual work in which Miyazawa Kenji, in the guise of the narrator's report as the Japanese delegate to a great festival of vegetarians held in a mountain village in Newfoundland, sets out at a stroke every argument about not eating meat. Objections from nutrition, economics, religion and evolutionary theory come one after another, and the debate itself, as they are answered, is where the interest lies.

Summary

"On the fourth of September last year, I attended, as representative of Japan's assembled believers, the Vegetarian Festival held at Hilty, a small mountain village on the island of Newfoundland" — so the report begins. After explaining that vegetarians divide broadly into the "sympathy faction," who hold that all animals cherish their lives no less than we do, and the "prevention faction," who avoid animal matter for the sake of preventing illness, the account turns to the festival grounds themselves. Believers gathered from every corner of the world sing hymns, and speeches begin. But then, one after another, invited "heathens" rise to their feet and hurl objection after objection at vegetarianism: Do not plants too possess life? Is not meat nutritionally necessary? What would become of the economy if livestock farming were to vanish? Is not the survival of the fittest simply the law of nature? Were not animals born, after all, only to be killed? Question after question comes pouring in, and the believers answer each one carefully, in turn — and this very exchange forms the true heart of the work, laying out Kenji's own reflections directly, one after another. Just as the debate reaches its fiercest pitch, the man who has argued most fiercely against them reveals his true identity from the podium: he is Hilgard, an actor of the New York Theatre, who had been asked by the deputy high priest to play a part, purely to liven up the festival. Roars of laughter, cheers, and applause seem fit to tear the festival grounds apart. But "I" am left in a daze at how anticlimactic it all is, and the pleasant fantasy of the Vegetarian Festival breaks apart right there.

Characters

  • I　Attends as the representative of Japan's vegetarians
  • Hilgard　The true identity of the "heathen" who raised the objections
  • Mr. Chen　A Chinese believer who attends the festival together with the narrator

The opening (original text)

On the fourth of September last year, I attended, as representative of Japan's assembled believers, the Vegetarian Festival held at Hilty, a small mountain village on the island of Newfoundland. Now, as many of you already know, we vegetarians are, in essence, a fellowship united in the conviction that one ought not eat anything of animal substance. In Japan the word is translated as "one who holds to the principle of a plant diet," though the term, in truth, often carries a good deal more force than the word "principle" alone would suggest.

Bijiterian taisai by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"We've been had — thoroughly had," Mr. Chen said, doubling over with laughter, and the roars of laughter, the cheers, and the applause seemed fit to tear the very festival grounds apart. But I myself was left in a daze at how anticlimactic it all was. And being so thoroughly dazed, the pleasant fantasy of the Vegetarian Festival broke apart for me right there. As for the rest, I must ask each of you to finish it off for yourselves, however you please — with some ordinary dance number, say, of the kind that closes out a moving picture.

Bijiterian taisai by Miyazawa Kenji (Aozora Bunko)
