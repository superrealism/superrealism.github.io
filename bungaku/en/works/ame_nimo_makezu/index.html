About this work

A poem Miyazawa Kenji wrote in pencil in a pocket notebook found after his death. He set it down in November 1931 while confined to his sickbed; it was never published in his lifetime and came to light only in 1934. Yielding neither to rain nor to wind, wanting nothing, always quietly smiling, content with the plainest food, going wherever anyone in the four directions is suffering — and being called a good-for-nothing for it, neither praised nor troubled over: such a person, Kenji writes in katakana and the simplest of words, is what "I want to become." Grounded in his deep faith in the Lotus Sutra, it reads almost as a prayer for selflessness and service, and is among the best-loved poems in Japan.

Summary

"Not losing to the rain, not losing to the wind" — the poem begins from a line that anyone in Japan can recite. To have a strong body, to want nothing, never to grow angry, always to be quietly smiling. To eat four cups of brown rice a day with miso and a little vegetable; in all things to leave oneself out of the reckoning, to look and listen well, and to remember. To live in a small thatched hut in the shade of a pine wood in the fields; if there is a sick child to the east, to go and nurse it; if there is a tired mother to the west, to go and carry her sheaves of rice; if there is someone dying to the south, to go and say there is no need to be afraid; if there is a quarrel or a lawsuit to the north, to go and say it is not worth the trouble. To shed tears in a drought, to walk about at a loss in a cold summer, to be called a good-for-nothing by everyone, praised by no one and a burden to no one. Such a person is what I want to become. An ideal of selflessness, apart from name and gain and given wholly to others, is sung plainly and without ornament in unadorned katakana, and yet with urgency. At the end come the invocations of the Lotus Sutra, showing how deeply this prayer was rooted in Kenji's faith. Written in a pocket notebook by an ailing Kenji and shown to no one, the poem was found after his death and is a support to many still.

A prayer-like poem found written in Kenji's notebook after his death. In plain, simple words it sings of an ideal way to live, and remains beloved and recited to this day.

The closing of this poem, written down in his notebook. He wishes to be neither praised nor a trouble to anyone, only a “good-for-nothing,” as people call it.
