About this work

A short story Kōda Rohan published in 1938, known as a fine late work by a man who loved fishing above everything. It takes the form of an old man's telling, and, with an account of a disaster on an Alpine climb for a pillow, gives one uncanny day on the sea off Edo. A samurai goes out fishing with a boatman, and at dusk seizes a splendid rod that appears out of the water by breaking the fingers of the drowned man who will not let it go. The next day the phantom of the rod appears again on the same sea, and the samurai at last gives it back to the water. A masterpiece in the conversational manner, in which the lore of angling and an air of the mysterious dissolve into each other.

Summary

In the height of summer heat, an old man begins to tell a strange tale of the sea. One evening, a samurai — addressed as "the guest" — goes out with the boatman Kichi for offshore fishing in the waters off Edo, and out of the dim evening sea, something like a fishing rod bobs into view, again and again. Drawing near, they find it to be a magnificent one-piece rod of wild hotei-chiku bamboo — and, what is more, a drowned man, addressed as "sir," grips it fast and will not let go. The old corpse, apparently a man of about sixty who suffered a stroke and fell into the sea while fishing, still clutches the butt of the rod with his little finger even in death. Struck with an unaccountable longing for so fine a rod, the samurai hesitates, then breaks the dead man's finger and at last wrests the rod free, carrying it home. The next day, examining the rod in the rain, he notices the care taken to keep water from seeping in, and the line tapered in graduated thinness — and from these he and the boatman come to sense just how dearly its dead owner had cherished it, an attachment so fierce it would not release its grip even in death. That evening, going out to fish once more, though the rod is safely in the samurai's own hands, the very same phantom of the rod bobs again into view on a sea utterly unchanged from the evening before. Their bravado — seeing an uncanny thing and refusing to call it uncanny — fails them, and samurai and boatman find themselves searching, in each other's eyes, for some glimpse of the other world. At last the samurai takes out the rod, murmurs "Namu Amida Butsu, Namu Amida Butsu," and returns it to the sea. A masterpiece of Rohan's late-life discursive style, in which deep angling lore mingles with an air of otherworldly mystery.

Characters

  • The guest (a samurai)　A samurai fond of fishing, who goes out for offshore fishing with the boatman Kichi. Captivated by the fine rod gripped by a drowned man, he breaks the corpse's finger to take it
  • Kichi　The Edo-front boatman who takes the guest out in his boat. He spots the rod appearing on the sea and holds forth on the lore of fishing
  • The drowned man ("sir")　The corpse of an old man found in the sea, gripping a magnificent fishing rod that he will not release. He appears to have suffered a stroke and fallen into the sea while fishing

The opening (original text)

Now that the weather has turned this hot, it is only natural that some of you should go off to the high mountains, and others to some cool spot by the sea, meaning to spend these trying days as part of a full and vigorous life. But once a man has grown old and decrepit like myself, he can no longer go to the mountains, nor out to the sea; instead he must content himself with the morning dew in his small garden, the evening breeze at his veranda's edge, and pass his days quietly and without incident — that, I suppose, is simply the natural course for an old man to settle into.

Gendan by Kōda Rohan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

With the bravado of one who sees an uncanny thing and refuses to call it uncanny, he said, in effect, "Never mind — the rod's right here with us, so there's nothing to it" — even as something strange seemed to show itself. The boatman, too, stooped low for a moment to peer toward where the rod should be. The guest peered up into the darkness overhead. But by now the night had grown so black, and the spot lay so deep in the shadow of the matting roof, that one could hardly tell whether a rod was there at all or not. Instead, the guest found himself studying the boatman's strange expression, and the boatman found himself studying the guest's — each, it seemed, searching in the other's eyes for some glimpse of a world not of this one. The rod, of course, had been there in the boat all along. But the guest took it up, murmured, "Namu Amida Butsu, Namu Amida Butsu," and cast it back into the sea.

Gendan by Kōda Rohan (Aozora Bunko)
