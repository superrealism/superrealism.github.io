About this work

The diary of Tomii, a literary young man who has moved to the university in Kyoto, written while he is scorched by the success of the friends he left behind in Tokyo. His jealousy and impatience toward Yamano and Kuwata, who bring out a coterie magazine and win recognition one after another, and even his shame at feeling so, are set down without mercy. Yamano is said to be modeled on Akutagawa Ryūnosuke and Kuwata on Kume Masao, and Kikuchi Kan's own gloom in his Kyoto years lies behind it. It was the work that carried its author into the literary world at a stroke.

Summary

In September Tomii leaves Tokyo and moves to the literature faculty at Kyoto. Satisfied as he is with the books in the research room and with the water of Kyoto, there sits at the bottom of his heart the fear that he may have no gift as a writer. Yamano and Kuwata, left behind in Tokyo, are full of talent, and that he could not bear the pressure they put on him is the real reason he came to Kyoto. At the end of the year a letter comes from Yamano saying that they are bringing out a coterie magazine. There is not a word in it inviting Tomii, and he takes it for a malicious challenge, resolves to come out by his own strength, writes the play "A Threat in the Night," and entrusts it to Professor Nakata. But three months later the professor has not read a page of it. When the coterie magazine appears, the story by Yamano at the head of it is so good that it draws Tomii in against his determination not to read it, the literary columns of the newspapers praise it one after another, and at last Yamano appears in one of the great central magazines. When Tomii gets seven pages of his own into a small magazine, Satake, a classmate who is doggedly writing a novel of fifteen hundred pages, suddenly begins to abuse that very magazine — and Tomii recognizes there the same impatience as his own. Then a letter comes from Yamano with an air of kindness about it, and "A Threat in the Night," which he gladly sent, is returned with the coterie's harsh verdicts. Two and a half years later the magazine has folded, but its members have established themselves as writers, and Tomii no longer feels either rivalry or envy. When he leaves the university he will be a schoolmaster in the country, he writes, and enter the peaceful life of an ordinary man.

Characters

  • Tomii　The "I" studying in the literature faculty at Kyoto. He means to be a writer and cannot trust his own gift
  • Yamano Toshio　A friend in Tokyo, who comes into the literary world at a stroke with a brilliant piece
  • Kuwata　A friend who brings out the coterie magazine with Yamano
  • Yoshino Tatsuzō　A senior man once known as a contributor to magazines, who still has not made his way into the literary world
  • Satake　A classmate who goes on silently writing a novel of fifteen hundred pages
  • Professor Nakata　The Kyoto professor to whom Tomii entrusts his play

The opening (original text)

13 September. I have come to Kyoto at last. Yamano and Kuwata may think that I came to Kyoto because I could no longer bear the pressure they put on me. But what do I care what they think? I mean to think about them as little as I can. Today I saw the research room of the literature faculty for the first time. There are better books there than I expected. I shall read through them one after another, as a silkworm devours mulberry leaves. In point of scholarship I shall certainly not be beaten by the crowd in Tokyo: so I thought, with real confidence, when I saw that room. And besides, I have taken to Kyoto itself. Today in particular, passing in front of the university, I saw in a little ditch where clear water ran down with a murmuring sound a number of bright red berries, come down, it seemed, from the hills of Shirakawa. That fresh scene, such as one could not dream of in the streets of Tokyo, drew my heart to Kyoto in early autumn. I have come to like Kyoto. I do not in the least regret having come here.

The ending (original text)

A popular writer! A rising writer! That I should have longed after such empty titles seems to me a little shameful now. How many works of the Meiji and Taishō literary world does one suppose will remain as masterpieces? Reading Anatole France one day, I came upon something to this effect: (As the heat of the sun gradually cools, the earth will cool with it, and human beings will at last die out. But the earthworms living in the ground may unexpectedly survive. And then the plays of Shakespeare and the sculptures of Michelangelo may be laughed at by earthworms.) What a splendid piece of irony. Even the works of genius will one day be laughed at by earthworms. All the more will the works of a Yamano, in ten years' time, not be worth an earthworm's laughter.
