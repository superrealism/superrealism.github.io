What is Literature Under Wartime Conditions?

Category
Other

The condition of literature placed under state control from the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937 to the defeat. Ishikawa Tatsuzō's "Ikiteiru Heitai" ("Living Soldiers"), depicting the realities of soldiers in Nanjing, was banned from sale the same day its March 1938 issue of "Chūō Kōron" was published, and Ishikawa himself was found guilty of violating the Newspaper Law: expression touching the dark side of the war was suppressed. That same August, meanwhile, a "Pen Corps" was organized at Kikuchi Kan's initiative, at the request of the Cabinet Information Bureau, to send writers to the front, and Kume Masao, Yoshikawa Eiji, Hayashi Fumiko, and Ozaki Shirō, among others, wrote accounts of their service. In May 1942, under the guidance of the Information Bureau, the Nihon Bungaku Hōkokukai (Japan Literature Patriotic Association) was established, and, with Tokutomi Sohō as its president, nearly every writer joined, mobilized to hold events such as the Greater East Asia Writers' Conference in service of national policy (Nakazato Kaizan and Uchida Hyakken refused to join). Dazai Osamu's "Sekibetsu" ("Regret at Parting"), written at the request of the Information Bureau and the Literature Patriotic Association, is an example of a writer avoiding overt propaganda by shaping the story around the friendship between a young Lu Xun and his friend. Wartime paper controls also brought repeated mergers and closures of magazines, and the Literature Patriotic Association was dissolved in September 1945.
