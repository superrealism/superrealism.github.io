About this work

The sequel published a little over half a year after "Discourse on Decadence." It states flatly that a return to the sound morality of the prewar years is exactly what is not to be wished, and attacks harshly the worship of village culture and the spirit of endurance. On the imperial system it exposes the trick of a history in which those who most defiled the emperor have most worshipped him, and calls out: let the widow love and fall into hell, let the demobilized soldier become a black marketeer. To fall is to be alone, and only the passage of solitude leads to God — the piece in which Ango's thought appears at its sharpest.

Summary

Since the defeat, they say, the moral sense of the people has decayed. Is it then to be wished, and to be welcomed, that we should return to the "sound" morality of before the war? Ango judges that it is the very last thing to be wished. The anecdote handed down in his native Niigata of a millionaire who beat a fifty-sen fare down to thirty. That this is still held up as a rule of life by the townspeople is where he sees the root of the trouble. The cry "back to the culture of the villages," screamed all through the war, is the same. Is there any culture in the villages at all? The essence of culture is progress, and what is in the villages is nothing but the spirit of exclusion, and distrust, and a stubborn reckoning of gain and loss. Ever since the Taika Reform the spirit of the villages has been an indomitable spirit for devising ways of evading taxes. Calling privation and endurance virtues, calling the wish for what one needs slackness, working the body to exhaustion instead of relying on machines — that is how Japan brought upon herself the ruin of her country. ... It is the same with the imperial system. For the Fujiwara and for the warrior houses it was more convenient to have the Emperor give the orders and to be the first to show themselves obeying, than to take sovereignty into their own hands. The soldiers who most profaned the Emperor worshipped him blindly; and we the people, who could hardly wait to stop the war, could not say so, and lost it in tears "because it is His Majesty's command." What a contrivance. The right shape of a human being is to want plainly what he wants and to say that he dislikes what he dislikes. To strip off the false clothing called duty and human feeling and come to a naked heart. Falling in itself is a trifling thing; but in it there is the great reality of a human being, which is solitude. The good man throws himself wholly upon the social order and dies calmly; the fallen man juts out of it and walks alone through the wilderness. That road alone leads to heaven.

The opening (original text)

Since the defeat, they say, the moral sense of the people has decayed; but is it then to be wished, is it to be welcomed, that we should return to the "sound" morality of before the war? I think it the very last thing to be wished.

Zoku darakuron by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

What we can do is only to become a little better; and the limit of a man's falling can in fact be, unexpectedly, only about that much. Men are not blessed with spirits sturdy enough to fall infinitely. They will not be able to help halting the fall by leaning on some contrivance. They make the contrivance, they break the contrivance, and so men go forward. Falling is the womb of institutions; and all that is needed is that we should first look with the greatest sternness at that painful reality of the human being.

Zoku darakuron by Sakaguchi Ango (Aozora Bunko)

Famous passages

Decadence itself is always a paltry thing, nothing more than evil; yet among its qualities there sternly exists that great reality of human beings called solitude.

A passage from the sequel to “Discourse on Decadence,” probing the value hidden within decadence. Those who fall bear a solitude in which they can rely on no one—and there, it argues, lies the true face of humanity.
