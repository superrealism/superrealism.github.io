About this work

A fine early essay in which the physicist and essayist Terada Torahiko remembered the first wife who died young. Published in 1905 in Hototogisu, the haiku magazine of Masaoka Shiki's circle. He sets down evenly, in a restrained hand, the straitened days of a newly married couple up to nursing a wife ill in the lungs, and the innocent figure of that wife absorbed in gathering acorns at the Koishikawa botanical garden. The ending, in which he finds her face again in the child she left, delighting in gathering acorns at the same garden, is full of grief and strikes the reader. A small piece in which a scientist's eye for observation and a warm affection dissolve into each other: the origin, one might say, of Terada's essays.

Summary

On an evening near the close of the year, my wife, just back from the fair at the Shitaya temple of Marishiten, suddenly coughs up blood and collapses. "I," at the time still nothing more than a student, am helped through the ordeal by a kind, gentle-hearted maid named Miyo, who has come to replace the maidservant who fled at the sight — and between nursing my tubercular wife and scraping together the preparations for a poor man's New Year, my days are given over entirely to the two. The new year comes, and my wife's condition neither worsens nor improves; the two of us pass quiet, uneventful days together. On a winter day, I take my wife out to the botanical garden in Koishikawa. Walking along the edge of the pond, my wife spots countless acorns lying among the fallen leaves beside the path, and crouches down, gathering them up in a kind of rapture. Her handkerchief fills, and still she is not satisfied — she borrows mine as well, and goes on gathering, with all the innocent absorption of a child. That wife, who once found such joy in gathering acorns, is no longer among the living. After her death, I take our six-year-old daughter, Mitsu-bō, the child she left behind, to that same botanical garden, and in the sight of my daughter's flushed, delighted profile as she gathers acorns exactly as her mother once did, I find, vivid and unmistakable, the likeness of my late wife.

The opening (original text)

I can no longer recall how many years ago it was, but I remember the day. On the evening of the twenty-sixth, with the year already drawing to its close, my wife went out with the maid to the fair at the Shitaya temple of Marishiten. She came home past ten o'clock, and, drawing out from her sleeve some kintsuba cakes and roasted chestnuts she had bought as gifts, set them quietly down at the corner of the desk where I sat reading over my notes; then she went into the privy, but before long came out again, her face gone pale, and just as she sat down beside the desk she was suddenly seized by a fit of coughing and brought up blood. It was not she alone who was startled — she told me afterward that, seeing all the color drain at that moment from my own face as well, she had lost heart all the more for it.

Donguri by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The wife who once found such joy in gathering acorns is no longer among the living. On the earth of her grave, the moss has flowered, season after season. In the mountains the acorns fall as they always have, and at the cry of the brown-eared bulbul the leaves come drifting down. This February, now that she has turned six, I brought Mitsu-bō, the child she left behind, out to this same botanical garden, and let her gather acorns just as in the old days. Whether such a small thing as this can truly be inherited, I cannot say, but Mitsu-bō took to it with tremendous delight. Every time she gathered five or six, she would come running to me, breathless, and toss them into the handkerchief spread open inside my hat. Peering in to watch her prize slowly grow, her cheeks flushed, she wore a face of melting happiness. And from some corner of that innocent face, the unmistakable likeness of her mother flickers into view, calling back a memory of the past that had begun to fade.
