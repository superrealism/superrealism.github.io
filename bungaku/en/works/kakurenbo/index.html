About this work

Saitō Ryokuu's central work of pleasure-quarter fiction, published in the series Shincho Hyakushu in 1891. An innocent young gentleman sets foot in the quarter and, taking up with one geisha after another, works his way deep into the manoeuvres of love and money. The vanity and calculation of people who hide their real feelings from one another and probe for the truth, and the frailty of love, are caught lightly in Ryokuu's cool, humorous style. It reads on the strength of its dialogue and its dapper prose rather than its plot: a revival of the Edo gesaku tradition in modern form.

Summary

A young man brought up as a sheltered son of a good family sets foot in the pleasure quarters. At first he is green and does not know his right hand from his left; but before long he learns the game and comes to have dealings with one geisha after another. Thrown about by the suggestive flattery and the maneuvering of the geisha and by exchanges that have money at their bottom, the young man sinks deeper and deeper into the world of love and desire. The people who talk of falling in love and being fallen in love with are, like children playing hide-and-seek, only hiding their true feelings from one another and sounding out one another's insides. With a detached irony and a light humor Ryokuu draws the vanity and the calculation of those who live in the pleasure quarters, and the frailty of love. Rather than an intricate plot, it is the deftness of the dialogue and the elegance of the style that carry the reader — a novel of the quarters that carried the tradition of Edo popular fiction into modern times.

The opening (original text)

Though Hideyoshi wore a golden crown and Goemon wore a hooded cloak, when the monkey and Tomoichi were born they were the same infants at the breast, and if a deaf man heard it the sound of "Regent" and "great thief" would be no different; but what changes is the rise and fall of the vermin of this dusty world, and that one turn is for all eternity the doggerel sutra also preaches. Our tale is of one Yamamura Toshio, a young man raised in the bosom of his family, who on his way home from seeing Danjūrō and Kikugorō at Shimabara was taken up to an upstairs room on nothing more than an invitation to eat, and that was the very beginning of it; and when a cup was held out to him with "Come, let me pour you one," he broke his "No, I really —" into three parts to make his awkward refusal, and being pressed with the law of the drinking table, that one must not shame a man, he received it at last with a hand that shook for no reason, half of it spilling over the soup bowl in a shower like a bundle of bamboo; and when the woman pouring said lightly, "Oh, forgive me," Toshio only fidgeted and did not so much as take up his chopsticks, and watching her back as she went out to change the bottle he thought this was the time to make himself presentable, and got as far as stuffing into his mouth one doubtful pale-gray sweet chestnut ball, and then, startled at the quickness of the footsteps coming up the stairs, swallowed it in a hurry — and one cannot help laughing at the way he held down the rumbling that must come when a thing goes down unchewed; while his companion was already thoroughly drunk and let his eyes, which were unequal triangles to begin with, go slack, and said, Well, Yamamura, my fine fellow, shall we have a beauty brought in for your inspection? — a barrel-mouth turned on him with fire away; and Toshio, hiding behind the smoke of the tobacco he had lately learned to smoke, smiled and made no answer, so that his companion, taking it that a card would come back neither way, grew impatient and asked what beauties there were in this quarter; and there are, there are, said the other, Koharu is a jewel though she is not yet of an age to wear the shimada, and for a chaperone send for old Daikichi. And before they had even come Toshio's breast was beating as it had not since the day his diploma was given him: if from behind the scent of the aloes a fan should be raised and he should be beckoned, what should he answer, having no move to make? — with the air of a man preparing for a preliminary hearing he pushed the candlestand away on purpose, holding that not to have his face seen was the first line of defense; and hardly had he built his palisade when there was a rustle of silk, and Toshio, thinking here they come, trembled again, and with nothing in heaven or earth to rely on but the post of the alcove behind him, leaned against it and folded his arms — and old Daikichi, who has crossed seas and mountains and had her eyebrows shaved twice even in this quarter alone, took him in at a glance and struck him at once with "Are we out of spirits, sir?" so that Toshio shrank into himself and had not a word to say for very awe; and his companion came to his rescue and made a diversion of the play they had seen that day, so that Toshio too managed a little answering; and though he fretted inwardly to be gone before the night grew deep, his companion showed no sign of rising, and while he watched uneasily the shine of old Daikichi's forehead, which she has polished as her trademark these thirty years until it gleams like a bottle, he bore it, thinking that nothing prospers against the obligations of this world — and gazing without any thought at all at Koharu, who sat before him without a care, sixteen or so, plump and fair and round of face and overflowing with charm, he was caught at it again by Daikichi, who, holding the one down and lifting the other with a strategy that ranged in every direction, said, For you a gentleman like this is just the thing; for boil down your great heroes and your holy men and it comes at last to there being a woman. And hearing it, more than Koharu, who denied it with the regulation "Oh, sister!" it was Toshio who flushed to the ears — an unmanly touch of autumn color, and how much the novelist owes, on such occasions, to the favor of that word — and in his greenness quietly straightened the collar of his silk coat; and when his companion put in carelessly that Koharu and Toshio go ill together in sound and that they must not become a charge on the Shijimi river, he hung back the more. But who was to know that the Jihei of a later day was this same Toshio, and that this night was the first steeping of him in love and drink? The phoenix chick and the unicorn colt keep the name given them the day they came out of the womb; and alas, the celebrated prodigy went from this night onward to ruin.

The ending (original text)

Consider: even the storm in the pines does not blow on without stopping, and what comes after it has taken breath is the fiercest thing of all. Toshio passed two months and three months admirably; but he was at the very height of wanting to play, and when someone who saw the ground was prepared came sounding him with "Well, Yamamura, how about it?" he thought, so be it, I shall go, and putting spurs to the horse said he would accompany him — and from the preliminaries of an extraordinary general meeting he began to run wild again, and, changing his name and changing his style, went into the old quarter with a long black silk coat and a silver pipe of the Nyoshin make, meaning to sell himself as a rogue outright, driving in the awl of Mao Sui's bag to the hilt and working it about. But there is no rogue who calls himself a rogue, and one Yukie, twenty-one or two and fair-skinned and easily melted, fixing her eye on the gentleness that still lingered somewhere in Toshio's look, climbed carelessly onto his glib talk; and had she heard the name Yamamura she would have kept her distance, but as they had missed each other until then she never dreamed it was he, and told everything at the first word and the second to her elder sister O-Shimo, and begged her to lend her the wit to play just one day with that gentleman. O-Shimo said she understood, and putting her mouth to Toshio's ear said, Do you not hear the bell of the telephone that calls to say everything is at an end? — at which he pushed her off with, however far I am fallen I do not paint white stuff on my face; and leaving O-Shimo, who was about to loose her second arrow, with a look over his shoulder, Toshio went out from there, sent his man Kinta home ahead of him — Kinta, who was then engaged in the study of whether even the yawns of waiting servants have a rhythm — and himself, at the Kikubishi this side of the bridge where his face was not known, waited two hours and would have waited three for Yukie, who was to say she was engaged; and at the moment when she cried Oh! in surprise, he made it appear that the connection had come from his side, and Yukie boasted to O-Shimo of a stroke as quick as lightning and flint. O-Shimo said, Really! and opened her mouth, and if one measured her astonishment by the school of Kyokutei it was about half — at any rate her face was undamaged, but meaning to talk and talk and talk out the grudge of having been beaten, she watched and dogged Toshio's steps: But can you answer for it? — I cannot, I cannot, truly I cannot. — Can you certainly not? — I certainly cannot. — And to whom is it that you cannot answer? — To my ancestor Sukeroku. — What is that! — and Toshio caught the hand O-Shimo raised as if to strike, saying that this would answer even less; and love went down and down until at last the two of them were parted as water from fish, and while there was any pulse in it they made each other spit blood, so that Yukie had the Yamamura crest struck on a cord chain and said Look at it, and O-Shimo, not to be outdone, had it lacquered on her comb; and taking that day for the last of the run, Toshio rang down the curtain and burned again on his old bad connection with Fuyukichi of the Motoki, and at the blazing rumor of it Yukie and O-Shimo looked at each other and bit the mouse-colored brocade of their tobacco pouches between their back teeth and flung them down on the matting, and said, So it was Yamamura, and our eyes were not sharp enough; and next day they took it out even on the hairdresser, and cried it about that one must not go astray like a crow calling on a moonlit night after being deceived. And from that time the mura in Yamamura became the mura of muraki, caprice; and it was said that before he was thirty he was not to be held with a rope, and after the play of Modoribashi he was nicknamed behind his back Sayuri, a notorious handsome villain whom no one denied when his name was spoken. To say that people change is not a happy thing, not a happy thing at all.
