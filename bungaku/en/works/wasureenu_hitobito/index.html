About this work

At a travellers' inn at Mizonokuchi, a post town on the Tama river, the unknown writer Ōtsu and the painter Akiyama talk through a night. The manuscript Ōtsu shows him, "People I Cannot Forget," holds not friends or benefactors but nameless people he passed just once on a journey. Bound to him by no interest and no obligation, and yet he cannot forget them — one of Doppo's finest short stories, drawing the sadness at the bottom of a solitary life and a quiet fellow feeling for strangers.

Summary

On a cold, dreary evening at the start of March, in the post-town of Mizonokuchi, just beyond the Futako ferry crossing of the Tama River, a traveling man in Western dress, Ōtsu Benjirō, comes into the inn Kameya seeking a night's lodging. Also staying at the inn is an unknown painter, Akiyama Matsunosuke, and as the two share cups of sake they warm to one another and fall into talk. Before long Ōtsu shows Akiyama a manuscript he has been writing, titled "Unforgettable People." It is, he explains, a record not of those one must not forget — parents, siblings, teachers to whom one owes a debt — but of people whom forgetting would break no obligation at all, and yet who somehow will not fade from the heart. A man walking alone along the shore in the shadow of an island in the Inland Sea; a horse-driver passing through the tranquil scenery at the foot of Mount Aso; a man begging while playing the biwa on a street corner amid the bustle of a festival — such nameless people, glimpsed only once in passing on his travels, Ōtsu finds he cannot, strangely, forget. When he tastes the loneliness and desolation of life, he says, and calls to mind those figures with whom he shares no interest, no bond of obligation, he feels that he and they alike are fleeting beings living out their span between the same heaven and earth, and cannot help but be moved to tears. Two years later, on the desk of Ōtsu, now living in a place in the Tōhoku region, that same manuscript lies. Added anew at its end is not the name of Akiyama, the friend with whom he had spoken so intimately that night, but the name of the master of the Kameya.

Characters

  • Ōtsu Benjirō　An unknown writer who lodges at the inn in Mizonokuchi. He cannot forget people met only once, in passing, on his travels
  • Akiyama Matsunosuke　An unknown painter who happens to be staying at the same inn. He spends a night in talk with Ōtsu
  • The master of the Kameya　The keeper of the travelers' inn where the two men lodge. At the story's close he unexpectedly becomes one of the "unforgettable people"

The opening (original text)

Cross the Futako ferry over the Tama River and go on a little way, and there is a post-town called Mizonokuchi. About halfway through it stands a travelers' inn called the Kameya. It was just the beginning of March. The wide sky that day hung heavy with cloud, a north wind blew hard, and this town, dreary enough even at the best of times, wore a look sadder still, gloomy and cold. The snow that had fallen the day before still lay unmelted, and from the uneven eaves of thatched roofs the meltwater, caught by the wind, went scattering and falling. Even the puddled mud in the footprints left by straw sandals seemed to shiver with cold little ripples. Almost as soon as evening fell, most of the shops had shut their doors, and the single dark street lay hushed. Only at the Kameya, being a travelers' inn, did lamplight shine bright through the paper screens — though tonight, it seemed, there were few guests, for the house within lay just as hushed, with nothing to be heard but, now and again, the tap of a thick-stemmed pipe against the rim of the brazier. All at once the paper screen slid open, and a man came shambling in. The innkeeper, who had been leaning against his long brazier, lost in calculations, had no time even to look up in surprise before the man had crossed the wide earthen floor in three long strides and planted himself right under his nose — a man not yet thirty-two or thirty-three by the look of him, dressed for travel in Western clothes, leggings, and straw sandals, a hunting cap on his head, an umbrella in his right hand, and a small leather bag tucked under his left arm. "I'd like to trouble you for a night's lodging."

The ending (original text)

Two years passed after that. For reasons of his own, Ōtsu had come to live in a certain place in the Tōhoku region. His acquaintance with Akiyama, whom he had first met at the inn in Mizonokuchi, had ceased entirely. It was, as it happened, just the same season as when Ōtsu had stayed at Mizonokuchi, on an evening of falling rain. Ōtsu sat alone at his desk, sunk in thought. On the desk lay that same manuscript, "Unforgettable People," which he had shown Akiyama two years before, and newly added at its end was the name "the master of the Kameya." Not "Akiyama."

Famous passages

Cross the Futako ferry over the Tama River, go on a little way, and you come to a post town called Mizonokuchi. About halfway through it stands an inn called the Kameya.

An opening that begins with a single night at a post town in March. A short story steeped in Doppo's poetic sensibility, on the strange inability to forget a stranger glimpsed only in passing.

The unforgettable are not necessarily those one must not forget.

The phrase that the unknown writer Ōtsu shows Akiyama as the first line of his manuscript. It distills the theme of the work: that the people we cannot forget are not those to whom we owe a debt, but those we passed only once on a journey.
