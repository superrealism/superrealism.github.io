About this work

From looking at a single teacup of hot water the talk widens to thunderstorms, waterspouts, heat shimmer, land and sea breezes and monsoons — Terada Torahiko's best-known science essay, contributed to the children's magazine Akai Tori. Droplets of steam, eddies, the wavering pattern cast on the bottom of the cup. His way of reading a miniature of atmospheric phenomena in an everyday cup of hot water later became a standard text in science teaching.

Summary

Here is a teacup, and in it is hot water to the brim. It seems that this is all there is to it; but if one looks carefully, all sorts of small things catch the eye and all sorts of questions arise. First there is the white steam rising from the surface. This is water vapor cooled into small droplets, and it is the same thing as cloud and mist. Held against the sunlight one can see the grains, and sometimes there is even a color like a rainbow. The talk extends to the researches of scholars showing that vapor needs a fine speck of dust for a core before it will make a droplet. By the way the steam rises one can guess roughly how hot the water is, and just as it rises a number of vortices form. The same vortex, in a garden in early spring, becomes a column like a whirlwind, and on a still larger scale it is the great vortex in the air that brings a thunderstorm. ... Next he looks at the water itself. Carrying it out into the sunlight and looking at the bottom, bright lines and dim lines move slowly in an irregular pattern. This is because water cooled and grown heavy sinks while hot water rises, and this circulation makes unevennesses from place to place, and light is bent at the boundaries of those unevennesses. The shimmer that flickers over a wall or a roof is the same thing. If one contrives this method, one can even photograph how a flying bullet presses the air, and the pattern of a propeller cutting through it. The unevenness of water cooled at the surface leads to the currents by which lakes and seas cool in winter; the unevenness of air warmed at the ground becomes the gust that threatens an airman; and on a great scale it becomes the land and sea breeze, the mountain and valley wind, and at last the monsoon. There is no end to what may be said about the water in a teacup, but let us leave it here for this time — so Torahiko lays down his pen.

The opening (original text)

Here is a teacup. In it is hot water to the brim. It seems that this alone has nothing amusing and nothing wonderful about it; but if you look carefully, little by little all sorts of small things catch the eye and all sorts of questions arise. Even this one cup of hot water is a fine sight for anyone who is fond of observing and studying the phenomena of nature.

Chawan no yu by Terada Torahiko (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The same sort of thing takes place between a mountaintop and a valley, and is called the mountain and valley wind. And when it becomes larger still and occurs, for example, between the continent of Asia and the Pacific Ocean, that is the so-called monsoon, which becomes the northwest wind we feel in winter and the southerly wind of summer. There is no end to what may be said about the water in a teacup, but let us leave it here for this time.
