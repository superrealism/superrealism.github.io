About this work

A short story Kikuchi Kan published in Chūō Kōron in 1921: a historical piece about the band of Kunisada Chūji fleeing to Shinshū with the authorities after them. Since too many followers would slow them down, the few who are to go with Chūji are to be decided by ballot. Kurōsuke, an older follower, in his eagerness to be chosen commits the fraud of slipping in two papers with his own name — and when that meanness comes close to being exposed, he suffers deep self-disgust. A fine thesis story characteristic of Kikuchi, cutting sharply into the vanity of a man who cares for his name, and the wretchedness of it.

Summary

Kunisada Chūji and his band, having cut down a magistrate of Jōshū, flee the pursuing officers of the Eight Provinces, abandoning Mount Akagi for the road into Shinshū. But traveling with fourteen or fifteen followers only makes them easier to track. And so it is decided that only a few will go on accompanying Chūji, while the rest scatter and flee on their own — and who is to follow Chūji will be decided by "the ballot": each man writing down the name of the one he wishes to follow, and casting it into the box. Kurōsuke, a veteran follower who has of late fallen somewhat out of favor, longs so desperately to be chosen that he secretly slips a second ballot, bearing his own name, into the box when no one is looking. But when the votes are counted, there is a vote for Kurōsuke that he did not cast himself — cast by a follower named Yasuke, who, currying favor with Kurōsuke, lies and says, "I voted for you." That his own shabby fraud went unnoticed even by the shallow, flattering Yasuke, and that it was Yasuke's very lie that so nearly exposed it, fills Kurōsuke with a shame so deep he can find nowhere to hide it. To think that he had committed a deed so base that he could not even conceive of a lie to cover it, as easily as Yasuke had — with that realization settling over him, even the clear sky of Jōshū and the calling of the small birds overhead seem to Kurōsuke drained of all savor. A piece in which Kikuchi sharply lays bare the vanity, and the wretchedness, of a man who cannot bear to lose face.

Characters

  • Kurōsuke　A veteran follower of Kunisada Chūji. In the ballot to choose who will follow Chūji, he cheats by casting two ballots for his own name, and suffers deep shame over it
  • Yasuke　One of the followers. Flatters Kurōsuke with the lie, "I voted for you"
  • Kunisada Chūji　A Jōshū outlaw hero. Pursued by the authorities, he chooses his followers by ballot and flees toward Shinshū

The opening (original text)

The band of Kunisada Chūji, who had cut down and killed the magistrate of Iwahana in Jōshū, had holed up on Mount Akagi to evade the pursuing officers of the Eight Provinces, but once even that position could no longer be held, Chūji and his fourteen or fifteen followers hacked open a single desperate line of escape and fled down the road into Shinshū. They crossed the Tone River at midnight. Since the bridge at Shibukawa was held by the officers, they crossed about a league downstream instead. The current ran so fierce that they strung a rope between both banks to cross by, but even so, one of the followers lost his grip on it and was swept away.

Irefuda by Kikuchi Kan (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

What was more, Kurōsuke thought, even Yasuke, who was telling him this bald-faced lie, could never in his wildest dreams imagine that he himself had done anything so base — that was precisely why he could tell so shameless a lie so easily — and thinking this, Kurōsuke grew disgusted with himself. To think that he had done something so base that even Yasuke, who could lie so glibly and without a second thought, could never have conceived of it — the thought left him feeling as though the late-spring sun blazing overhead had gone dark all at once, drained of every savor. The mountain-ringed sky of Jōshū was clear from end to end. Hundreds of small birds, passing from peak to peak, flashed their yellow wings as they crossed cheerfully, calling, above Kurōsuke's head. Ahead of him, Mount Haruna rose blue and sharp against the sky, cutting it in two.

Irefuda by Kikuchi Kan (Aozora Bunko)
