About this work

The first story of the Hanshichi series, published in the magazine Bungei Kurabu in 1917. In the form of an old man's reminiscence, the Edo constable Hanshichi unravels by plain reasoning a case that had looked supernatural. Calling Hanshichi "a hidden Sherlock Holmes of the Edo period," it became the starting point of the Japanese detective story in period dress.

Summary

The affair of O-Fumi as the narrator heard it in childhood from his uncle K. In the first year of Genji, O-Michi, married to the bannerman Obata Iori of Banchō in Edo, suddenly asks for a divorce and goes back to her family. Pressed for a reason, she says that every night the ghost of a young woman, soaking wet, appears at her pillow, and that even her small daughter O-Haru cries out that Fumi has come. Her brother and her husband drain the old pond, clean out the well and dig up the camellia, and can find nothing. Then K, the second son of a bannerman in the neighborhood, takes the matter up and consults Hanshichi, a constable of Kanda. Hanshichi discovers that in a picture book, the New Usuzumi Sōshi, that the wife had borrowed from a lending library, there is a picture of the ghost of a lady's maid, O-Fumi, drowned in an old pond. O-Michi had used the picture to frighten her child, and so O-Haru had taken to crying out O-Fumi's name at night. Hanshichi further sees through the scheme of a corrupt priest at the family temple, who had his eye on O-Michi's beauty and frightened her with a false prophecy — that to have a husband would cost her her life — meaning to get her divorced and take her for himself. Warned off by Hanshichi, the wicked priest goes in terror, and half a year later is arrested for breaking his vows with a woman. Saved from the edge of a precipice, O-Michi holds a memorial service and the affair is closed. The narrator calls Hanshichi "the hidden Sherlock Holmes of the Edo period."

Characters

  • Hanshichi　A constable of Kanda. Honest and highly capable, called the hidden Sherlock Holmes of the Edo period
  • Uncle K　The second son of a bannerman, an acquaintance of the narrator's, who takes the matter to Hanshichi and goes along on the inquiry
  • O-Michi　Obata Iori's wife, terrified by the ghost that appears each night, who asks for a divorce
  • Obata Iori　O-Michi's husband, a bannerman with a residence at Nishi-Edogawabata
  • O-Haru　O-Michi's three-year-old daughter, who cries out every night that Fumi has come
  • The priest of the Jōenji　A corrupt priest, who frightens O-Michi in order to have her divorced and take her for himself

The opening (original text)

My uncle was born at the end of the Edo period, and so knew a great many of the gloomy and uncanny legends most current in that age — the sealed rooms of haunted houses, the living spirits of jealous women, the vengeful ghosts of obstinate men, and the like. And yet, under the influence of a samurai education which held that a samurai must not believe in monsters, he seems to have tried to deny all of it. That temper did not leave him even after the Meiji Restoration. When we were children and began some rambling ghost story, my uncle would always pull a sour face and hardly deign to answer us. And then, just once, that same uncle said this: "But there are things in this world one cannot explain. That affair of O-Fumi, now...."

Hanshichi torimonochō: Ofumi no tamashii by Okamoto Kidō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

The story was engraved on my childish mind as something of the greatest interest. But thinking of it afterward, such detective work was for Hanshichi no more than the work of a moment before breakfast, and there were a great many other adventures of his still more striking than this. He was the hidden Sherlock Holmes of the Edo period. It was ten years later, just about the time the war with China came to an end, that I began to see Hanshichi often. Through some chance I became friendly with the old man and went frequently to visit him at his retirement house in Akasaka. Over tea I heard many of his tales of the old days, until a whole notebook was filled with almost nothing but his detective stories. Out of them I intend to pick, one after another, those I found most interesting — without regard to the order of the years.

Hanshichi torimonochō: Ofumi no tamashii by Okamoto Kidō (Aozora Bunko)
