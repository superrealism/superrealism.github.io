About this work

A historical story Yamamoto Shūgorō published in Kōdan Zasshi in 1945. Migita Tōroku, said to be the finest spearman in the country, deliberately takes service as a common foot soldier. It draws his hidden ability and the exchange of hearts between him and the good lord Abe Tadaaki. A piece with Yamamoto's characteristic theme running through it: that a man of real ability seeks neither fame nor gain.

Summary

Migita Tōroku, watching the foot soldiers at their archery practice, cannot help grinning at how right on the mark his comrades' running commentary is. "Standing like he's about to lay an egg," "aiming like a bird-catcher after a sparrow" — small wonder his fellow foot soldiers criticize each other's form so bluntly, for this same Tōroku was, in fact, a master of the spear, said to be the finest in the country, several degrees above them in skill. A man of such real ability, seeking neither fame nor gain, deliberately serves as a mere common foot soldier. In time, Tōroku's hidden ability comes to the notice of the good lord Abe Tadaaki. Tadaaki, valuing Tōroku, tries to take him formally into service. At the close of the story, Tadaaki shows an elegant touch of tact, bringing together Sae, the woman he himself wishes for, with the person Tōroku wishes for. "They say the Takashima domain keeps, as a mere foot soldier, the finest spearman in the land" — Tadaaki's voice, calling for his cooled sake to be changed, brings a lively, lamplit warmth to the palace on this snow-filled night. A piece of Yamamoto's, drawing the way of life of a man of true ability, and the discerning eye of a good lord who sees through to it.

Characters

  • Migita Tōroku　The finest spearman in the land, serving nonetheless as a common foot soldier
  • Abe Tadaaki　The good lord who values Tōroku

The opening (original text)

"What's with that stance? Looks like he's about to lay an egg." "That fellow over there looks like a bird-catcher aiming at a sparrow — hey, aim properly." "Ugh, missed again." "This is like a contest of who's worst at it." Right Tōroku couldn't help grinning. His fellow foot soldiers' running commentary really did hit the mark — standing like he's about to lay an egg, aiming like a bird-catcher after a sparrow — every remark so fitting he couldn't even scold them for it.

Ashigaru bōkō by Yamamoto Shūgorō (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

"...You shall bring me Sae, whom I have wished for, and I shall take Tōroku, whom you have wished for — that makes us even, five and five. ...When you return, tell Lord Miharu this: that the Takashima domain keeps, as a mere foot soldier, the finest spearman in the land." Tadaaki's voice, calling that the sake had gone cold and should be changed, brought a lively, lamplit warmth to the hushed palace within. Outside, the snow was still falling steadily.
