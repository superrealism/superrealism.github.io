About this work

A court novel Tanizaki serialized in the Mainichi Shimbun from 1949. Drawing on classics such as the Konjaku Monogatarishū, it tells, in a distinctive manner mixing documentation with story, the failures of the amorous Heichū; how the powerful minister of the left Fujiwara no Tokihira took a young and beautiful wife from the old retainer Kunitsune; Kunitsune's terrible ascetic meditations on impurity after the loss of his wife; and how Shigemoto, parted from his mother as a small child, meets her again forty years later. The scene of that reunion under the moon is famous as one of the finest in all Tanizaki.

Summary

In the Heian era when Fujiwara no Tokihira held supreme power, the celebrated womanizer Heichū was being thoroughly toyed with by the Lady-in-Waiting of the Hon-in. Beginning with the tale of Heichū's misadventures, the story then turns to the aged Major Counselor Kunitsune, nearly eighty, and his young and beautiful wife, the Kita no Kata. Tokihira, smitten with the Kita no Kata, visits Kunitsune's mansion for the New Year's banquet and, in an outrageous act, receives the Kita no Kata as a parting gift from the old man, plied with drink. Kunitsune's young son Shigemoto grows up secretly cherishing the memory of the mother torn from him. Kunitsune, robbed of his wife, tries to sever his attachment by making nightly visits to a graveyard to practice the meditation on impurity, contemplating corpses — but this only teaches him the depth of his own obsession all the more. In time both Tokihira and Kunitsune pass from the world, and the years go by. Past fifty, Shigemoto learns that his mother has become a nun and built a hermitage at Nishi-Sakamoto on Mount Hiei, and on a spring evening, beneath blossoms lit by moonlight, he is at last reunited with the mother from whom he was parted forty years before.

Characters

  • Fujiwara no Shigemoto　Kunitsune's son. Torn from his mother in childhood, he cherishes her memory his whole life
  • Shigemoto's mother　Kunitsune's young and beautiful Kita no Kata. Taken from him by Tokihira, and later becomes a nun
  • Fujiwara no Kunitsune　Shigemoto's father. An aged Major Counselor nearing eighty, who suffers over the wife taken from him
  • Fujiwara no Tokihira　The Minister of the Left, whose power has no equal. Takes the Kita no Kata from his uncle Kunitsune
  • Heichū (Taira no Sadafumi)　The greatest womanizer of his age, tormented by his love for the Lady-in-Waiting of the Hon-in

The opening (original text)

This tale begins with that celebrated womanizer, Heichū. Toward the end of the "Suetsumuhana" chapter of The Tale of Genji, there occurs the passage: 'Deeply moved with pity, she drew near and, wetting a sheet of Michinoku paper in the water of the inkstone basin, tried to wipe it away for him, whereupon he teased her, saying, do not go adding more color to it, as with Heichū — I could bear it if it were only red, and so on.' This refers to the moment when Genji, having deliberately smeared red upon the tip of his own nose and pretending it would not wipe off no matter how he tried, so troubles Murasaki no Ue — then eleven years old — that she wets a paper and moves to wipe his nose clean with her own hand, at which Genji teases her, saying, 'It would be a trouble indeed if you smeared it with ink, as was done to Heichū — I could still put up with red.'

The ending (original text)

"Might I ask — could it be that you are the mother of the late Middle Counselor?" Shigemoto said, stammering. "While I was still of this world, I was indeed such a person as you say — but who might you be?" "I am — I am the son left behind by the late Major Counselor. I am Shigemoto." And then, as though a dam had suddenly given way, he cried out, "Mother!" The nun, startled as the great bulk of a man came rushing to cling to her, staggered and just barely managed to lower herself onto a rock by the roadside. "Mother," Shigemoto said again. He knelt upon the ground, looking up at her from below, and leaned his body against her knees as though he might collapse into them. His mother's face, deep within her white hood, blurred by the moonlight filtering through the blossoms, looked small and dear, as though haloed in light. The memory of being held in her arms behind a curtained screen, on a spring day forty years before, now rose vividly before him, and in an instant he felt himself become a child of six or seven again. Brushing aside, in his rapture, the spray of yellow kerria she held in her hand, he drew his face closer and closer to hers. And, catching once more, in the fragrance that clung to her ink-black sleeve, the scent of a bygone age, he pressed his face into her sleeve again and again, wiping away his tears like a child clinging to its mother.
