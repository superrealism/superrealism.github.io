About this work

A collection of poems and prose by Takehisa Yumeji, the painter known for his Yumeji-style beauties, subtitled "An Unknown World." A flower pattern seen over the shoulder of the woman carrying him, the first discovery of death in a theatre booth, a boy's innocent love — a linked sequence of prose poems in which Yumeji set down his own childhood and boyhood in a hand like drawing.

Summary

"Amid the blue fields, a white road stretched on and on" — so opens "The Road," the poem at the head of the collection, beginning with a memory of being carried on the back of a woman he can no longer identify as mother, elder sister, or nurse, and weeping without knowing why, drawn in by her own tears. Pressing his cheek to her shoulder, he watches the floral pattern of her kimono bloom and bud within his tears, until a fallen boxwood comb turns, before his eyes, into a crescent moon — a piece as painterly as Yumeji himself, in which the boundary between reality and vision dissolves. In "Death," which follows, a boy who has watched, on the runway of a theater, a princess cut down by a bandit in a wig of white hair, is overwhelmed by "the first discovery of death" — the bandit's laughter as he holds up a severed head dripping with blood, the dead face dissolving into tears. Though his mother wipes away his tears and tells him it was only a play, the memory of the princess struck down on stage never leaves him. What follows — scenes of festival days, memories of travel, a print-shop, the first stirrings of love — are strung together, each as a short prose poem. Near the close, the boy is shown being kissed on his reddened hand by the older Mikano-san, as the two of them sing to the koto by the kotatsu, timid but yielding entirely to her will. The boy is even too embarrassed to look up into her face — and with that, this "unfamiliar world" comes to a close.

The opening (original text)

The Road Amid the blue fields, a white road stretched on and on. I no longer remember whether she was my mother, my elder sister, or my nurse. Because the woman carrying me on her back was weeping, I too, drawn in without knowing why, wept and wept, softly. When I pressed my cheek to her shoulder, the floral pattern of her kimono bloomed and budded within my tears; white petals flickered and shone, like stage snow, against the blue sky, and vanished.

Sakura saku shima by Takehisa Yumeji (Aozora Bunko)

The ending (original text)

So saying, Mikano-san leaned in against me, soft and yielding, as though coaxing, "Oh, you poor thing," she said, and pressed her hot lips against my reddened hand, kissing it. Timid as a small dove burrowed deep beneath its quilt, I let her have her way with me, though trembling all the while. I was even too embarrassed to look up into her face.
